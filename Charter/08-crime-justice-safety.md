# Chapter 8: Crime, Justice & Safety

*Reimagining justice and safety in a post-scarcity world*

---

## Introduction: Dismantling the Machinery of Desperation

Too many current justice systems lean on armed force, mass incarceration, and the threat of state violence. They treat people as threats to control rather than people who can change.

Scarcity contributes to some crime, especially survival-driven property crime and illegal markets. It is not the only cause, and its importance changes by crime, place, and time. Debt people cannot pay, housing they cannot afford, addiction tied to untreated trauma, and underground economies created when people lose income and decision-making power all deserve serious attention. Incarceration alone does not solve those conditions.

When housing, food, mental healthcare, and material security become unconditional rights, some pressures behind property crime and street violence may ease. Other causes of harm still need their own responses.

Harm will not disappear overnight. People will still face psychiatric crises, betrayal, domestic conflict, and predatory behavior. A post-scarcity society still needs safety and accountability. The proposal is restorative justice: repair the harm, protect victims, and address dangerous behavior through therapeutic containment and community restoration instead of brutal cages.

Restorative, therapeutic, preventive, community-based, and proportionate protective approaches may be combined or selected for different harms and contexts. They are evaluated alternatives under evidence, affected-community participation, transparent safety outcomes, and democratic review—not a reason to weaken due process, equal rights, protection from violence, or access to appeal and remedy.

## The Proposed Constitutional Floor: Rights and Due-Process Matrix

The **Rights and Due-Process Matrix** is this Charter's proposed constitutional floor for any public or delegated decision that can materially affect rights. Chapter 8 is its reader-facing home, and Chapter 5 describes the institutional authority that applies alongside this floor. This is a proposed design until lawfully adopted. Present law, current-world examples, and transition mechanisms remain labeled as such and do not become destination rules merely by appearing in this chapter.

The matrix follows the effect of an action rather than the label, ownership, contract, algorithm, registry, emergency declaration, or identity of the decision-maker. It covers government, courts, police and public services; contractors or private providers performing a coercive, essential, or registry-based public function; public AI; and private actors exercising delegated coercive authority, administering an essential public service or required registry, or deciding for government. Outsourcing cannot evade the floor. An ordinary private labor, consumer, contract, or civil dispute remains in its ordinary lane when no covered public function or delegated power is involved.

An affected person is anyone whose liberty, privacy, movement, property, access to essential services, legal status, livelihood, or meaningful opportunity is materially threatened, restricted, denied, monitored, recorded, ranked, or impaired. Directly affected accountable groups—including cultural or Indigenous bodies, unions, disability organizations, and public-interest advocates with a demonstrated connection to the people or systemic harm—have collective standing as well as individual standing for the affected person. Collective standing supplements and never replaces an individual's independent review.

The independent rights office classifies impact using severity, duration, reversibility, cumulative effects, vulnerability, dependence on essential resources, breadth, automated scale, and the risk that an error will be concealed or difficult to repair. **Lower impact** is genuinely lower, reversible, bounded, and reparable. **High impact** includes liberty, detention or containment, movement, essential services, legal status, substantial assets, intrusive monitoring, serious livelihood or opportunity loss, and comparable harm. **Cumulative harm** aggregates individually small actions across time, actors, services, or automated decisions; uncertainty defaults to high impact until an independent reviewer resolves the classification. The rights office investigates and may seek judicial relief, but it has no policy veto; authority roles remain those in Chapter 5.

### Universal Rights and Due-Process Matrix

These safeguards apply to every covered domain. A domain rule may provide more protection, but may not weaken this floor.

| Stage | Required rule | Minimum protection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger and classification | Classify by effect and cumulative harm before using a lower-impact process. | Independent rights-office classification; high-impact default under uncertainty; rapid judicial appeal of classification. |
| Notice and reasons | Give advance notice of the proposed action, legal authority, reasons, material evidence, criteria, expected duration, and challenge route whenever feasible. | Notice and reasons must be understandable and accessible; no hidden criterion may decide the case. |
| Imminent-threat exception | Use only for a specific, documented, serious and immediate threat when advance notice is not safely feasible. | Record reasons immediately; give safe notice normally within 24 hours; obtain independent **judicial review within 48 hours**; provide **a full hearing within 7 days**. Missing the deadline normally ends the restriction unless a court documents an exceptional lawful reason. |
| Evidence disclosure | Disclose sources, criteria, reasoning, and material evidence on which the action rests. | Redaction is narrow and court-approved for a specific safety, privacy, or security risk; provide a meaningful summary and security-cleared independent advocate; no adverse decision may rest solely on evidence nobody can effectively challenge. |
| Participation and representation | Provide a real opportunity to respond before action when feasible and an effective hearing afterward when emergency action was necessary. | The person may choose a representative. Publicly funded counsel is required when liberty, movement, housing, essential services, substantial assets, legal status, or a similarly serious interest is threatened. Lower-impact matters receive an independent advocate, with counsel when complexity or power imbalance requires. Translation, disability access, and communication support are mandatory. |
| Burden and least restriction | The authority bears the burden to prove each required element. | It must show legal authority, legitimate objective, reliable evidence, necessity, the **least restrictive** effective measure, proportionality, and nondiscrimination. Uncertainty weighs against severe or irreversible action. |
| Independent review | Match the reviewer and timing to impact. | High-impact action requires advance judicial authorization; emergency action receives judicial review within 48 hours; lower-impact administrative action may use an independent administrative reviewer with court appeal. AI can never be the final authorizer. |
| Proof | Use a proof standard proportionate to consequence. | Criminal guilt requires proof **beyond reasonable doubt**; high-impact civil or preventive action requires **clear and convincing evidence**; genuinely lower-impact action uses a balance of probabilities. Emergency temporary action requires the documented specific imminent threat and later normal proof standard. |
| Appeal and stay | Provide a meaningful, affordable, and timely challenge. | An optional rapid internal correction may be offered, but exhaustion is never required for high-impact, urgent, or constitutional claims. Courts review rights and proportionality and at least one affordable appellate level is available. A timely appeal presumptively stays high-impact action unless a court records a specific imminent danger; a reversible lower-impact action may continue only when fully reparable. |
| Record and transparency | Preserve a complete record for the affected person and public accountability. | The person receives full reasons and the record subject only to narrow approved redaction. The public receives anonymized decisions, governing law, criteria, aggregate statistics, error and reversal rates, algorithm documentation, and audit results. Identifiers are protected; sealing is narrow, independently approved, and periodically reviewed. |
| Expiry and renewal | No restriction persists by inertia. | Every covered restriction has an automatic end date. Renewal requires the authority to re-prove its legal and factual basis; repeated renewals receive stricter scrutiny and cannot become indefinite by serial paperwork. |
| Remedy | Repair the full effect, not only the original order. | Restore rights, access, status, property, and services; correct or delete records; notify downstream decision-makers; reimburse losses and legal costs; compensate material and dignitary harm; publish an anonymized correction; and require systemic reform when warranted. An independent public remedy fund promptly pays restoration and compensation, then recovers from agencies, contractors, or insurers. Personal liability is reserved for deliberate abuse, corruption, or concealment established with due process; discipline may also follow due process. |

Peaceful protest, criticism, organizing, journalism, whistleblowing, refusal to endorse a policy, and association are **lawful dissent**. Lawful dissent cannot by itself justify monitoring, AI scoring, financial or travel restriction, service denial, employment consequence, or a risk registry. A covered authority must prove specific unlawful conduct or a specific imminent threat; ideology, identity, lawful association, protected viewpoint, or prediction alone is insufficient.

No emergency may authorize torture, slavery, disappearance, collective punishment, arbitrary killing, punishment without law, denial of personhood, denial of court access, inhumane treatment, denial of essential survival needs, or suppression of lawful dissent. Any other limitation must be strictly necessary, least restrictive, proportionate, time-limited, recorded, and independently reviewed.

### Domain Matrix

These domain rules are additions to the universal floor, not alternatives to it.

| Domain | Additional rule | Required evidence, review, and remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Justice risk assessment | Independently validated and audited tools are advisory only; they cannot determine guilt, detention, sentencing, supervision, or service access. | Provide inputs, reasoning, limits, error rates, and a challenge route. Individual evidence controls. Protected traits and unreliable proxies are prohibited; a human decision-maker gives fresh reasons and remains accountable. |
| Surveillance | Intrusive monitoring requires a particularized judicial warrant naming evidence, purpose, person or defined system, minimization controls, duration, access logs, deletion rule, audit, and eventual notice. | Delayed notice needs separate court approval and ends when the risk passes. Generalized monitoring of lawful populations, beliefs, associations, or protests is prohibited. Unlawfully collected material is deleted and downstream use is unwound. |
| Human containment | Containment is a temporary, humane, nonpunitive last resort for a specific imminent serious threat. | Clear and convincing evidence, no less restrictive option, counsel, communication and basic resources, prompt review, automatic expiry, and new evidence for renewal. **Indefinite preventive detention is prohibited.** |
| Emergency powers | Professional emergency administrators may take immediate, narrowly defined life-safety action only under preapproved protocols. | Record the specific imminent threat and reasons immediately; safe notice normally within 24 hours; independent judicial review within 48 hours; full hearing within 7 days; and immediate court access. A rotating emergency committee reviews within 24–48 hours, and the full standing assembly reviews within 7 days. Extraordinary powers expire after 30 days where applicable; renewal requires 90% quorum of currently filled seats, two-thirds approval of votes cast for or against, and a new public justification. Emergency powers cannot permanently change law, suppress lawful dissent, delay lottery rotation, or make themselves permanent. |
| Financial registry | Public beneficial-ownership and control information is available for corporations, trusts, major public contractors, and public funds. | Ordinary balances and transactions remain private and data-minimized unless specifically authorized by law. People can inspect and correct records; privacy-preserving proofs are permitted. |
| Asset freeze | Freeze only specified assets with a clear and convincing nexus to a specified unlawful act or enforceable judgment. | Limit amount and property. Protect housing, food, health care, counsel, caregiving, and ordinary expenses. Automatic expiry and proof-based renewal apply. Imminent dissipation permits temporary action with notice within 24 hours and judicial review within 48 hours. |
| Travel | A judicial restriction requires individualized evidence of a specific serious risk: imminent violence, unlawful flight, or a defined quarantine danger. | Geography and duration are narrow; counsel, humanitarian, and medical exceptions are available; automatic expiry and current-evidence renewal apply. |
| Rights-affecting AI | AI is independently audited decision support only and never a final authorizer. | Before deployment: public rights-impact assessment and testing. During deployment: continuous independent audit, incident reporting, notice, understandable explanation, input challenge, and a fresh accountable human decision. The full public-data-only rule requires **source code, model weights, configurations, prompts and decision rules, training procedures, every training/evaluation record, evaluation suites, version histories, and deployment changes** to be fully public and independently reproducible. If any dataset cannot lawfully be made fully public, including confidential personal records, the system cannot be used in rights-affecting decisions. |
| Reproductive autonomy | Reproductive freedom is a constitutional right. No covered authority may impose forced pregnancy, abortion, sterilization, contraception, birth quotas, family-size limits, reproductive permits, benefit or service penalties, or reproductive surveillance. | Protect informed consent, privacy, accessibility, and timely access. A conscience-based refusal may concern only the provider's own participation; the public system must arrange another qualified provider. Courts provide remedy and block reproductive-rights violations, and no ecological or demographic policy may weaken this floor. |
| Cultural rights | Protect meaningful autonomy in language, heritage, education, ceremony, and internal life, with accessible mediation and affected participation. | Individual constitutional rights bind communities and public bodies; neither community consent nor majority vote waives another person's rights. No coercion, violence, forced membership, discrimination in essential rights, or denial of exit. Mediators come from an independent pool and are jointly selected or approved by affected parties, culturally competent, and voluntary. Dissenters may use a confidential advocate; direct court access remains available. No internal exhaustion is required for constitutional, urgent, coercive, or serious-harm claims. No accommodation waives a constitutional right. Restrictions require reliable evidence of an actual rights violation or specific serious risk and must be necessary, proportionate, and least restrictive. |
| Indigenous sovereignty | Treat Indigenous peoples as distinct nations, not merely cultural-interest groups. | Existing treaty, territorial, reparative, self-government, and collective rights remain in force during any transition. The universal individual-rights destination does not displace them unilaterally: each affected people freely negotiates conversion, and independent review verifies free and informed consent, without a forced deadline or unilateral cancellation. Applicable nation-to-nation procedures continue until consensually converted. Individuals retain independent rights review. |

### Institutions, remedies, and transition

Standing citizen assemblies set policy, approve laws and budgets, and respond to oversight findings; constrained professional administrators execute approved law and operations; experts advise; independent oversight investigates; and courts issue binding rights rulings, authorize or review high-impact coercion, and order remedies. The independent rights office classifies impact, inspects and audits, assists affected people, preserves evidence, investigates systemic harm, publishes findings, and may seek an emergency judicial pause. It has **no policy veto** and cannot create policy or permanently restrict a person. Private and contracted providers remain accountable for covered public power. Treaty bodies act only within enumerated, ratified scope, preserving consent-based treaty governance and Indigenous nation-to-nation rights.

After lawful adoption of the implemented matrix, existing covered restrictions receive independent review; these deadlines do not replace Chapter 24's readiness periods. Detention or containment must be reviewed within **7 days**; travel restrictions, substantial asset freezes, or essential-service deprivation within **30 days**; intrusive monitoring or a high-impact AI decision within **90 days**; and all other covered actions within **one year**. Failure to complete the required review by the deadline causes the covered restriction to automatically expire. Any later restriction requires fresh lawful authorization and cannot retroactively ratify the expired or unlawful action. Review does not ratify an unlawful action: reviewers restore access, correct or delete records, notify downstream recipients, and order remedy. Existing emergency actions follow the shortest applicable deadline, and an emergency label cannot extend transition time. Chapter 24's Foundation 0–8, Expansion 8–16, Global Floor 16–30, and Maturity 30+ periods and its GO/REVISE/PAUSE/STOP-REPLACE gates remain the transition readiness framework.

The matrix is a floor rather than one substantive policy answer. Communities may choose restorative, preventive, clinical, or community approaches inside it when participation is voluntary, safety outcomes are measured, and courts and appeals remain available. This preserves bounded pluralism while preventing prediction-based punishment, secret evidence, generalized surveillance, indefinite containment, collective punishment, and weaker appeal rights.

For authority and decision-making, see **Chapter 5**. Privacy and warrant safeguards are developed in **Chapter 9**; cultural and Indigenous protections in **Chapter 11**; failure modes and cumulative-harm risks in **Chapter 15**; rights-affecting AI in **Chapter 17**; treaty and cross-border protections in **Chapter 19**; financial registries and asset restrictions in **Chapter 20**; readiness-gated transition in **Chapter 24**; and local implementation, records, review routes, and remedy capacity in **Chapter 25**.

## The Crime-Scarcity Connection

### What Shapes Crime and Harm?

To understand how abundance affects crime, we have to separate its causes. Not every crime will be affected equally, and different harms require different responses.

| Factor or harm | Examples | What Could Material Security Change? |
|--------|----------|-------------------------|
| **Economic Need** | Some theft, robbery, illegal markets | Can reduce some pressures |
| **Desperation** | Survival crime, prostitution driven by economic coercion | Can reduce some pressures |
| **Inequality** | Class-based crime, resentment | Could reduce some pressures |
| **Addiction** | Drug-related crime | Reduces but persists |
| **Mental-Health Crises** | Situations requiring prompt care or safety support | Material security may ease some stress; timely care remains essential |
| **Status/Power** | White-collar crime, corruption (driven by accumulation rather than need) | Transforms but persists |
| **Repeated Serious Harm** | Coercive abuse, repeated violence, deliberate cruelty | Requires protection and accountability beyond material security |
| **Ideology** | Political violence, extremism | Transforms |

Crime does not have one cause. Some forms are tied to scarcity and may decline as material security improves. Others have psychological, social, or ideological roots that need different responses.

### The Evidence: Crime and Poverty

Poverty is associated with greater exposure to violence, but that relationship does not prove one simple cause of crime:

- In U.S. data from 2008–2012, people in poor households experienced nonfatal violent victimization at more than twice the rate of people in high-income households (39.8 versus 16.9 per 1,000 people age 12 or older).[^ch8-bjs-poverty-victimization] That does not prove poverty alone caused the violence or that victims were offenders.
- A growing body of research finds that some cash, food, health, disability, and unemployment-support programs improve public-safety outcomes, although results differ by program and setting.[^ch8-safety-net-crime-review]

Economic security is one promising prevention tool, but cross-country comparisons and broad historical claims need careful definitions before they can support this proposal.

[^ch8-bjs-poverty-victimization]: U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics, *Household Poverty and Nonfatal Violent Victimization, 2008–2012* (2014), reports annual nonfatal violent-victimization rates of 39.8 per 1,000 people age 12 or older in poor households and 16.9 per 1,000 in high-income households. These are victimization rates and do not by themselves establish offending rates or causation: https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/household-poverty-and-nonfatal-violent-victimization-2008-2012
[^ch8-safety-net-crime-review]: Robert Apel, “Can Social Safety Net Spending Prevent Crime?”, *Annual Review of Criminology* 9 (2026), reviews studies of cash welfare, food and disability assistance, public health insurance, and unemployment insurance; results differ by program and setting: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-criminol-032924-114453

### The Hierarchy of Crime Drivers

The justice system must distinguish between harms material security may reduce and harms that need other responses:

| Category | Description | Examples |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| **Scarcity-Driven and Potentially Reducible** | Harm tied in part to lack of resources | Theft for survival, some robbery for money, some economically driven illegal trade, prostitution driven by economic coercion, and burglary for basic needs. Better material security may reduce these pressures; it cannot guarantee that every case disappears. |
| **Harms With Multiple Causes** | Material security may ease some pressures; protection, care, and accountability remain necessary | Domestic violence, gang violence, substance-related harm, reactive violence |
| **Harm Not Solved by Material Security Alone** | Serious harm needing its own safety response | Repeated serious violence, coercive abuse, deliberate cruelty |

This distinction sets limits on what abundance can solve.

## Crime Reduction Through Abundance

### Economic Crime: What Could Decline

When basic needs are guaranteed, the pressure behind some acts of survival-driven crime may decline:

**What Could Decline:**
- Theft for food or shelter
- Robbery for money during the transition phase
- Burglary for survival
- Drug trade driven by economic motivation
- Prostitution driven by economic coercion
- Fraud for basic needs

None of these categories can be assumed to vanish completely, even if economic coercion becomes much less common.

**Why:**

Universal Basic Resources are meant to reduce the need to break into buildings for shelter or use illegal activity to meet medical needs. Economic desperation is one pressure behind some property and survival offenses, not the only motive in every case.

Evidence on social safety-net programs and public safety is promising in some settings, but results vary by program, study design, and context.[^ch8-safety-net-crime-review]

**Timeline:** How much these offenses would decline, and how quickly, remains uncertain and would have to be measured during implementation.

### Property Crime: Gradual Reduction

Property crime would not disappear instantly. The proposal aims to reduce it, but the size of any change must be measured:

**What Could Decline:**
- Burglary
- Theft of possessions
- Vandalism driven by economic frustration
- Shoplifting

**Why:**

Material security could ease desperation and frustration that contribute to some property crime. Lower inequality could reduce resentment and perceived unfairness. Stronger community ties may provide informal accountability: people may be less likely to harm neighbors they know and care about.

**Possible timeline:** Property crime could decline gradually over 5–10 years where material security, lower inequality, and stronger community ties take hold.

### Violent Crime: Stress-Related Reduction

Violent crime has several causes. Lower stress and better support may reduce some forms, but the effect cannot be assumed:

**What Could Decline:**
- Assault in economic disputes
- Robbery-related violence
- Gang violence over territory and resources
- Domestic violence related to financial stress
- Hate crimes driven by scapegoating

**Why:**

Violence has many causes. This proposal would provide material security to ease economic stress, mental-health support to address underlying needs, stronger community ties to reduce isolation, and conflict-resolution services to offer alternatives to escalation. The proposal would measure their effects.

Financial stress can make it harder to leave an abusive relationship. Material security and safe housing could give people more ways to get safe.[^ch8-matjasko-economic-support] The effect on violence must be measured.

[^ch8-matjasko-economic-support]: Jennifer L. Matjasko, Phyllis Holditch Niolon, and Linda Anne Valle, “The Role of Economic Factors and Economic Support in Preventing and Escaping from Intimate Partner Violence,” *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management* 32, no. 1 (2013): 122–128, https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/55596/cdc_55596_DS1.pdf. The review describes poverty and limited affordable housing as barriers to leaving violent relationships and says economic support may help people leave. It cautions that increased financial resources may increase violence risk in coercive-control relationships, recommends safety planning, and says more research is needed on housing-assistance outcomes.

**Possible timeline:** Depending on community conditions and implementation, some violent crime could decline gradually over 10–20 years as material security, mental-health support, community ties, and conflict-resolution services expand.

### White-Collar Crime: Transformation

White-collar crime would still need separate prevention and accountability systems:

**What Transforms:**
- Corporate fraud
- Embezzlement
- Insider trading
- Corruption

**Why:**

Less wealth concentration reduces the stakes. Proposed governance mechanisms include:

- **Transparency requirements:** Publicly accessible transaction records, mandatory disclosure of financial relationships, and open-book corporate governance make concealment difficult 
- **Public ownership structures:** Democratized ownership of enterprises reduces opportunities for personal value capture by limiting concentration of control and establishing cooperative oversight 
- **Accountability systems:** Community-based oversight, whistleblower protections, and restorative consequences ensure responsibility

However, some white-collar crime may persist because it's driven by greed and power rather than need. These require specific governance solutions. These mechanisms are draft proposals. They need careful design, testing, and adaptation before adoption.

**Timeline:** Depends on governance changes and implementation of transparency systems.

## What Harm Might Persist

Some harm will persist even in abundance. The system has to be honest about that limit.

### Addiction-Related Harm

**Why It Persists:**

Addiction involves biological, psychological, and social components (the biopsychosocial model).[^ch8-nida-addiction] While economic factors are one driver, addiction can persist due to neurological pathways, trauma, emotional coping, and social factors that aren't eliminated by abundance alone.

**Approach:**

- Treatment, not punishment
- Harm reduction strategies
- Support systems and recovery networks
- Understanding root causes and trauma
- Community-based recovery programs

Addiction needs medical and psychological support. Treatment and harm-reduction programs should be judged by evidence, while criminal penalties should not be treated as a substitute for care.

### Mental Health-Related Harm

**Why It Persists:**

Mental illness has biological components, though their expression and severity can be influenced by social and economic factors.[^ch8-who-mental-health-determinants] Depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and other conditions have complex etiologies involving genetics, biology, trauma, and social determinants. While these conditions can occur regardless of economic conditions, abundance society may reduce certain triggers and provide better support for management.

**Approach:**

- Universal mental health care
- Early intervention and prevention
- Community support networks
- Treatment-focused response rather than incarceration
- Crisis intervention teams with mental health professionals

Mental health-related harm needs treatment and compassion, with support and healing as the goal.

### Serious Harm Beyond Scarcity

**Why It Persists:**

Research finds that higher psychopathy scores are associated, on average, with larger deficits in affective empathy than cognitive empathy. The pattern varies by trait and by how empathy is measured.[^ch8-psychopathy-empathy] Sadism involves deriving pleasure from causing harm. Material security will not remove every source of serious harm. Some people may continue to present risks that need treatment, close supervision, or separation when safety cannot otherwise be maintained.

[^ch8-nida-addiction]: U.S. National Institute on Drug Abuse, *Drugs, Brains, and Behavior: The Science of Addiction*, describes addiction as a medical disorder affecting the brain and behavior and discusses biological and environmental risk factors: https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction
[^ch8-who-mental-health-determinants]: World Health Organization and Calouste Gulbenkian Foundation, *Social determinants of mental health* (2014), reviews how mental health and common mental disorders are shaped by social, economic, and physical environments across the life course: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241506809
[^ch8-psychopathy-empathy]: Campos et al., “Refining the link between psychopathy, antisocial behavior, and empathy” (2022), meta-analysis of 477 records involving 142,692 participants, found larger average deficits in affective than cognitive empathy among samples scoring higher in psychopathy; effects differed across psychopathy dimensions and conceptual models: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35349788/

**Approach:**

- Safety and protection for potential victims
- Treatment where possible
- Containment where necessary
- Honest assessment of limits
- Ongoing ethical review of containment decisions

**Key Insight:** Some individuals may require temporary, humane containment for public safety. Any such containment remains a nonpunitive last resort under the Rights and Due-Process Matrix: it requires clear and convincing evidence, the least restrictive effective option, counsel, prompt independent review, automatic expiry, and new evidence for renewal. Indefinite preventive detention is prohibited. This raises serious ethical questions that cannot be dismissed lightly.

From a **utilitarian framework**, containment is justified when it prevents greater harm to potential victims. From a **restorative justice perspective**, the challenge is that some individuals may be incapable of participating meaningfully in restorative processes.

The ethical distinction between "containment" and "punishment" hinges on intent: containment aims solely at protection, not retribution. However, philosophers disagree about this distinction. Critics argue that any deprivation of liberty requires robust justification, procedural safeguards, and regular review.

A humane approach would include:
- Regular risk assessment by independent evaluators
- Due process protections for those subject to containment
- The restriction automatically expires unless the authority re-proves the required basis with current evidence; independent risk assessment remains advisory evidence and is never the release gate
- Humane conditions focused on dignity and available treatment
- Recognition that this represents a failure to achieve full restoration

### Ideological Harm

**Why It Persists:**

People will always have different values and beliefs. Some may oppose the abundance system itself. Extremism can exist without scarcity. Ideological violence has motivations beyond economic desperation.

**Approach:**

- Freedom of belief protected
- Protection from harm when beliefs lead to action
- Dialogue and engagement where possible
- Prevent violence, not ideas

The difficult line is between unpopular beliefs, which remain protected, and harmful actions, which must be prevented. Freedom of thought does not include freedom to harm.

## Justice Systems in Abundance

### Problems with Current Justice Systems

The current justice system has failures this proposal must address:

| Problem | Impact |
|---------|--------|
| **Punitive Focus** | Recidivism rates range from 70-77% within 5 years when measured by rearrest, though rates vary by jurisdiction and measurement method[^ch8-bjs-recidivism] |
| **Mass Incarceration** | About 1.85 million people were held in U.S. state or federal prisons or local jails in 2023.[^ch8-bjs-correctional-populations] |
| **Economic Bias** | Access to counsel, bail, fees, and ability to pay deserve separate review with specific evidence. |
| **Racial Disparities** | Disparities require separate measures at arrest, charging, sentencing, and incarceration. |
| **Reintegration Gaps** | The availability and quality of support after release vary and need direct assessment. |
| **Victim Support Gaps** | Whether victims receive needed care and restitution should be measured, not assumed. |

A punishment-first system can break family ties, damage communities, and leave victims without care or restitution. We should measure reoffending and these other harms.

### The Restorative Justice Model

Restorative justice starts from a different approach:

**Core Principles:**

1. **Harm is caused to people and relationships** - From a restorative justice perspective, crime is harm to real people
2. **Justice should repair harm** - The goal is healing.
3. **Voluntary participation** - People harmed, people who caused harm, and community members may take part when it is safe.
4. **Focus on healing, not punishment** - Transformation rather than retribution

**The Restorative Process:**

1. **Acknowledge harm** - The person who caused harm recognizes what they did
2. **Understand impact** - They learn how their actions affected others. Direct dialogue happens only if everyone involved freely agrees.
3. **Make amends** - They work to repair damage where possible
4. **Reintegrate** - They receive support to return to community and show change over time.

**Outcomes:**

We want community healing and accountability without dehumanization. Evidence for particular programs is narrower: a systematic review of ten randomized studies of face-to-face restorative-justice conferences found greater benefits for victims and a modest average reduction in repeat offending among consenting participants.[^ch8-restorative-justice-review] That evidence does not cover every restorative practice or every case.

Restorative justice treats people as capable of change, aims to address causes of harm, and involves the community in healing. Making it more humane than punitive justice is a goal of this proposal, not an outcome proven for every program.

[^ch8-bjs-recidivism]: U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics, *Recidivism of Prisoners Released in 34 States in 2012: A 5-Year Follow-Up Period (2012–2017)* (2021), reports that 71% of people released in the 2012 cohort were arrested within five years; among comparable 19-state cohorts, the five-year rearrest measure was 77% for 2005 releases, 75% for 2008 releases, and 71% for 2012 releases: https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/recidivism-prisoners-released-34-states-2012-5-year-follow-period-2012-2017
[^ch8-bjs-correctional-populations]: U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics, *Correctional Populations in the United States, 2023 – Statistical Tables* (2025), reports 1,852,900 people in state or federal prisons or local jails; prison and jail counts use different measurement dates: https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/correctional-populations-united-states-2023-statistical-tables
[^ch8-restorative-justice-review]: Strang et al., “Restorative Justice Conferencing Using Face-to-Face Meetings of Offenders and Victims” (2013), a Campbell systematic review of ten randomized experiments involving consenting participants, found substantial victim benefits and a modest average reduction in repeat offending. The finding applies to the studied conferencing model, not every restorative-justice practice: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.4073/csr.2013.12

### Community-Based Justice

In this proposal, justice becomes a community function:

**Structure:**
- Local community justice centers
- Trained facilitators and mediators
- Victim-offender mediation programs
- Community service as restoration
- Support networks for reintegration

**Benefits:**
- Potentially faster resolution than traditional bureaucratic systems (context-dependent)
- Some restorative programs may cost less than incarceration, but costs and results depend on the program and the cases it handles
- Community involvement in accountability
- Real understanding of harm
- Focus on reintegration from the start

Harm happens in communities, so healing should involve them. Community-based justice keeps people connected and builds local ability to resolve conflict.

## Prevention Strategies

The best justice prevents harm. An abundance society invests heavily before harm occurs.

This prevention framework is adapted from public health models, which use similar tiered approaches to disease prevention.[^ch8-cdc-public-health-approach]

[^ch8-cdc-public-health-approach]: The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes violence prevention as a public-health application and separately defines primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention as interventions before injury, early detection and intervention, and minimizing effects after illness or injury: https://www.cdc.gov/violence-prevention/about/about-the-public-health-approach-to-violence-prevention.html and https://www.cdc.gov/Mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00016403.htm

### Primary Prevention: Stop Harm Before It Starts

**Goal:** Prevent harm before it occurs by addressing root causes.

**Strategies:**
- **Economic Security** - Reduce pressures behind some desperation-driven crime through UBR
- **Mental Health Support** - Universal access with early intervention
- **Education** - Conflict resolution, empathy, emotional intelligence
- **Community Building** - Connection reduces isolation and increases accountability
- **Substance Treatment** - Accessible, non-punitive support
- **Family Support** - Resources to reduce domestic violence

**Implementation:**
- Universal mental health care integrated into communities
- Community centers with support services
- Education programs teaching emotional skills
- Family support services with proactive outreach
- Substance treatment programs with no barriers

Primary prevention creates conditions where harm is less likely instead of waiting to react.

### Secondary Prevention: Intervene When Risk Appears

**Goal:** Intervene when risk factors appear, before harm occurs.

**Strategies:**
- **Crisis Intervention** - Mobile de-escalation teams
- **Risk Assessment** - Identify potential harm early
- **Support Programs** - Help for struggling individuals
- **Conflict Resolution** - Mediation services available
- **Mentorship** - Support for at-risk youth

**Implementation:**
- Mobile crisis teams with mental health professionals
- Community support workers for ongoing assistance
- Education programs identifying at-risk children
- Workplace support for employees in crisis

Secondary prevention catches problems before they become harm.

Risk assessment in this preventive discussion is support for voluntary care and safety planning, not prediction-based punishment. Any tool that affects rights is advisory only, must disclose its inputs and limits, and remains subject to the Rights and Due-Process Matrix; a score or prediction can never establish guilt, detention, supervision, or denial of services.

### Tertiary Prevention: Prevent Re-Harm

**Goal:** Prevent re-harm after incidents occur.

**Strategies:**
- **Restorative Justice** - Repair harm and address causes
- **Reintegration Support** - Help people return to community
- **Continued Treatment** - Ongoing support for underlying issues
- **Particularized Monitoring** - Safety for the community only where lawful, necessary, and independently reviewed
- **Accountability** - Real responsibility for actions

**Implementation:**
- Restorative justice programs for appropriate cases
- Reintegration services and support
- Treatment programs addressing root causes
- Particularized, lawful monitoring where safety requires and the matrix permits it

Tertiary prevention works to keep harm from happening again. It balances accountability with support for change.

## Response to Harm

When harm occurs, the response should match its severity.

### Tiered Response System

**Level 1: Minor Harm**
- **Examples:** Petty theft, minor vandalism, low-level conflicts
- **Response:** Restorative justice, community service, mediation
- **Goal:** Learn, make amends, reintegrate

**Level 2: Moderate Harm**
- **Examples:** Assault, significant property damage, repeated minor harm
- **Response:** Restorative justice plus treatment plus particularized monitoring where lawful
- **Goal:** Address root causes, ensure safety, support transformation

**Level 3: Serious Harm**
- **Examples:** Serious violence, sexual assault, serious injury
- **Response:** Protect people and offer treatment and a voluntary restorative process where safe. Use temporary, humane containment only when the Rights and Due-Process Matrix permits it, with independent review.
- **Goal:** Protect community, heal victims, transform offender

**Level 4: Extreme Harm**
- **Examples:** Murder, serial violence, extreme harm
- **Response:** Protect people and offer treatment. Use time-limited, humane containment only when the Rights and Due-Process Matrix permits it, with independent review.
- **Goal:** Protect society, provide treatment where possible

Minor harms receive restorative responses. Serious harms may require temporary containment for safety, with treatment still provided; any restriction requires the matrix's due process, automatic expiry, and independent review.

**Decision-Making Process:** Trained assessors, victim input, and community justice representatives may provide advisory evidence and program recommendations about the level of harm; they do not decide rights impact. The independent rights office classifies rights impact under the Rights and Due-Process Matrix, courts authorize high-impact coercion, and mediation cannot authorize or continue a restriction. Ordinary court appeals and other matrix review routes remain available.

### The Response Process

**1. Immediate Safety**
- Remove immediate threat
- Protect victims
- Ensure community safety
- De-escalate first

**2. Investigation**
- Understand what happened
- Gather facts objectively
- Identify root causes
- Give people affected a safe way to share information, without requiring them to join a restorative process.

**3. Response**
- Match response to harm level
- Focus on healing
- Include accountability
- Plan for reintegration

**4. Follow-Up**
- Monitor progress
- Provide ongoing support
- Ensure safety
- Evaluate effectiveness

This process keeps responses proportional, focused on healing, and directed toward preventing future harm.

## Special Cases

Some harms need specialized responses.

### Domestic Violence

**Why It Persists:**

Domestic violence has multiple risk factors: power dynamics, psychological factors, learned behavior, trauma cycles, and economic stress.[^ch8-cdc-ipv-risk-factors] Meeting basic needs will not eliminate abuse.

[^ch8-cdc-ipv-risk-factors]: CDC guidance here is specifically about intimate partner violence (IPV). It lists multiple risk factors, including economic stress and desire for power and control in relationships, and says risk factors may or may not be direct causes. It supports a multiple-risk-factor framing but does not establish that every factor named here applies to every case of domestic violence. [CDC, “Risk and Protective Factors”](https://www.cdc.gov/intimate-partner-violence/risk-factors/index.html).

**Approach:**
- Immediate safety for victims (safe housing, protection)
- Treatment for perpetrators (accountability programs)
- Community support for both parties
- Education on healthy relationships
- Time-limited accountability and particularized monitoring where lawful

Domestic violence requires recognizing that it's about power and control. Responses must prioritize victim safety while offering perpetrators pathways to change.

### Substance Abuse

**Why It Persists:**

Substance use is not the same as addiction. Addiction changes the brain and has biological and environmental risk factors.[^ch8-samhsa-sud] Some people may use substances to cope with trauma, pain, or existential suffering. Material security alone will not eliminate the need for treatment and support.

[^ch8-samhsa-sud]: U.S. Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, “Substance Use Disorders Treatment Options,” explains that not everyone who uses substances develops a substance use disorder, distinguishes substance use disorders from addiction, and describes biological and environmental factors and brain changes: https://www.samhsa.gov/blog/substance-use-disorders-treatment-options

**Approach:**
- Treatment
- Harm reduction strategies
- Support networks and recovery communities
- Address root causes (trauma, mental health)
- Community integration and support

Criminal penalties should not substitute for care. This proposal treats substance use as a health issue and calls for treatment and support whose outcomes can be measured.

### Mental Health Crises

**Why They Occur:**

Mental health crises have biological factors, trauma responses, stress triggers, and isolation components.

**Approach:**
- Crisis intervention teams with mental health professionals (not armed responders)
- Treatment
- Community support networks
- Ongoing care and follow-up
- Prevention through universal mental health care

Mental health crises need medical and psychological care. People in crisis need help, not punishment.

### Serious Harm That Needs Protection

**Why It Persists:**

The cited research finds average differences in empathy among people with higher psychopathy scores, with results varying by trait and measure. It does not establish a single neurological cause, predict what any one person will do, or show whether treatment will help someone with psychopathy traits or a severe personality disorder. Material security will not prevent every act of serious harm. A trait score or diagnosis alone cannot justify containment. The Rights and Due-Process Matrix requires a documented, specific, imminent serious threat, independent review, and the least restrictive effective measure.

**Approach:**
- Safety and containment to protect potential victims
- Offer appropriate treatment and assess whether it helps
- Ongoing, independently reviewed risk assessment and particularized monitoring where lawful
- Humane treatment even when containment is necessary

**Implementation in Abundance Society:**

Specialized containment settings, if lawfully adopted, would focus on safety, humane conditions, and available therapeutic interventions. They would be separate from punitive prisons, with the explicit goal of protecting society while treating residents with dignity. They would be temporary, nonpunitive, independently reviewed, and subject to automatic expiry; no facility may become a route to indefinite preventive detention.

## Community Safety

### Community-Based Safety Model

Safety becomes a shared responsibility, combining local knowledge with professional oversight and clear accountability.

**Principles:**
- Safety is a shared responsibility among community members, trained professionals, and oversight bodies
- Prevention over reaction
- Support over punishment
- Healing over harm

**Structure:**
- Community safety teams with trained members
- Trained mediators for conflict resolution
- Crisis responders with mental health training
- Support networks for vulnerable individuals
- Prevention programs addressing root causes

**What this model must demonstrate:**
- Faster response from people who know the community
- Community trust in safety systems
- Lower cost than traditional policing
- Better outcomes through prevention and support
- Real accountability through community involvement

### Challenges and Safeguards

Community-based safety carries serious risks. The design has to protect individual rights as well as the community.

**Potential Risks:**

1. **Vigilante Justice**
   - Untrained community members taking enforcement into their own hands
   - Escalation of conflicts through informal approaches
   - Inconsistent application of safety measures

2. **Bias and Discrimination**
   - Community members may harbor unconscious or conscious biases
   - Marginalized groups could face disproportionate scrutiny
   - Historical patterns of discrimination may replicate in community responses

3. **Accountability Gaps**
   - Lack of formal oversight structures
   - Unclear chains of responsibility
   - Potential for abuse of power by community safety personnel

4. **Privacy Concerns**
   - Community monitoring could infringe on personal autonomy
   - Pressure to conform to community norms
   - Risk of social surveillance becoming coercive

**Recommended Safeguards:**

1. **Training and Certification Requirements**
   - Mandatory training in de-escalation, bias recognition, and conflict resolution
   - Regular certification updates and competency assessments
   - Clear codes of conduct and professional standards

2. **Oversight Mechanisms**
   - Independent review boards with community representation
   - Transparent complaint and grievance procedures
   - Regular auditing of community safety decisions and outcomes
   - Data collection on demographic patterns in interventions

3. **Due Process Protections**
   - Clear criteria for interventions and containment decisions
   - Right to appeal community safety decisions
   - Documentation requirements for all interventions
   - Publicly funded counsel is required when liberty, movement, housing, essential services, substantial assets, legal status, or another severe interest is threatened; lower-impact advocacy follows the Rights and Due-Process Matrix

4. **Bias Mitigation**
   - Diverse composition of community safety teams
   - Mandatory bias training and ongoing education
   - Pairing practitioners to provide mutual accountability
   - Regular review of decision patterns for disparate impacts

5. **Transparency and Documentation**
   - Public reporting on community safety activities
   - Anonymized data on interventions and outcomes
   - Clear communication about rights and processes
   - Community forums for discussing safety concerns

6. **Professional Boundaries**
   - Clear distinction between community safety teams and law enforcement
   - Limits on authority and enforcement powers
   - Separation of support functions from decision-making powers
   - Conflict of interest policies

7. **Legal Framework**
   - Clear statutory authority for community safety programs
   - Defined scope of practice and limitations
   - Liability protections for good-faith actions
   - Legal consequences for misuse of community safety mechanisms

**The Role of External Oversight:**

While community-based safety emphasizes local control, external oversight remains essential:

- Regional review bodies may provide supplemental administrative oversight and correction, but are not a final appeal; court review remains available without exhaustion wherever the matrix requires
- Civil rights protections enforced by independent agencies
- Regular third-party evaluations of program effectiveness and equity

**Balancing Community Autonomy and Protection:**

The goal is to design systems that:
- Use community knowledge and relationships
- Protect against abuse and discrimination
- Maintain accountability through multiple mechanisms
- Adapt based on evidence and community feedback

Community involvement can improve safety, but it needs strong safeguards against causing the same harms it is meant to prevent.

### The Transformed Role of Traditional Law Enforcement

Traditional law enforcement still exists but with a transformed role:

**Transformed Role:**
- Crisis intervention for complex situations
- Complex investigations requiring specialized skills
- Protection from external threats
- Coordination with community safety teams

**Reduced Role:**
- Less focus on poverty-related crime if material security reduces it
- Less focus on drug enforcement (treatment approach)
- Less focus on minor offenses (restorative approach)
- More focus on serious harm and protection

The proposed role for traditional law enforcement is smaller and more focused on serious harm and complex situations. Whether community-based systems can handle more safety work has to be tested in practice.

## The Transition Pathway

The move from the current justice system happens in phases.

**Note:** The phases below describe a possible sequence of work, not a schedule or a crime forecast. Material security, treatment, and stronger communities are meant to reduce preventable harm. How much crime would fall, and how quickly, remains uncertain and would have to be measured during implementation.

This justice workstream nests under Chapter 24's transition periods. Its sequence can overlap or pause by jurisdiction; any expansion must identify evidence, affected-community participation, public reasons, and the applicable Chapter 24 readiness gate.

### Phase 1: Economic Security

**What to track:**
- Measure any change in survival-driven crime
- Track property-crime outcomes
- Measure changes in desperation and stress

**Actions:**
- Implement universal basic resources
- Expand mental health support
- Increase substance treatment access
- Build community infrastructure

### Phase 2: System Reform

**What to track:**
- Track availability and use of restorative justice programs
- Measure incarceration rates
- Measure outcomes for people harmed and people who cause harm

**Actions:**
- Launch restorative justice programs
- Train community safety teams
- Provide crisis intervention training
- Develop reintegration support

### Phase 3: Cultural Shift

**What to track:**
- Assess whether prevention becomes a routine community practice
- Assess shifts in community accountability norms
- Track use and outcomes of healing-centered approaches

**Actions:**
- Education on conflict resolution
- Community building initiatives
- Mental health normalization
- Widespread restorative practices

**Factors Affecting Timelines:** Several factors could accelerate or delay these phases, including: the speed of technological advancement, political and institutional readiness, public acceptance of new systems, resource allocation, coordination between communities, and the effectiveness of pilot programs. Different regions may progress at different rates based on local conditions and priorities.

## A Justice System Focused on Preventable Harm

### The Answer to the Justice Question

**"How does justice work without scarcity?"**

**Answer:**

**1. Preventable harm can be reduced**
- Material security can ease some pressures behind survival-driven crime
- Treatment and community support may help reduce some violence
- Results must be measured rather than assumed

**2. Remaining harm requires new approaches**
- Restorative justice, not punishment
- Community-based safety systems
- Treatment over incarceration
- Healing for all involved, including victims, offenders, and communities

**3. Prevention is primary**
- Economic security is meant to remove the need to commit crime to meet basic needs
- Mental health support addresses underlying issues
- Community connection provides informal accountability
- Education builds conflict resolution skills

**4. Safety is maintained**
- Appropriate response to harm at all levels
- Protection for those at risk
- Accountability for those who cause harm
- Community involvement in safety

### Key Principles for Justice in Abundance

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Prevention** | Address root causes before harm occurs |
| **Restoration** | Repair harm, don't just punish |
| **Community** | Safety is a shared responsibility |
| **Treatment** | Address underlying issues, not only symptoms |
| **Accountability** | Real responsibility, not only punishment |
| **Reintegration** | Support return to community after harm |

### The Vision

A justice system in abundance society:

- **Prevents** harm through security, support, and community
- **Responds** to harm with healing, not punishment
- **Restores** relationships and community connections
- **Protects** those at risk of harm
- **Transforms** those who cause harm

**Justice is about healing.**

Punishing survival-driven crime and then returning people to the same unmet needs does not solve the problem. Incarceration can fracture families and communities, while victims may still lack care or restitution.

This Charter offers a different path. When people have what they need, support for mental health and addiction, and strong communities, some preventable harm may decline. Restorative processes aim to help victims, hold people accountable, and strengthen communities when appropriate.

The proposal draws on evidence from specific restorative-justice programs, mental-health approaches, and research on material conditions and public safety. Comprehensive implementation at societal scale remains largely untested.
