*Real-world examples and demonstrations of abundance principles*

## Introduction

The main text presents a future vision. This appendix examines real examples where parts of it have been tested or put into practice at smaller scales.

## Case Study 1: Nordic Social Democracy

### Overview

Among existing examples, I see the Nordic countries (Denmark, Finland, Norway, Sweden, and Iceland) as the closest approximation to aspects of an abundance society, especially in public services, social protection, and worker representation.

**Historical Development (1930s–Present):**
- 1930s–1950s: In Denmark, Social Democrats led coalition governments from 1929 to 1940 and won 45 percent of the vote in 1935. The 1933 Social Reform was a major step toward the Danish welfare state. A pension law passed in 1956 took effect on 1 April 1957. ([Danmarkshistorien/Lex, Danish politics in the 1930s](https://danmarkshistorien.lex.dk/Kanslergadeforliget_og_dansk_politik_i_1930%27erne); [1956 pension law](https://danmarkshistorien.lex.dk/Lov_om_folkepensionen,_2._oktober_1956))
- 1950s–1970s: Denmark's welfare laws were based on the principle of benefits for all citizens. Denmark reformed public schooling in 1958, introduced sick pay in 1960 and disability insurance in 1965, and moved to tax-financed health insurance in 1971–1973. Enrollment in secondary and higher education grew. ([Lex, Denmark 1945–1999](https://lex.dk/Danmarks_historie_1945-1999))
- 1970s: Denmark's 1973 oil crisis prompted long-term energy planning. Its 1976 policy initially shifted power stations from oil to coal and set goals to reduce oil dependence and energy use, diversify supply, and develop renewables. In 1976, the government also funded wind-energy research and introduced subsidies for wind-generated electricity. ([Danish Energy Agency & State of Green, From Black to Green, 2021, pp. 10–11](https://ens.dk/sites/default/files/media/documents/2024-11/sog_fromblacktogreenreport_210x297_v08_web_spreads.pdf))
- 1980s–1990s: Finland, Norway, and Sweden faced financial crises around 1990. Across the Nordic region, credit-market liberalization in the 1980s and EU/EEA realignment in the 1990s brought economic and social-policy revisions while universal welfare states remained a core pillar. ([Nordic Council of Ministers, 2021, sections 1.1 and 8.1](https://pub.norden.org/temanord2021-520/))
- 2000s–Present: In Denmark, mandatory Digital Post for communicating with public authorities began in November 2014; the national system gives residents and businesses a shared digital channel for public-sector letters. ([Danish Agency for Digital Government](https://en.digst.dk/about-us/))

**Key Metrics:**
- The [World Economic Forum's 2020 summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/forum-institutional/these-are-the-10-countries-with-the-best-social-mobility/) describes the five Nordic countries as strong performers in its Global Social Mobility Index, which assessed 82 economies. The index included healthcare, education, social protection, technology access, fair wages, and work opportunities.
- The Nordic models are described as universal, tax-funded welfare states; compare public-spending shares country by country, in a named year and spending series rather than as one Nordic range ([Nordic Council of Ministers, TemaNord 2021:520](https://pub.norden.org/temanord2021-520/))
- A 2009 study describes the Nordic healthcare model as tax-funded, with universal and equal access as its aims. A 2021 review describes publicly funded, free primary and secondary schooling across the five countries, including nine or 10 years of comprehensive compulsory education ([Magnussen et al., 2009, pp. 3–5](https://eurohealthobservatory.who.int/docs/librariesprovider3/studies---external/nordic-health-care-systems.pdf); [Eriksson, 2021, pp. 30–31](https://nordicwelfare.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/FINAL_School-achievement-and-health-development-in-the-Nordic-countries.pdf)).
- In the OECD's 2023 survey, the shares reporting high or moderately high trust in national government and in other people, respectively, were: [Denmark](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results-country-notes_a8004759-en/denmark_ac5b6973-en.html)—44% and 75%; [Finland](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results-country-notes_a8004759-en/finland_596ba5da-en.html)—47% and 78%; [Iceland](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results-country-notes_a8004759-en/iceland_77669daf-en.html)—36% and 82%; [Norway](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results-country-notes_a8004759-en/norway_d9a67b9b-en.html)—48% and 76%; and [Sweden](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results-country-notes_a8004759-en/sweden_11ca1946-en.html)—43% and 62%. High or moderately high trust means a response of 6–10 on the survey's 0–10 scale.
- Worker representation: the [OECD's 2025 update](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/membership-of-unions-and-employers-organisations-and-bargaining-coverage_fe47107c-en/full-report.html) reports union membership above or close to 50% in Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Sweden, and Norway in 2024. Union membership density is the share of employees who belong to unions; adjusted bargaining coverage is the share of eligible employees covered by valid collective agreements.

### What Works

| Feature | Implementation | Associated outcome or aim, not sole causation |
|---------|---------------|--------|
| **Universal Healthcare** | A 2009 study describes a tax-funded model built around universal, equal access and low cost sharing ([Magnussen et al., 2009, pp. 3–5](https://eurohealthobservatory.who.int/docs/librariesprovider3/studies---external/nordic-health-care-systems.pdf)) | Policy aims: high health outcomes and lower inequality |
| **University tuition** | For academic year 2022/23, the [OECD reports](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/education-at-a-glance-2025_1c0d9c79-en/full-report/how-is-tertiary-education-financed_2845d742.html) tuition-free bachelor's, master's, and doctoral study for national and EU/EEA students in Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Sweden | High educational attainment and social mobility (policy aims) |
| **Strong Unions** | Union membership and bargaining coverage vary by country and year | Policy aims: worker representation and wage equality |
| **Tax-Funded Redistribution** | An OECD 2018 account describes taxes funding public services and the combined tax-and-transfer system as more redistributive than in most OECD countries [OECD (2018), sec. 2.3](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/is-the-last-mile-the-longest-economic-gains-from-gender-equality-in-nordic-countries_9789264300040-en/full-report/component-6.html) | Policy aims: fund public services and reduce inequality |
| **Parental Leave** | In 2016, all five Nordic countries provided paid leave to mothers and fathers, with fathers holding their own non-transferable paid leave periods [OECD (2018), sec. 2.4.2, fig. 2.7 (2016 entitlements)](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/is-the-last-mile-the-longest-economic-gains-from-gender-equality-in-nordic-countries_9789264300040-en/full-report/component-6.html) | Policy aims: family support and gender equality |

### Limitations

I compare the Nordic cases with the Charter's vision through five lenses:

- the relationship between growth dependence and reliable access to essentials;
- inequality and access to essentials;
- migration and inclusion;
- ecological sustainability;
- whether people can rely on essentials regardless of employment or ability to pay.

### Lessons Learned

1. **Universal programs can build broad support** - Shared access can give people a reason to defend the services they use.
2. **Trust can support cooperation** - High social trust can make cooperation easier and reduce the costs of working together.
3. **Worker representation matters** - Strong unions and worker representation can be paths toward better outcomes.
4. **Gradual reform can build momentum** - Incremental changes can help people take on larger changes over time.
5. **Culture matters** - Shared values of solidarity can support the redistribution of resources.

## Case Study 2: Bhutan's Gross National Happiness

### Overview

Bhutan's Gross National Happiness (GNH) measures people's well-being across economic and non-economic parts of life. It gives a wider view of progress than GDP's measure of economic activity ([2022 GNH Survey Report, pp. 117 and 129–131](https://bhutanstudies.org.bt/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/2022-GNH-Survey-Report_compressed-1.pdf)).

**Four Pillars of GNH:**
1. Sustainable and equitable economic development
2. Conservation of the environment
3. Preservation and promotion of culture
4. Good governance

### GNH Dimensions

The 2022 GNH Survey measures nine domains through 33 indicators ([Table 13, p. 68](https://bhutanstudies.org.bt/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/2022-GNH-Survey-Report_compressed-1.pdf)):

| Domain | Indicators in the 2022 GNH Survey |
|---|---|
| Psychological wellbeing | Life satisfaction; positive emotions; negative emotions; spirituality |
| Health | Self-reported health status; healthy days; disability; mental health |
| Time use | Work; sleep |
| Education | Schooling; literacy; value; knowledge |
| Cultural diversity and resilience | Zorig Chusum skills (artisan skills); speak native language; cultural participation; Driglam Namzha |
| Good governance | Government performance; fundamental rights; services; political participation |
| Community vitality | Donation (time & money); community relationship; family; safety |
| Ecological diversity and resilience | Ecological issue; responsibility towards environment; wildlife damage; urban issue |
| Living standards | Assets; household per capita income; housing |

### Achievements

- Bhutan's [Constitution, Article 5(3)](https://www.rcsc.gov.bt/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Constitution-of-Bhutan-Eng-2008.pdf) requires at least 60% forest cover in perpetuity. Based on field measurements from 1,969 of 2,424 planned cluster plots and mapped classifications for inaccessible plots, the [2023 National Forest Inventory](https://bfl.org.bt/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/National-Forest-Inventory-Volume-I_State-of-Forest-Report-2023.pdf) estimated forest cover at 69.71% (2.68 million hectares) in 2022.
- In Bhutan's 2015 national greenhouse-gas inventory, emissions were 3.81 million tonnes of CO2e excluding forest removals, while forests sequestered 9.39 million tonnes of CO2e, for net emissions of -5.57 million tonnes of CO2e ([Bhutan Second NDC, Annex I, p. 12](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/NDC/2022-06/Second%20NDC%20Bhutan.pdf)).
- Bhutan's constitution commits the state to free basic public health services in modern and traditional medicine and free schooling for school-age children through tenth grade. It also calls for generally available technical and professional education and equal access to higher education on merit ([Articles 9(16) and 9(21)](https://www.rcsc.gov.bt/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Constitution-of-Bhutan-Eng-2008.pdf)).
- GNH treats cultural preservation as a measurement and policy priority ([2022 GNH Survey Report, pp. 67–68, 182–183](https://bhutanstudies.org.bt/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/2022-GNH-Survey-Report_compressed-1.pdf))
- In the [2022 GNH Survey](https://ophi.org.uk/sites/default/files/2024-03/Bhutan_GNH_2022_%282023%29_compressed.pdf), 48.1% of Bhutanese aged 15 and above met the index thresholds for deeply or extensively happy. The classification measures sufficiency across weighted indicators.

### Limitations

- The World Bank's population series estimates Bhutan's midyear population at 796,682 in 2025, about 800,000; it counts de facto residents regardless of legal status or citizenship. ([World Bank population series, dataset updated 13 July 2026](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/country/BTN/indicator/SP.POP.TOTL?format=json&per_page=5); [indicator definition](https://api.worldbank.org/v2/indicator/SP.POP.TOTL?format=json))
- The World Bank's Bhutan country overview describes Bhutan as a lower-middle-income country. ([World Bank country overview, accessed 7 October 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/bhutan))
- In 2022, 12.4 percent of Bhutan's population lived below the national poverty line of Nu 6,204 per person per month, calculated using the cost-of-basic-needs method. ([World Bank, Poverty and Equity Brief: Bhutan, October 2024](https://datacatalogfiles.worldbank.org/ddh-published/0064942/9/DR0092386/Global_POVEQ_BTN.PDF))
- My starting point for using Gross National Happiness in another country is to adapt the approach to that country's needs and circumstances.
- In the fourth quarter of 2025, Bhutan's youth unemployment rate was 20.6 percent, compared with an overall unemployment rate of 3.8 percent. ([World Bank, Bhutan country overview, updated 28 April 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/bhutan))

### Lessons Learned

1. **Alternative metrics matter** - Bhutan's Gross National Happiness (GNH) is a model for keeping well-being in view alongside economic activity.
2. **Environment can be prioritized** - Bhutan's constitutional forest commitment shows how law can set environmental protection as a public priority.
3. **Culture matters** - Identity and tradition belong in policy thinking about well-being.
4. **Small scale may support experimentation** - A smaller setting may make experiments easier to carry out and measure.
5. **A holistic approach captures more** - Measures of progress should include well-being and social and environmental conditions alongside economic activity.

## Case Study 3: Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (Rojava)

### Overview

The Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (DAANES), often discussed under the name Rojava, describes its institutions in terms of local councils, women's participation, and ecological commitments. Their implementation and territorial reach have varied under war and remain disputed. This account describes principles and reported structures, not an independently verified uniform system.

**Historical Context:**
- In Jongerden's account of Rojava during the Syrian civil war, when the central state administration collapsed in 2012, locally administered communities took on security, justice, fuel, and food provision ([Jongerden, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joac.12449)).
- In Jongerden's account, Abdullah Öcalan's principles of democratic autonomy and democratic confederalism guided agricultural policy in Rojava/AANES ([Jongerden, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joac.12449)).
- The Syrian Democratic Forces and their Syrian Arab Coalition partners launched the offensive to dislodge ISIS from Raqqah on June 6, 2017. CENTCOM identified the SDF as the coalition's local ground-force partner and described coalition support for the operation ([CENTCOM, June 6, 2017](https://www.centcom.mil/MEDIA/PUBLIC-RELEASES/Article/1203949/sdf-begins-raqqah-offensive/)).
- Political situation has evolved significantly through 2024-2026

**What is Democratic Confederalism?**
Democratic confederalism, as Jongerden describes it, is Öcalan's political project of networked self-government, with an intellectual influence from Murray Bookchin's Social Ecology ([Jongerden, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joac.12449)). In Öcalan's account, six stated principles are:
- Direct democracy through local assemblies and councils, with direct elections and public participation ([Öcalan, 2017, pp. 20-25, 28](https://ocalanbooks.com/downloads/EN-brochure_democratic-confederalism_2017.pdf); [Hammy and Miley, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full)).
- Gender liberation and feminism are central to social change ([Öcalan, 2017, p. 21](https://ocalanbooks.com/downloads/EN-brochure_democratic-confederalism_2017.pdf); [Hammy and Miley, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full)).
- Ecology is central, and an alternative economy should increase society's resources rather than exploit them ([Öcalan, 2017, p. 21](https://ocalanbooks.com/downloads/EN-brochure_democratic-confederalism_2017.pdf); [Hammy and Miley, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full)).
- The proposal is multicultural and allows ethnic, cultural, and religious communities to organize and coexist ([Öcalan, 2017, pp. 21-22, 24](https://ocalanbooks.com/downloads/EN-brochure_democratic-confederalism_2017.pdf); [Hammy and Miley, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full)).
- Local communities can form confederations that connect across local, regional, national, and global levels ([Öcalan, 2017, pp. 24-25, 28](https://ocalanbooks.com/downloads/EN-brochure_democratic-confederalism_2017.pdf)).
- Öcalan describes the proposal as democracy without a state and rejects founding a separate Kurdish nation-state ([Öcalan, 2017, pp. 19-21](https://ocalanbooks.com/downloads/EN-brochure_democratic-confederalism_2017.pdf); [Hammy and Miley, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2021.815338/full)).

**Core Principles:**
- Radical democracy (neighborhood assemblies)
- Gender liberation (co-presidency, women's councils)
- Ecological economics
- Multi-ethnic cooperation
- Economic democracy (cooperatives)

The administration's [2023 Social Contract](https://rojavainformationcenter.org/storage/2023/12/Social-Contract-English-Final.pdf) describes communes and people's councils at neighborhood, town, city, canton, and regional levels. Councils coordinate and integrate their work. The diagram summarizes that stated design.

### Reported institutions and achievements (2012–2024)

- In Jongerden's historical account of Rojava, local councils formed an interconnected network, distinct from local councils elsewhere in Syria ([Jongerden, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joac.12449)).
- Under the 2023 Social Contract's stated design, commune co-chairs are elected, councils have 50% women's representation, and women's councils are provided for ([2023 Social Contract, Arts. 76, 78, and women's council provisions](https://rojavainformationcenter.org/storage/2023/12/Social-Contract-English-Final.pdf)).
- A 2020 Rojava Information Center field report documented Kurdish, Arab, and Assyrian communes represented on the Tel Nasri village council, Yazidi participation in regional bodies, and Armenian and Turkmen civic or political organizing amid war-related displacement. Participants at a 2019 Atlantic Council conference described Arab-majority local councils as having limited influence over regional decisions. ([Rojava Information Center, 2020, pp. 6, 27–28, 33–38](https://rojavainformationcenter.org/storage/2020/09/rojava-information-center-ensuring-a-future-for-ethnic-religious-minorities-final-sept-2020-medium.pdf); [Atlantic Council, 2019](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/in-depth-research-reports/report/the-future-of-northeast-syria/)).
- Jongerden's historical account of Rojava reports that local communes developed cooperatives and that local agriculture promoted less water-intensive production ([Jongerden, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joac.12449)).
- By March 2019, the Syrian Democratic Forces and international counter-ISIL coalition announced the capture of Baghuz, the last ISIL stronghold in Syria ([UN Secretary-General report, S/2021/398, para. 4](https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/atf/cf/%7B65BFCF9B-6D27-4E9C-8CD3-CF6E4FF96FF9%7D/S_2021_398.pdf)).

### Dated status note (not a live conflict update)

**Political Changes:**
- AP reported that the Syrian government fell early on December 8, 2024, after opposition forces entered Damascus ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/syria-assad-sweida-daraa-homs-hts-qatar-7f65823bbf0a7bd331109e8dff419430)).
- In a statement published on December 7, 2025, DAANES described the fall of the Ba'athist regime as a long-awaited dream and said the work of building a democratic, free, decentralized Syria still lay ahead ([DAANES statement](https://daanes.org/en/statement-to-the-public-opinion-2/)).
- On 10 March 2025, the Syrian Presidency and SDF announced an agreement to integrate civilian and military institutions in northeast Syria into state institutions. It called for executive committees to work toward implementation by the end of 2025 ([published agreement](https://sana.sy/en/syria/349228/)). [AP later reported](https://apnews.com/article/syria-sdf-kurdish-washington-military-islamic-state-dcf6869b06e95931a7a40fa4494006c2) that US-mediated negotiations had stalled for months.
- On [25 August 2026](https://channel8.com/english/news/64471), SDF commander Mazloum Abdi announced that the force had completed its integration into Syrian army brigades and would no longer operate as an independent military force.
- A [16 July 2026 European Parliamentary Research Service briefing](https://eprs.europarl.europa.eu/contents/publications/EPRS/2026/07/EPRS_BRI%282026%29791451.html?locale=EN) reported progress in integrating civilian administration, including healthcare staff and appointments, alongside obstacles in transferring judicial institutions and continuing discussions about education. It also reported that the fate of the Women’s Protection Units remained undecided. These dated reports do not establish the status of every civilian institution today.

**Dated pressures and open political constraints:**
- On 9 October 2019, Turkish armed forces supported by the Syrian National Army began Operation Peace Spring in northeast Syria; a UN Commission of Inquiry report records strikes in Tall Abyad and Ra’s al-Ayn and a ground advance beginning October 10 ([UN Commission of Inquiry, A/HRC/43/57, paras. 45–47](https://www.ecoi.net/en/file/local/2025856/a_hrc_43_57_E.pdf)).
- Amy Austin Holmes’s 2020 account of Afrin and Ra’s al-Ayn describes Turkish-affiliated militias during Turkey’s 2018 and 2019 interventions, noting that many espoused Islamist ideologies and documenting harms and displacement affecting Yezidi communities ([Holmes, July 2020, pp. 1–2](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/uploads/documents/Syrian%20Yezidis%20under%20Four%20Regimes.pdf)).
- In a statement published on December 1, 2025, DAANES said hundreds of thousands of people from Afrin and Shahba were forced to leave their homes for North and East Syria on December 2, 2024 ([DAANES statement](https://daanes.org/en/statement-to-the-public-3/)).
- In a 2019 account of Northeast Syria, Atlantic Council conference participants described the SDF’s political future as dependent on U.S. policy and exposed to Turkish military intervention ([Atlantic Council, The Future of Northeast Syria, 2019](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/in-depth-research-reports/report/the-future-of-northeast-syria/)).
- On 20 October 2021, Catalonia's regional parliament recognized the existence of the Autonomous Administration of Northeast Syria as a political entity and called for Catalan institutions to establish relations with it. The resolution highlighted democratic confederalism's potential as a peaceful, inclusive, democratic approach based on local self-government, feminism, and social ecology. ([Parliament of Catalonia, Resolution 132/XIV, BOPC 140, p. 11](https://www.parlament.cat/document/bopc/224076842.pdf)).

### Principles I Take From This Case

1. **Direct participation** — People should have direct ways to shape local decisions.
2. **Women's liberation** — Gender equality and women's freedom belong at the center of social change.
3. **Cross-community cooperation** — Build cooperation across ethnic and cultural differences.
4. **Economic democracy** — Explore cooperative ownership and decision-making at different scales.
5. **Resilience through decentralization** — Test whether decentralized institutions can continue serving people under pressure.

## Case Study 4: Mondragon Corporation

### Overview

Mondragon is a federation of worker cooperatives in the Basque Country, Spain. It demonstrates economic democracy at industrial scale.

**Key Facts:**
- Founded 1956
- [Average workforce of 70,085 people in 2024](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/people/site/assets/files/103207/annual-report-2024.pdf); this is employees, not a count of cooperative members or worker-owners
- [EUR 11+ billion annual sales (2024: EUR 11.213 billion)](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/people/site/assets/files/103207/annual-report-2024.pdf)
- Sectors: industry (manufacturing, appliances, automotive), retail (Eroski supermarket chain), finance (Laboral Kutxa banking services), and knowledge (research and education). The count of cooperatives and worker-membership total need separate dated definitions.

### Governance Model

The table summarizes the governance overview in MONDRAGON's undated media kit ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 2](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf)).

| Structure | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| **General Assembly** | Supreme governing body within each cooperative; one member, one vote in elections for cooperative governing bodies and important decisions ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 2](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf); [democratic organization principle](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/about-us/)) |
| **Company Council** | Represents members in relation to cooperative company bodies; council size depends on cooperative membership ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 2](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf)). |
| **Supervisory Committee** | Ensures compliance with accounting procedures and other procedures within its remit ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 2](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf)). |
| **Board of Directors** | Coordinates the management team's duties and advises the Governing Council; the Governing Council elects the Board's chairperson ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 2](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf)). |
| **Co-operative Congress** | Cooperatives choose its 650 delegates, and it decides the strategic criteria governing and steering MONDRAGON Corporation ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 2](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf)). |

The media kit's 2021–2024 strategic plan lists intercooperation as a priority for launching business projects ([MONDRAGON media kit, p. 3](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/MONDRAGON-media-kit-EN.pdf)).

### Economic Principles

- Worker ownership and control
- Democratic governance (one worker-one vote)
- Wage solidarity through cooperative-specific pay-ratio rules; a universal maximum of 3:1 is not established
- Profit allocation: [MONDRAGON's principles](https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/about-us/) base member allocations on work contributed, with capital's share limited and subordinate to labor. They also commit to supporting sustainable development in the local community.
- Education and R&D investment
- Inter-cooperative cooperation

### Achievements to pursue

- Economic resilience during crises
- Worker ownership and dignity
- Community investment
- Innovation and competitiveness
- Employment stability

### Challenges

I use these as possible design issues for cooperative models:

- limits on cooperative growth;
- competition with capitalist firms;
- capital constraints;
- international expansion difficulties;
- internal bureaucracy.

### Recent Challenges (2020s)

- **COVID-19 impact**: What changed in sales and employment during the pandemic at Mondragon cooperatives, including Eroski, Mondragon's retail cooperative?
- **Employment reductions**: What changes to employment and pay did cooperatives make during the pandemic?
- **Global supply chains**: Which international supply-chain disruptions affected manufacturing cooperatives?
- **Energy prices**: How did rising energy costs in Europe affect industrial cooperatives?
- **Succession**: Which cooperatives are planning a transition as their founding generations grow older and new leaders take over?
- **Growth and cooperative values**: What pressure does competition in global markets place on cooperative principles as MONDRAGON grows?
- **Non-worker employees**: Has the share of regular employees who are not worker-owners changed in any cooperative, and what would that mean for democratic governance at scale?

### Lessons I draw

1. **Worker ownership** - I see worker ownership as a way for cooperatives to compete.
2. **Democratic governance** - I want worker control to work at scale.
3. **Solidarity** - I want wage solidarity to benefit everyone.
4. **Cooperation** - Inter-cooperative support can help cooperatives grow.
5. **Education** - Training is foundational to cooperation.

## Case Study 7: Time Banks and Mutual Aid

### Overview

Edgar Cahn's Time Dollar model dates to the 1980s in Mayumi Hayashi's history ([Hayashi, 2012, pp. 32-33](https://www.ijccr.net/article/download/9150/8900/25124)). I see time banking and mutual-aid networks as ways to share resources and support community life outside traditional markets.

**How Time Banking Works:**
The model described by [TimeBanks.Org](https://www.timebanks.org/how-it-works) works like this:

- Members choose to join a network and offer services.
- One hour of service earns one time credit, whatever the service.
- Members use credits to receive services offered through the network.
- Credits record service exchanges; members can give help to one person and receive help from another.
- The network emphasizes reciprocity and community building.

Time banking represents a transitional community mechanism. In a mature post-scarcity future, this exchange system becomes unnecessary as needs are met directly. Universal Basic Resources remain rights; people do not need to earn time credits to receive them.

**Concrete Example:**
A retired teacher provides 2 hours of tutoring (earning 2 time credits). They then use those credits to receive 2 hours of home repair help from another member. No money changes hands, but both parties receive valuable services.

### Historical Development

- 1980s: Mayumi Hayashi's peer-reviewed history dates Edgar Cahn's Time Dollar model to the 1980s ([Hayashi, 2012, pp. 32-33](https://www.ijccr.net/article/download/9150/8900/25124)). A 1989 profile reported Time Dollar activity in ten U.S. states ([Nader, 1989](https://nader.org/1989/09/05/edgar-cahntime-dollar/)).
- Japan: Hayashi dates Teruko Mizushima's Volunteer Labour Bank in Osaka to 1973 and describes Fureai Kippu's expansion during the 1990s ([Hayashi, 2012, pp. 32-33](https://www.ijccr.net/article/download/9150/8900/25124)).
- 2000s: The Community Exchange System's history page dates the Cape Town Talent Exchange's internet-based Local Exchange Trading System (LETS) launch to 2003 and says the group later evolved into CES as other groups asked to share its exchange platform. CES describes itself as a global network and says it includes time banking among several exchange methods ([About CES](https://www.community-exchange.org/home/about-us/)).
- December 2021: A DCMS-commissioned analysis reported that the Covid-Mutual-Aid registry dataset listed 4,158 UK-based group entries among more than 5,500 group entries worldwide ([Avdoulos et al., 2021, p. 45](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1056530/Understanding_local_patterns_of_volunteer_activity_during_COVID-19.pdf)).

### Examples

| Network | Size | Services |
|---------|------|----------|
| **TimeBanks.Org directory** | [Directory active in September 2026](https://www.timebanks.org/timebank-map-and-directory); it describes itself as a work in progress and provides no verified total | Diverse services; confirm each listed bank's current status |
| **Community Exchange System** | Network counts need a dated public directory export and a stated coverage method | International exchange |
| **Local Mutual Aid Groups** | Neighborhood scale | Crisis support, food, resources |

### Research Evidence

In a COVID-era, non-randomized Hong Kong study conducted in 2021–22, researchers analyzed 116 timebank participants and 114 comparison participants, all aged 50 or older. After adjustment, the timebank group showed greater gains in volunteering hours within the program at six and twelve months, with no statistically detected reduction in volunteering elsewhere ([Lu et al., 2025](https://academic.oup.com/gerontologist/article/65/11/gnaf200/8253627)). Assessments of social contact, reciprocal help, mental health, resilience, economic value, and integration with formal services need their own outcome measures, comparison groups, and defined periods.

### Aims

- Build community connections
- Value all contributions equally
- Provide services without money
- Empower participants
- Create social capital
- Reduce social isolation
- Support vulnerable populations

### Questions for program design

- At what scales can a time bank work, and what changes when it grows beyond the local level?
- How do members coordinate diverse services?
- How can a program maintain service quality?
- What funding can sustain the program?
- Which services fit a time-bank model?
- How can people take part if they have no skill to offer?

### Lessons to carry forward

1. **Non-monetary exchange** - I want alternatives to money-based exchange to work for useful services.
2. **Community support** - I see mutual aid as one way communities can meet needs formal systems miss.
3. **All labor has value** - Every kind of work has value, including care and informal contributions.
4. **Connection** - I want time banks to build social connection and social capital.
5. **Local action** - I want neighborhood initiatives to help communities become more resilient.
6. **Reciprocity** - Members give help to one person and receive help from another through a shared network. I see that exchange as a way to support dignity and empowerment.

## Case Study 8: Open Source Software Movement

### Overview

The open source movement demonstrates how collaborative, non-proprietary development can create world-changing technology.

**Examples across software and shared knowledge:**
- **Software projects:** Linux (operating system); Apache (web server); Kubernetes (container orchestration)
- **Programming languages and related ecosystems:** Python; JavaScript
- **Shared knowledge:** Wikipedia (collaboratively edited encyclopedia)

### Success Factors

| Factor | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| **Open Collaboration** | Global talent pool |
| **Meritocratic Governance** | Quality-driven decisions |
| **Open-source licensing** | [Permissive and copyleft licenses](https://opensource.org/faq) allow reuse, modification, and redistribution under their terms |
| **Community Building** | Sustained engagement |
| **Transparent Development** | Trust and security |

### Economic Impact

- Foundation of modern internet infrastructure
- [An estimated $8.8 trillion in demand-side replacement value, not realized revenue or GDP (Harvard Business School working paper, 2024)](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/24-038_51f8444f-502c-4139-8bf2-56eb4b65c58a.pdf)
- Started startups and innovation
- Reduced software costs globally
- Created new business models

### Governance Challenges

- **Maintainer capacity**: In a December 2023 announcement, Apache Logging Services said three Log4j maintainers were receiving support through the Sovereign Tech Fund ([project announcement](https://logging.apache.org/blog/20231214-announcing-support-from-the-stf.html)).
- **Funding disparities**: The project said it had relied mostly on unpaid volunteers before that support.
- **Decision-making conflicts**: Disputes over project direction can lead to forks (e.g., Node.js/io.js split, Elasticsearch/Opensearch dispute)
- **Corporate influence tension**: Large corporate sponsors may exert disproportionate influence despite community governance models
- **License disputes**: Redis announced a 2024 change from BSD-3-Clause to RSALv2 and SSPLv1 ([announcement](https://redis.io/blog/redis-adopts-dual-source-available-licensing/)); MongoDB Community Server releases on or after October 16, 2018, moved from AGPLv3 to SSPL ([official FAQ](https://www.mongodb.com/legal/licensing/server-side-public-license/faq)). The Open Source Initiative said in 2021 that SSPL does not meet its Open Source Definition and explained its objections ([OSI statement](https://opensource.org/blog/the-sspl-is-not-an-open-source-license)).
- **Diversity and inclusion**: Many projects struggle with welcoming diverse contributors and addressing harassment
- **Security responsibility**: Questions about who bears responsibility for security in publicly available code

### Lessons Learned

1. **Open collaboration works** - Distributed development is effective
2. **Sharing creates value** - Open source benefits everyone
3. **Community drives quality** - Peer review improves outcomes
4. **Non-proprietary models succeed** - Open collaboration and shared ownership models are viable
5. **Public goods are valuable** - Shared resources benefit all

## Case Study 9: Participatory Budgeting

### Overview

Participatory budgeting allows citizens to directly decide how to allocate public funds.

**Origins:**
- [Started in Porto Alegre, Brazil (1989)](https://pbhandbook.participatorybudgeting.org/)
- [The Participatory Budgeting Project's handbook reports that participatory budgeting has been used in over 11,500 municipalities worldwide](https://pbhandbook.participatorybudgeting.org/)
- Large public budgets have been allocated through PB in some jurisdictions, but there is no single audited global dollar total here

### Illustrative Process

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│      PARTICIPATORY BUDGETING            │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                         │
│  1. Community Assemblies                │
│     Citizens identify priorities        │
│         ↓                               │
│  2. Idea Collection                     │
│     Proposals gathered from public      │
│         ↓                               │
│  3. Project Development                 │
│     Volunteers develop feasible plans   │
│         ↓                               │
│  4. Voting                              │
│     Residents vote under each process's │
│     participation rules                 │
│         ↓                               │
│  5. Implementation                      │
│     Winning projects are funded and     │
│     carried out under the adopted       │
│     budget and process rules            │
│                                         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Results

| City | Budget | Participation | Outcomes |
|------|--------|---------------|----------|
| **Porto Alegre** | Historical program; use a specific municipal fiscal year before quoting a budget | Voter count needs a named cycle | Infrastructure and services proposed and funded across cycles |
| **New York City** | [City Council district PB program](https://council.nyc.gov/pb/); amounts vary by fiscal year and member | Participation varies by cycle | Schools, parks, and other local projects |
| **Paris** | [Nearly €83 million for 121 projects in the 2024 cycle](https://www.paris.fr/pages/budget-participatif-2024-decouvrez-les-121-projets-laureats-28844) | 2024 participation must be taken from the city's same-cycle record | Green spaces, transport, and other projects |
| **Global Total** | No comparable audited aggregate in this directory | No common global denominator | Programs differ in authority and design |

### Reported outcomes to evaluate

- In a study of 3,650 comparable Brazilian municipal areas over 1990–2004, the main estimate associated participatory budgeting with a 3.3-percentage-point larger budget share for health and sanitation, alongside smaller shares for administration/planning, housing/urbanism, and education/culture ([Gonçalves, 2014, Table 5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.01.009)). Civic engagement and participant access should be measured through each program's attendance and access records.
- Equity, corruption, trust, and project quality should be evaluated against each program's baseline and a credible comparator, not assumed from the existence of a vote

### Limitations

- **Budget scope**: PB allocates a portion of funds; the amount and eligible uses depend on the budget and rules each process adopts.
- **Participation inequality**: A [2024 study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/state-politics-and-policy-quarterly/article/who-is-interested-in-participating-in-participatory-budgeting/58EF38C9673BC3F4694DE148EE2CDEC2) analyzed stated interest in participatory budgeting among 1,446 online opt-in survey respondents in Cook County, Illinois. Higher income and education were associated with greater interest. Younger respondents showed more interest in submitting ideas, committees, and meetings; Black and Latine respondents generally expressed more interest than White respondents.
- **Project implementation delays**: How do bureaucratic hurdles affect whether and when winning projects are completed?
- **Superficial engagement**: A [2024 World Bank review of participatory budgeting in Kenya](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099602105062425388/pdf/IDU-d07f2a43-23a7-45db-b86b-9b96408c9e4c.pdf) warns that participation which fails to shape public spending can become tokenistic. It calls for a defined budget that actually funds eligible project proposals.
- **Scalability challenges**: What changes when participatory budgeting moves from neighborhood and district decisions to city-wide decisions?
- **Technical complexity**: Residents may lack expertise to evaluate complex infrastructure proposals
- **Voter fatigue**: In its [2024 review of participatory budgeting in Kenya, section 5.1.2](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099602105062425388/pdf/IDU-d07f2a43-23a7-45db-b86b-9b96408c9e4c.pdf), the World Bank describes participants losing motivation when selected projects are not implemented, especially where feasibility checks follow community decisions.
- **Dependence on political will**: PB can be discontinued when leadership changes

### Commitments and outcomes to pursue

1. **Sound decisions** - Give residents real authority and the information to make wise budget decisions.
2. **Inclusion** - Bring people with different experiences into budget decisions.
3. **Transparency** - I want open decisions that let people see how public money is used; building trust is part of the aim.
4. **Satisfaction** - Make participation satisfying and give people a sense of ownership.
5. **Practicality at scale** - What would help participatory budgeting work across a city?

## Case Study 10: Costa Rica's Environmental Transformation

### Overview

Costa Rica's forest recovery, electricity mix, protected-area system, and payments for environmental services are distinct achievements to evaluate. None alone proves a complete environmental transformation.

**Key Actions:**
- Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES) program
- National parks expansion
- Renewable energy investment
- Net-zero emissions goal by 2050

### Results

| Metric | Dated earlier measure | Dated later measure | What the comparison can and cannot show |
|--------|-------|-------|--------|
| **Forest Cover** | Earlier national forest inventories require a matched definition | [57.1% forest cover in the 2021 national map, reported by SINAC](https://sinac.go.cr/ES/docu/Planificacion/Plan%20Estrat%C3%A9gico%20Institucional%20SINAC%202023-2030.pdf) | Recovery is visible, but a percentage change needs comparable map methods and years |
| **Renewable Electricity** | Earlier generation share needs a matched domestic-demand basis | [86.8% of domestic demand from renewables in 2024 (ICE)](https://apps.grupoice.com/CenceWeb/documentos/3/3008/23/INFORME%20GENERACION%20RENOVABLE%202024.pdf) | Electricity is not total energy; imports and drought affect the year |
| **Protected Areas** | Earlier protected-area share needs a matched SINAC series | [25.5% official terrestrial coverage by legally recognized state protected areas in the first half of 2024 (SINAC)](https://enbcr.go.cr/sites/default/files/sinac-se-de-1011-2024_informe_sinac_metas_1-2024_direccion_sinac.pdf) | Legal designation is not the same as measured biodiversity recovery |
| **Carbon Emissions** | Use an official greenhouse-gas inventory by sector | The 2050 net-zero goal is a target, not a measured current reduction | Transport and other sector emissions need the same-year inventory before assigning shares |

### Programs

1. **Payment for Ecosystem Services**
   - Pays landowners to preserve forests
   - [FONAFIFO's FAQ](https://www.fonafifo.go.cr/en/informacion-general/preguntas-frecuentes/) describes fuel-tax revenue as the largest funding source: 3.5% of fuel-tax receipts, plus 25% of water-use fees paid by concessionaires. Other funding comes from donations, agreements, and carbon credits.
   - [FONAFIFO's 2024 budget documents 284,276.7 hectares under payment in 2022](https://www.fonafifo.go.cr/media/4047/anteproyecto-plan-presupuesto-fonafifo-2024.pdf); hectares paid are not necessarily new forest or additional protection

2. **Renewable Energy Investment**
   - Hydroelectric, geothermal, wind
   - The 2024 ICE domestic-demand measure was 86.8% renewable; a days-per-year claim or a 2025 percentage requires a separate same-definition utility report
   - Goal: Net-zero emissions by 2050

3. **Protected Areas**
   - Consult SINAC's dated terrestrial and marine protected-area inventories before quoting a national percentage
   - National park system
   - Wildlife corridors

### Ongoing Challenges

- **Deforestation pressure**: Forest-protection plans need to account for pineapple and banana plantations. A study using crop maps and satellite images estimated more than 6,300 hectares of forest loss associated with pineapple crops in northern Costa Rica between 2000 and 2019, mainly in Los Chiles and San Carlos ([Girres et al., 2023](https://ica-adv.copernicus.org/articles/4/9/2023/)).
- **Tourism impact**: How should environmental plans account for the infrastructure and resource use that come with eco-tourism?
- **Transportation emissions**: Transport is a major emissions challenge; quantified claims require a cited inventory, year, and gas basis
- **Water scarcity**: Reliable water access matters for communities and agriculture. The [OECD's 2025 review, pp. 75–78](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/03/oecd-economic-surveys-costa-rica-2025_8f08995b/048cf07b-en.pdf) reports water restrictions in the Greater Metropolitan Area of San José despite Costa Rica's abundant renewable water resources. It links constrained availability to public-network losses, limited seasonal reservoirs and tanks, storage and distribution problems, and illegal connections.
- **Climate vulnerability**: What do rising temperatures and changing rainfall mean for biodiversity and agriculture?
- **Economic dependency**: How do banana, coffee, and pineapple production and exports shape environmental pressure?
- **Urban sprawl**: How does metropolitan expansion around San José affect nearby ecosystems?
- **Funding sustainability**: [FONAFIFO's FAQ](https://www.fonafifo.go.cr/en/informacion-general/preguntas-frecuentes/) identifies fuel-tax revenue as the largest PES funding source, alongside water-use fees and other contributions. If electric-vehicle adoption reduces fuel-tax receipts, other funding would need to cover any gap to keep payments going.

### Lessons Learned

1. **Environmental protection is a firm commitment.** Does eco-tourism revenue cover conservation costs and create net benefits for the people affected?
2. **Policy can contribute** - PES is one of several policies associated with conservation; its additional effect needs evaluation
3. **Renewable energy is achievable** - Costa Rica has reached high renewable-electricity shares, but the dated 2024 measure above is 86.8% of domestic demand, not “near 100%” for that year or for total energy
4. **Reversing damage is possible** - Deforestation can be reversed
5. **Small nations can lead** - Costa Rica influences global policy

## Synthesis: Lessons From the Cases

### How I Compare These Cases

These are the lessons I take from the ten cases. I use three questions to judge how useful a lesson could be for the Charter:
- **Cross-case presence**: Does the element appear in 5+ case studies?
- **Documented impact**: What positive outcomes do the original sources document?
- **Transferability**: What would need to change for the principle to work in a different setting or at a different scale?

### What We Can Learn From These Cases

| Factor | What I take from the cases |
|--------|----------|
| **Participation works** | PB, Rojava, and the Nordic models offer ways for people to take part in decisions that affect them |
| **Cooperation beats competition** | Mondragon, open source, and time banks show different ways people can build through shared work and resources |
| **Alternative metrics matter** | Bhutan's GNH makes well-being part of how progress is measured |
| **Technology supports democracy** | Estonia shows how digital public services and online voting can be organized |
| **Environment and economy can align** | Costa Rica brings conservation, renewable electricity, and economic activity into the same case |
| **Social investment pays off** | Kerala gives me a reason to put education and health at the center of development |
| **Small scale supports experimentation** | I would test approaches locally, learn from the results, and use that experience before wider adoption |
| **Culture matters** | Nordic trust, Bhutan values, and Rojava solidarity point to the importance of people's values when they build institutions |

### Transferable Principles

1. **Universal access** - Services for all, not selective
2. **Democratic control** - People govern systems affecting them
3. **Solidarity** - Mutual support rather than competition
4. **Transparency** - Open processes build trust
5. **Sustainability** - Long-term thinking over short-term gain
6. **Inclusion** - Marginalized voices central to decisions
7. **Adaptation** - Learning and adjusting based on results

## Conclusion

Parts of an abundance society are already being tried. None of these cases is a complete model; together they offer approaches tested in different settings, with mixed evidence and limits on transferability:

- **Nordic Model** - Universal services and worker protection
- **Bhutan** - Well-being metrics and environmental protection
- **Rojava** - Direct democracy and gender liberation
- **Mondragon** - Worker ownership and economic democracy
- **Estonia** - Digital governance and participation
- **Kerala** - Social investment despite limited resources
- **Time Banks** - Non-monetary exchange and community support
- **Open Source** - Collaborative creation and sharing
- **Participatory Budgeting** - Direct citizen control of resources
- **Costa Rica** - Environmental restoration and renewable energy

People are already building, testing, and improving these approaches around the world. The question is what we can scale, what needs adapting, and how quickly we can do it.
