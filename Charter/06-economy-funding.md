# Chapter 6: Economy and Funding

*How to fund abundance and find meaning when survival doesn't require labor*

---

## Introduction: The Twin Challenges of Post-Scarcity

> **Illustrative future vignette:** Maria's day describes one possible mature-condition scenario, not a prediction or universal promise. It assumes that Universal Basic Resources have been lawfully adopted and reliably delivered while the funding and governance questions in this chapter remain under public review.

Maria Chen works in an art studio at the community hub, surrounded by VR equipment and creative tools. She spent the morning building an immersive experience about climate adaptation. There is no deadline, no boss demanding output, and no fear that the piece will fail to pay her bills. Universal Basic Resources cover her needs. She creates because it matters to her, connects with others, and lets her contribute.

That life still has to be paid for and built.

The transition to abundance society faces two intertwined challenges:

1. **The Funding Problem:** How do we pay for universal basic resources, infrastructure, and public goods when traditional economic models break down?

2. **The Meaning Problem:** When work is no longer required for survival, how do people find purpose and identity?

These issues cannot be separated. The economy shapes the values people can live by. Funding that depends on endless growth runs into ecological limits. Tying human worth to productivity keeps the same scarcity mindset in place.

This chapter addresses both problems: funding abundance through taxation and ownership during the transition, and helping people find purpose beyond a job.

## The Funding Challenge

### Why Current Economic Models Fail

The current economy depends on assumptions that will not hold in an abundance future:

**The Labor-Based Tax Model**

In fiscal year 2024, individual income taxes provided about half of U.S. federal revenue and payroll taxes provided about 35%.[^labor-tax-sources] These categories are heavily connected to work, but individual income taxes also include some nonlabor income:
- Individual income tax: about 50%
- Payroll taxes: about 35%
- Corporate income tax: about 11%
- Other sources: about 5%

**Note:** Tax mixes differ by country. Many governments rely heavily on taxes connected to income and employment, while others rely more on consumption taxes.

[^labor-tax-sources]: **Federal Revenue Sources:** CBO Infographic (FY 2024): https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61185 | Tax Foundation (2024): https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/federal/us-tax-revenue-by-tax-type-2024/ | U.S. Treasury Fiscal Data (2026 YTD): https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/americas-finance-guide/government-revenue/ | Tax Policy Center: https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/what-are-sources-revenue-federal-government

This model draws heavily on income and payroll. If AI and robotics reduce paid work, those sources could shrink just as more people need support after losing work. The size and timing of any change remain uncertain.

**The Growth Imperative**

Current economic systems require continuous growth to function. When growth slows:
- Unemployment rises
- Tax revenue falls
- Debt becomes unsustainable
- Social instability increases

Infinite growth on a finite planet is impossible. Living well cannot continue to depend on endless material expansion.

**The Scarcity-Based Value System**

In scarcity economics, value comes from relative shortage. Things are valuable because they're rare. This creates incentives for:
- Artificial scarcity (patents, monopolies)
- Planned obsolescence
- Resource hoarding
- Competitive consumption

These incentives contradict the abundance model, which seeks to make essential resources widely available.

### The Economic Foundation of Abundance

The proposed economy starts somewhere else:

**Post-Scarcity Principle**

As AI and robotics perform more routine production labor, and as validated energy, materials, repair, and circular-manufacturing advances reduce some costs, those gains should expand reliable access for everyone. The question changes from "how do we allocate scarcity?" to "how do we manage expanding abundance within ecological limits?"

**Universal Basic Resources (UBR)**

UBR is a rights floor of direct goods and services, guaranteed without employment conditions or point-of-use charges. It is not the same as universal basic income (UBI). The canonical scope is:

**Core necessities:**
- Nutritious food and safe water, including sanitation where appropriate
- Safe, adequate housing
- Comprehensive healthcare, including mental healthcare
- Education and lifelong learning

**Universal enabling services:**
- Adequate basic energy
- Free transportation sufficient to reach essential services and participate in community life
- Reliable connectivity and communications
- An adequate basic computing device for each individual
- Necessary assistive technologies for people who need them
- Repair, maintenance, accessibility support, and reasonable replacement for enabling equipment and services

These guarantees are free at the point of use, not resource-costless: energy, materials, labor, maintenance, infrastructure, and ecological impacts remain part of public accounting. Delivery may vary by geography and technology, but minimum adequacy standards and equal rights do not. Communities may democratically add locally appropriate resources within ecological capacity and the rights floor.

During the transition, UBI or other cash support is a separate supplement. It may fill gaps while direct UBR services are incomplete and support personal discretionary needs. It must be reviewed as UBR becomes adequate; this Charter does not require a permanent cash dividend or promise to abolish all cash support.

**The New Economic Question**

If basic needs are guaranteed and production is largely automated, how do we fund the system?

## Funding the Transition (Years 1-30)

Before we can live in abundance, we must build it. This requires resources, and during the transition, we still use scarcity-based tools to get there. The mechanisms in this section are proposed bridges. Whether and when they can be retired depends on how the infrastructure and public needs develop.

### The Transition Challenge

The problem is circular: abundance needs AI and robotics infrastructure, but building that infrastructure requires resources before abundance exists. Where do those resources come from?

The proposal redirects existing resources and captures value during the transition. These are bridge tools, not the destination. The numbers below are examples for debate, not forecasts. Actual rates and revenue would depend on what is taxed, the jurisdiction, avoidance, behavior changes, and democratic decisions.

### Transition Funding Mechanisms

If AI and robotics handle a larger share of production, the economic base could change and taxes tied to paid work could weaken. The transition may need ways to capture value from the systems doing the production.

> **Status guide:** The mechanisms in this section are **draft policy options**. Their rates, dollar figures, worked examples, and revenue calculations are **illustrative numbers** for debate, not forecasts, adopted tax law, or commitments. Their feasibility depends on evidence, jurisdiction, administrative design, behavior, and public decisions.

Funding, tax, ownership, and cash mechanisms listed here are evaluated alternatives or complements, not an automatic cumulative mandate. The shared commitment is the tiered UBR rights floor; UBI or other cash support remains a separate transitional supplement. Public, cooperative, commons, regulated private, and mixed ownership can vary by context only when public-purpose safeguards, rights, oversight, and remedies are met. The canonical fiscal decisions are set out in the Master Fiscal-Mechanism Crosswalk below.

### Governing principle: an adaptive phase-based portfolio

This chapter treats fiscal policy as an adaptive portfolio rather than a single permanent package. During the transition, jurisdictions may combine mechanisms that address different needs, subject to common rights, transparency, ecological, anti-capture, and anti-double-charging safeguards. A mechanism may be complementary, substitutive, phase-specific, or a locally selected alternative. None is automatically permanent, and none is automatically mandatory at the same time as every other mechanism.

As reliable public provision, automated production, abundant clean energy, public productive assets, and lower material scarcity mature, taxes and ordinary user-facing prices may shrink or retire. A calendar date alone does not change a mechanism: evidence-based readiness gates control actual changes, while Chapter 24 supplies shared planning periods. Communities may adapt their portfolios to local conditions within the shared Charter floor, and alternatives that conflict are labeled as alternatives rather than simultaneous commitments.

Every proposed Charter rate, threshold, valuation, revenue projection, worked example, and modeled outcome in this chapter is illustrative unless the text expressly identifies the adopting jurisdiction and lawful authority, adoption date and effective period, covered base and exclusions, evidence and uncertainty, review and expiry conditions, and a source where the current adopted rule can be verified. Dated historical rates and empirical observations remain evidence labeled by place and date; they do not become illustrative Charter policy merely by appearing here. Repetition does not adopt a number. Illustrative numbers may explain mechanics or support debate; they are not Charter rates, revenue promises, or forecasts.

## Portfolio overlap and accounting rules

Each mechanism must disclose its tax, rent, fee, asset, transaction, extraction, or accounting base. Chapter 6 labels relationships as additive for distinct purposes, substitutive, phase-specific, or competing alternatives. Where bases materially overlap, implementing law must use credits, exemptions, deductions, rate adjustments, or a documented priority rule. A citizen assembly may authorize an overlap only after distributional and legal review, with the public purpose and interaction disclosed.

Revenue estimates must not count the same or overlapping base or value twice. A jurisdiction must disclose omitted mechanisms, chosen substitutes, expected interactions, administrative costs, uncertainty, and retirement logic. A public asset's monetary return must not be counted separately from the same enterprise's tax base without an explicit accounting explanation. The rights floor cannot be declared funded by adding overlapping projections as though they were independent.

## Energy accounting, payment, and allocation

Three distinct concepts govern energy and compute:

1. **Physical accounting:** full lifecycle energy and other constrained inputs used to understand production, maintenance, alternatives, and ecological effects.
2. **User-facing payment:** currency or another transferable payment demanded from an individual. Guaranteed essentials do not use this model at the point of use.
3. **Administrative allocation:** a rights-respecting decision about scarce capacity using need, urgency, public benefit, scientific value, ecological capacity, regional equity, affected-community input, and fair treatment of equally ranked requests.

Automated production costs may increasingly converge on full lifecycle energy requirements as evidence improves, but that is a direction and accounting model, not a household bill or an adopted tax base. Materials, land, ecological capacity, infrastructure bottlenecks, uncertainty, maintenance, recycling, and restoration remain visible constraints until evidence shows genuine substitution. Energy accounting does not prove that a good is abundant, environmentally acceptable, safe, or available. Guaranteed essentials, including adequate basic energy and computing devices, remain free at the point of use; scarce above-baseline capacity is allocated publicly rather than sold to the highest bidder.

For technical energy-accounting definitions and calculations, see [Energy Cost Formula in Post-Scarcity](#appendix-appendix-energy-cost-formula), [EROEI Calculation Framework](#appendix-appendix-energy-eroei-framework), and [Fission EROEI Calculation: Inputs and Limits](#appendix-appendix-energy-fission-eroei-calculation).

## Scarce Land and Destination Access

### Transition phase

Primary housing remains part of the guaranteed rights floor. Monetary land or resource rent may apply to exclusive commercial, luxury, vacant, speculative, or unusually intensive use, subject to lawful transition protections. Public rights of first refusal, vacancy rules, anti-speculation safeguards, ecological limits, notice, proportionality, review, appeal, and remedies may gradually expand common access. Existing rights may not be removed through arbitrary or retroactive action.

### Mature nonmonetary phase

Most scarce destination capacity (such as beaches, lakefronts, mountain destinations, and similar amenities) uses public, community, Indigenous, or cooperative lodging and access systems consistent with lawful ownership and sovereignty. Transparent reservations, rotating queues, and lotteries distribute oversubscribed access. Annual stay limits and population-sensitive cooldown periods respond to demand and the size of the eligible population.

A limited share may use time-limited, nontransferable stewardship leases for people or groups that maintain a place and fulfill public conditions. Stewardship rights cannot be sold, privately traded, accumulated, converted into permanent control, or automatically inherited. Permanent local communities, Indigenous sovereignty and treaty rights, accessibility, ecological capacity, safety, restoration, and essential-service needs take precedence over holiday access. No applicant receives priority merely because of former wealth, political office, administrative status, or side payment. Published rules, privacy-protecting records, conflict controls, complaints, review, appeal, and remedies apply.

## Revenue priority during the transition

Subject to lawful budgets and local conditions, transition revenue follows this default priority:

1. Guarantee the Universal Basic Resources rights floor and enabling access.
2. Maintain, repair, replace, and safely operate existing systems.
3. Fund accessibility, resilience reserves, continuity capacity, and emergency preparation.
4. Repair ecological damage and stay within ecological limits.
5. Support underserved regions and treaty-based global capacity building.
6. Expand productive and service capacity after the earlier obligations are credible.
7. Distribute individual monetary dividends only after the preceding obligations and reserves are satisfied.

This is a priority rule, not a promise that one funding source alone can meet every obligation. Jurisdictions must disclose shortfalls and may not claim the rights floor is funded by counting the same revenue twice.

## Treaty-based global capacity contribution

Global transition support operates through ratified treaties or agreements with enumerated authority. Contributions may consider measurable ability, present resource use, historical benefit, ecological burden, and unmet global needs. Each agreement must define contribution methodology, permitted uses, transparency, audits, procurement, technology transfer, affected-community participation, privacy, anti-corruption controls, appeals, withdrawal, amendment, and expiry.

No international body receives general taxing power, domestic fiscal control, or authority outside the ratified instrument. Contributions may be monetary while money exists and may later become energy, equipment, knowledge, infrastructure, training, maintenance support, or productive capacity. This model creates no central world tax authority or unratified global government.

## Review, renewal, and retirement

Every active mechanism must publish its purpose and beneficiaries; phase and relationship to other mechanisms; legal and administrative owner; base, exemptions, credits, and overlap handling; revenue and cost results with uncertainty; distributional, rights, ecological, behavioral, and administrative effects; readiness, failure, and retirement indicators; complaint, appeal, correction, and remedy channels; and review and automatic expiry dates.

Administrators report annually. At least every four years, a newly selected citizen assembly conducts a full renewal, revision, or retirement review using fresh evidence and ordinary Chapter 5 conflict rules. Earlier review is triggered by material revenue failure, inequitable burden, rights violations, access deterioration, ecological-limit breach, major administrative cost, corruption or capture evidence, material evasion, economic instability, or credible evidence that a safer or simpler substitute can meet the purpose.

A mechanism expires unless lawfully renewed with a public rationale and current evidence. A review may continue, revise, pause, reverse, or retire a mechanism. Institutional dependence on revenue is evidence to manage during transition, not sufficient justification for permanent renewal.

### 1. Resource and Production Tax (The "Robo-Tax")

**What:** Tax the output of AI and robotics systems

**How It Works:**
- Every unit of production (goods, services) has a small tax
- Automated collection at the point of production
- No human labor required to pay

**Examples:**
- Robot produces 1,000 cars → tax collected on all 1,000
- AI generates $1 million in value → tax collected automatically
- Farm produces 100 tons of food → tax collected at harvest

**Advantages:**
- Doesn't depend on human employment
- Scales with production (more abundance = more revenue)
- Hard to evade (automated collection)
- Incentivizes efficiency

**Challenges:**
- Requires tracking all production
- Could discourage production if rate too high
- Needs international coordination to prevent arbitrage

**Crosswalk status:** This is a transition-only complementary tool while substantial private automated production remains. Publicly owned direct provision or evidence of unnecessary or harmful effects can support retirement; no calendar date retires it automatically.

**Illustrative rate for debate:** 5-15% of production value
- Note: This estimate assumes widespread automation where AI and robotics constitute the majority of production. The actual rate required depends on total economic output, administrative costs, and the scope of universal basic resources provided. Economic modeling for post-scarcity taxation remains an emerging field with varying projections.

### 2. Energy and Compute Accounting and Optional Transition Levy

**What:** Account for energy consumption and computing resources; a separately adopted levy may be considered during infrastructure buildout.

**How It Works:**
- AI and robotics require massive energy and compute
- Measure lifecycle energy, capacity, maintenance, infrastructure, and ecological effects
- A jurisdiction may evaluate an illustrative levy per kilowatt-hour or compute-hour, but accounting is not itself a tax or a user-facing charge

**Advantages:**
- Directly targets the productive infrastructure
- Energy and compute are measurable and trackable
- Aligns with environmental goals (can make rates progressive)

**Example:**
- Data center uses 100 million kWh/year → tax collected
- Robot factory consumes 50 million kWh/year → tax collected
- AI training run uses 1 million compute-hours (equivalent to one hour of processing across 1 million parallel compute units) → tax collected

**Challenges:**
- Energy and compute efficiency improvements reduce the tax base
- May need to adjust rates over time
- Requires accurate metering

**Crosswalk status:** Physical accounting remains useful while capacity constraints exist. Any optional levy is a phase-specific transition tool, coordinated with production and ecological rules, and may be revised or retired as evidence changes; the adequate energy and computing baseline remains free at the point of use.

### 3. Land and Resource Rent (Georgist Model)

**What:** Tax the value of natural resources and land

**How It Works:**
- Land value tax (LVT) on all property
- Resource extraction fees (minerals, water, etc.)
- Spectrum rights, orbital slots, and other natural monopolies

**Philosophy:**
- Land and natural resources are shared heritage
- Individuals can use them, but pay rent to society
- Unlike labor, land cannot be created by individuals

**Advantages:**
- Doesn't penalize production or innovation
- Encourages efficient land use
- Captures value that belongs to everyone
- Hard to hide or move

**Example:**
- Urban land value: $10,000/sq ft → 5% annual rent
- Mineral rights: $1 million extraction value → 10% fee
- Spectrum licenses: $100 million/year → 15% rent

**Challenges:**
- Requires accurate valuation
- Opposition from existing landowners
- Transition from current property taxes

**Crosswalk status:** Monetary rent is transition-specific for exclusive scarce use. In a mature nonmonetary phase, stewardship rights, ecological limits, demonstrated need, rotation, queues, and lotteries replace monetary rent where money is no longer required; primary housing remains guaranteed.

**Illustrative rate for debate:** 5-20% of land/resource value annually

### 4. Financial Transaction Tax

**What:** Small tax on all financial transactions

**How It Works:**
- 0.1% on stock trades
- 0.05% on bond transactions
- 0.01% on currency exchanges

**Advantages:**
- Captures value from speculation and trading
- Hard to evade (all transactions pass through regulated channels)
- Doesn't depend on human employment
- Discourages harmful high-frequency trading

**Example:**
- Stock market trading: ~$50-100 trillion annually in major markets → $50-100 billion/year at 0.1%
- Global FX trading: ~$9.5 trillion/day average (BIS 2025 Survey on Foreign Exchange and OTC Derivatives)[^ch6-bis-fx-turnover]
- Crypto trading (perpetual contracts): ~$86 trillion annually (CoinGecko 2025 Annual Report - CEX perpetual contracts only; all perpetual swaps CEX+DEX = $92.9T)
- Total global financial assets under management: ~$300+ trillion (varies by measurement)

**Note:** Revenue estimates vary widely based on:
- Transaction types included (spot vs. derivatives, centralized vs. decentralized)
- Behavioral responses to the tax (transactions may shift to untaxed venues)
- International coordination effectiveness
- Actual trading volumes are difficult to measure comprehensively

Sources: Bank for International Settlement (BIS) surveys, World Federation of Exchanges, McKinsey Global Payments Report

[^ch6-bis-fx-turnover]: Bank for International Settlements, *Global FX markets when hedging takes centre stage* (2025), reports that over-the-counter foreign-exchange turnover averaged $9.5 trillion per day in April 2025: https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2512b.htm

**Note on Crypto Trading Volume:** The ~$86 trillion figure refers specifically to centralized exchange perpetual contract volume (CoinGecko 2025 Annual Report). Total crypto market capitalization at end of 2025 was approximately $3.0 trillion. Spot/exchange trading volume in H1 2025 was approximately $9.36 trillion (SSRN Paper).

**Challenges:**
- May drive transactions offshore
- Requires international coordination
- Could reduce legitimate trading

**Crosswalk status:** This is a supplemental transition tool while monetary and speculative markets remain material, not a substitute for regulation or enforcement. It retires as those markets diminish or evidence shows the tool is unnecessary or harmful.

### 5. Sovereign Wealth Fund

**What:** Government owns stakes in AI and robotics companies

**How It Works:**
- Government takes equity in major AI and robotics firms
- Dividends flow to public treasury
- Similar to Norway's oil fund, but for AI and automation

**Advantages:**
- Direct ownership of productive capacity
- Appreciates with technology growth
- No need to tax (dividends are return on ownership)

**Example (Long-term projection):**
- Government owns 10% of major AI companies
- AI sector generates $1 trillion profit (projected at maturity) → $100 billion dividend
- Reinvested or distributed to citizens

**Challenges:**
- Requires initial capital to acquire stakes
- Government management of investments
- Potential conflicts of interest

**Crosswalk status:** Public ownership income is transition revenue while money exists, coordinated with tax bases and subject to audit, rights, ecological, and anti-capture safeguards. Its mature function is direct productive capacity and provision, not revenue as the primary objective.

### 6. Consumption Tax (VAT/GST)

**What:** Tax consumption rather than production

**How It Works:**
- Value-added tax on all goods and services
- Progressive rates (basics lower, luxuries higher)
- Universal basic income offsets impact on poor

**Advantages:**
- Captures value from all consumption
- Doesn't depend on employment
- Encourages saving and investment

**Challenges:**
- Regressive without UBI
- Requires comprehensive tracking
- May reduce consumption

The abundance goal is wider access to useful goods and services within ecological limits.

**Crosswalk status:** This is a limited transition fallback. Guaranteed essentials remain exempt and free at the point of use; lower-income burdens require offsets. The mechanism declines and retires as direct provision replaces ordinary monetary consumption.

**Illustrative rate for debate:** 10-20% VAT with UBI offset

### 7. Data and Information Tax

**What:** Tax commercial data processing and monetization

**How It Works:**
- **Data Processing Fee:** Small tax per unit of data processed for commercial AI training
  - Example: $0.01 per 1,000 data points processed for model training
- **Data Monetization Tax:** Percentage of revenue derived from data-driven products
  - Example: 1-5% of advertising revenue from targeted ads
- **Data Dividend:** Portion of revenue distributed to individuals whose data was used
  - Individual opt-in/opt-out mechanisms

**Advantages:**
- Data is a key resource for AI and digital economy
- Everyone generates data, creating broad-based revenue
- Aligns with privacy regulations (GDPR, CCPA)
- Creates incentive for data minimization and privacy protection

**Challenges:**
- Privacy concerns require strong safeguards
- Valuation of data remains debated
- International coordination needed to prevent arbitrage
- Technical infrastructure for tracking data usage required

**Crosswalk status:** This is a transition mechanism only while commercial extraction or monetization of personal or community data persists. It never substitutes for consent, minimization, deletion, privacy, security, or remedies, and retires when data is governed as a protected relationship rather than a commodity.

**Illustrative revenue calculation:**
- Global digital advertising: ~$600 billion/year
- If 2-5% taxed: $12-30 billion/year (modest but growing with digital economy)

**Note:** This mechanism is more speculative than other funding sources and requires significant regulatory framework development.

### 8. Public Ownership and Universal Basic Assets

**What:** Instead of funding government, provide assets directly

**How It Works:**
- Every citizen owns stake in AI and robotics infrastructure
- Dividends from productive assets
- Could reduce reliance on some traditional taxes

**Philosophy:**
- Productive capacity is publicly owned
- Everyone gets share of output
- Government role shifts from funding to coordination

**Advantages:**
- Broad access to abundance
- Could simplify some parts of the tax system
- Aligns ownership with beneficiaries

**Challenges:**
- Requires massive reallocation
- Transition from current ownership
- Governance of public assets

**Crosswalk status:** Public, cooperative, commons, regulated private, and mixed ownership are locally selectable arrangements only with public-purpose safeguards, rights, oversight, and remedies. While money exists, public-asset returns may fund the rights floor; the mature function is direct provision and productive capacity, with distribution rules still subject to review.

### 9. Wealth Tax (Transition and Anti-Concentration)

**What:** Tax concentrated private wealth while large fortunes and money materially shape control and access.

**Transition role:** Wealth taxation can fund the rights floor, capitalize public assets, and limit concentrated private control. It is a transition and anti-concentration mechanism, coordinated with inheritance, land, production, and public-ownership rules rather than assumed to run alongside every other mechanism.

**Status:** Any threshold, rate, base, exemption, or revenue estimate is illustrative unless it includes the complete adoption record stated above. Lawful enforcement, valuation, appeals, privacy, and treaty coordination belong in Chapter 20; Chapter 6 owns the portfolio role and retirement logic.

**Retirement condition:** The mechanism declines or retires when private fortunes, concentrated ownership, and money no longer have meaningful control effects, or when evidence shows it unnecessary or harmful. Institutional dependence on its revenue is not sufficient reason for permanent renewal.

## The Post-Scarcity Economy (Years 30+)

If infrastructure makes some essentials much cheaper to produce, communities may be able to provide more of them directly. Whether bridge taxes, bonds, and wealth transfers remain necessary will depend on costs, resources, and public decisions at the time.

### Why the Role of Taxation Could Change

In the proposed mature economy, these changes could reduce the need for some traditional taxes:

1. **More publicly owned production** - Sovereign wealth funds and public infrastructure could change how productive assets are financed

2. **Less reliance on labor income** - AI and robotics could handle more production while human work becomes less tied to survival

3. **More direct allocation** - Some basic resources could be provided directly instead of purchased

4. **Lower production costs for some goods** - Energy is one cost, alongside materials, maintenance, labor, land, and ecological limits

### Lifecycle-Energy Accounting, Payment, and Allocation

Full lifecycle energy can serve as a physical production-accounting denominator as automated systems mature, alongside materials, land, maintenance, infrastructure, uncertainty, recycling, restoration, and ecological impacts. It is not automatically a price, a household bill, a tax base, or permission to ignore non-energy constraints:

- **Food:** Cost of energy to grow, harvest, and distribute
- **Housing:** Cost of energy to build and maintain
- **Transportation:** Cost of energy to operate
- **Manufacturing:** Energy and other lifecycle inputs required to produce and maintain goods

There are no profit margins, scarcity premiums, or markups passed on as point-of-use charges for the guaranteed UBR floor. A physical accounting signal supports planning; administrative allocation of scarce capacity uses need, urgency, public benefit, scientific value, ecological capacity, regional equity, affected-community input, and fair treatment of equally ranked requests, not ability to pay. Guaranteed essentials remain free at the point of use. Scarce land, materials, ecological capacity, unique locations, infrastructure bottlenecks, and limited time slots remain visible until evidence supports genuine substitution.

### How Economic Life Works

**Universal Basic Resources (UBR)**

Chapter 6's canonical UBR standard applies here: a core rights floor plus universal enabling services, delivered without employment conditions or point-of-use charges. Energy-cost accounting describes the real physical costs of provision; it is not a user-facing bill for guaranteed services.

**Currency During and After the Transition**

During the transition phase (Chapter 24's Years 1-30 periods), currency continues to support:
- Luxuries beyond the baseline
- Custom, handcrafted creations
- Extras and premium experiences
- Non-essential goods and personal discretionary needs

UBI or other cash support may temporarily fill gaps in direct provision, but remains separate from UBR and subject to review as UBR becomes adequate.

In a mature abundance economy, money may become less important for essentials. Scarce goods and ecological limits would still need rules for allocation.

Survival never depends on earning currency.

### The End of "Funding"

In a mature abundance economy, the funding question may change as more essentials are:
- Directly allocated based on need
- Produced at energy cost
- Publicly owned through public infrastructure

The economic question shifts from distributing scarcity to taking care of shared abundance.

## The Meaning Crisis

### The Problem

When Maria Chen creates her VR art, she's not doing it for survival. She's doing it because it matters to her. But not everyone has found this equilibrium.

The transition creates a serious personal and cultural problem:

**Current Reality:**
- Work matters to many people's identity
- Work provides structure, purpose, and social connection
- "What do you do?" is often the first question we ask new people

**What the surveys say:** A 2014 Gallup poll found that 55% of U.S. workers got a sense of identity from their job.[^ch6-gallup-work-identity] In 2023, Pew found that 73% said their job or career was at least somewhat important to their overall identity.[^ch6-pew-work-identity] These are different questions, not a single range.

[^ch6-gallup-work-identity]: Gallup, “In U.S., 55% of Workers Get Sense of Identity From Their Job” (2014), reports that 55% of U.S. workers said they get a sense of identity from their job: https://news.gallup.com/poll/175400/workers-sense-identity-job.aspx
[^ch6-pew-work-identity]: Pew Research Center, *How Americans View Their Jobs* (2023), reports that 39% of U.S. workers said their job or career was extremely or very important to their overall identity and another 34% said it was somewhat important: https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/03/30/how-americans-view-their-jobs/

**The Fear:**
- "If I don't work, what am I?"
- "What will I do all day?"
- "Will I still be valuable?"

This is the meaning crisis. Losing that identity can leave people isolated or unsure of their purpose, especially when support and new ways to contribute are missing.

### Why Work-Based Meaning is Problematic

Even in the current system, work-based identity has serious problems:

1. **Artificial Scarcity:** Work is valuable because it's scarce, not inherently meaningful
2. **Inequality:** Some work is valued more (higher pay) regardless of actual contribution
3. **Exploitation:** People trapped in meaningless jobs for survival
4. **Waste:** Human potential wasted on repetitive, automated tasks
5. **Mental Health:** Work stress, burnout, identity crisis upon job loss

#### 3. Learning and Mastery

- Personal growth

**Why It Works:**

#### 4. Exploration and Adventure

**Examples:**

**Why It Works:**

#### 5. Spiritual and Philosophical Pursuits

## Supporting the Meaning Transition

People cannot rebuild a work-based identity overnight. They will need support during the transition.

**Actions:**

**Structure:**

**Illustrative model, not a forecast:** ~$2-4 trillion/year initially (based on ~20% of global GDP from industrial production × 10% tax rate); actual revenue could be much lower or higher.

## Final Thoughts: Beyond Scarcity Thinking

Post-scarcity economics requires more than technical tools. It changes how we think about value, work, and human purpose.

For centuries, we have treated scarcity as natural and inevitable. We built institutions, laws, and identities around it. Changing that takes imagination and courage.

The difference between the transition and mature post-scarcity is vital. During the transition, we must use the tools available: progressive taxation, wealth funds, public investment, and targeted resource guarantees. Those bridges are not the destination.

In mature post-scarcity, survival is detached from wages. Physical energy and ecological boundaries guide resource accounting, and resources are allocated directly so people can live well. When survival is guaranteed and work becomes voluntary, creative possibilities suppressed for millennia become available.
