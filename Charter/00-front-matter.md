## Charter Summary

Material scarcity is not an unchangeable law of physics. Automated manufacturing, renewable energy networks, and machine intelligence give us powerful tools to expand access to food, housing, healthcare, and education, while land, materials, care, infrastructure, and ecological capacity can remain constrained.

But those gains will not distribute themselves. If control over computing power, patents, and automated capital stays concentrated, we move directly toward techno-feudalism: a small group of platform monopolists collecting private rents while most people face structural unemployment and insecurity.

This Charter offers a different direction: a society where basic survival no longer depends on wage labor. It maps the institutions, local government, and economic tools needed to make abundance a shared human right instead of an elite privilege.

### Much of today’s scarcity comes from how we organize society

Much of modern scarcity comes from choices about how our systems work. We distribute resources through artificial bottlenecks, speculative accumulation, and debt-driven consumption. We can instead organize them around universal access, distributed energy, and democratic stewardship.

Some enabling technologies already exist, while others remain developmental or conditional. The engineering and economic pathways require validation at scale. What remains disputed is both the technical path and the political distribution of power.

### A transition based on readiness

This is not an overnight revolution or a fantasy removed from everyday life. Chapter 24 is the Charter’s transition timeline. Its periods give communities a shared sequence to work from. Local readiness gates determine whether a community advances, and no place is required to move at the same speed. Reaching Year 30 does not guarantee an endpoint:

- **Foundation (Years 0–8):** Foundation building, enforceable minimum standards, and municipal pilots providing both UBR tiers, alongside sovereign infrastructure bonds and broad coalition organizing.
- **Expansion (Years 8–16):** Scaling automated production and making both UBR tiers universally guaranteed across participating countries and regions, subject to verified capacity and public review.
- **Global Floor (Years 16–30):** Expanding the guaranteed UBR floor globally, allowing democratically chosen local additions within ecological capacity, and strengthening multilateral wealth pacts and planetary-boundary resource accounting.
- **Maturity (Years 30+):** Review durable systems, remaining scarcity, ecological limits, and lawful revisions as evidence changes.

For each area of the transition, the Charter sets out how the institutions would work, measurable targets, and democratic fallback safeguards. The transition will be fiercely debated and challenged, and advancement remains subject to public evidence and the recorded GO, REVISE, PAUSE, or STOP/REPLACE gate in Chapter 24.

### Why This Matters Now

Automation is already changing the global economy.[^front-ilo-genai-work] Tens of millions of people in administrative, creative, technical, and service work are already seeing their work change or face significant exposure to change. Decisions made in this decade will determine whether the gains concentrate even more power in private monopolies or help free people from the constant anxiety of survival.

The time to decide how these systems should work is now. This Charter is a concrete starting point for that fight.

### Call to Action

This Charter is an invitation to help build the future. Whether you are a policymaker, technologist, activist, or concerned citizen, there is work you can do:

- **Educate yourself and others** about abundance possibilities
- **Organize in your community** to build local capacity
- **Advocate for policies** that distribute technological benefits
- **Participate in democratic processes** that shape our shared future
- **Design and build** systems that embody abundance values

No one can build this alone. The future will be shaped by what people are willing to create together.

### How to Read Proposal Status

The labels below help distinguish evidence, proposals, and possibilities. Ordinary prose without a label should not automatically be read as established fact; read its citations, caveats, and surrounding context.

| Status | Meaning in this Charter |
|--------|-------------------------|
| **Established evidence** | A sourced observation or finding supported by current evidence; its scope and uncertainty still matter. |
| **Plausible mechanism** | A reasoned causal or technical pathway that may work but is not demonstrated at the proposed scale. |
| **Draft policy option** | A concrete design offered for debate, testing, and democratic decision; it is not adopted law or a settled Charter commitment. |
| **Illustrative number** | An example rate, cost, threshold, quantity, or calculation used to make an idea concrete; it is not a forecast, target, or commitment unless separately adopted. |
| **Aspirational right** | A normative guarantee the Charter argues society should establish; it is not automatically available merely because it is proposed. |
| **Scenario** | One possible future or narrative used to explore implications; it is not a prediction. |
| **Target** | A measurable intended result with a baseline, scope, owner, review date, uncertainty, and revision or stop conditions; it is not a forecast or guarantee. |
| **Open question** | An issue intentionally unresolved and requiring evidence, debate, design, or public choice. |
| **Proposal** | A reader-facing umbrella for a system, policy, right, or institution offered for debate and lawful adoption; it is not yet adopted and does not replace the detailed categories above. |
| **Requirement** | A rule that becomes binding within its jurisdiction after lawful adoption; before adoption, it remains a proposed requirement. |
| **Planning Assumption** | A development the Charter expects and plans for while its timing, exact form, and demonstrated readiness remain open to evidence, revision, and review. |

Labels are selective. They appear where a reasonable reader could mistake a proposal, estimate, timeline, or narrative for an established commitment.

### Illustrative characters and scenarios

Named characters and family stories throughout this Charter are fictional illustrations, not empirical evidence. The recurring Chen household is Maria, an artist and VR creator; David, a civic mediator and human-systems facilitator; and their children, Leo and Sam, curious learners. Their geographic setting is intentionally unnamed. Dates, occupations, jurisdictions, access, and policy successes in these vignettes are possible scenario details, not predictions, guarantees, or roadmap commitments. In particular, 2047 is one possible future snapshot for the Chen family's local community, not a deadline for humanity or an automatic Chapter 24 phase outcome. Chapter 24's year ranges remain relative, readiness-gated, and variable by place.

### How the Charter Handles Alternatives

The Charter's shared commitments and outcomes are binding to its framework: human dignity and autonomy; universal access to essentials and enabling services; democratic accountability; ecological limits; and privacy, safety, equal rights, and protection against domination. It can preserve multiple implementation alternatives, but each alternative must be clearly labeled and evaluated through evidence, pilots, affected-community participation, transparent metrics, and democratic review, with power to revise, pause, reject, or replace it. Mutually incompatible options are alternatives, not simultaneous commitments. Local variation is welcome only when it does not fall below the shared rights and safeguards.
