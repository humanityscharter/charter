# Chapter 18: Growth, Sustainability & Limits

*How to reconcile abundance with planetary limits*

---

## Introduction: Abundance Still Has Physical Limits

Any credible economy has to respect a physical fact: **material extraction cannot grow exponentially forever inside a closed ecological system.**

For two centuries, industrial economies have often treated continued growth as the route to stable jobs, public revenue, and debt repayment. Some ecological economists argue that interest-bearing debt and other financial commitments add pressure for continued income growth; the strength of that pressure is debated. A need for enough money to service debt is not the same as a need to keep increasing physical use of materials and energy. Even if income grows, extraction, emissions, and waste still have to fit within ecological limits. Climate change, fisheries under pressure, soil degradation, and chemical pollution show why those limits matter.

Promises that digital efficiency will remove every physical constraint and erase the material cost of industrial growth ignore these limits. Policies that impose austerity, poverty, and forced degrowth put people's material security at risk.

Humanity's Charter rejects both approaches.

Abundance does not mean endless consumption or disposable luxury. It means guaranteeing material security within ecological limits. By replacing debt-driven growth with closed-loop manufacturing, physical energy and material accounting, and automated maintenance, society can eliminate material poverty while keeping resource use at sustainable and regenerative levels.

In this chapter, **planetary boundaries** and **ecological limits** mean evidence-informed constraints with uncertainty, regional variation, equity considerations, and formal revision rules. They are not a single immutable number. They bind all eight systems and guide allocation, accounting, and review.

Steady-state, degrowth, post-growth, green-growth, and related models are evaluated alternatives or conditional tools, not simultaneous mandates. Communities and governments may test compatible approaches against shared well-being outcomes, transparent resource metrics, affected-community participation, and democratic review. Ecological limits remain binding whichever model is chosen; no growth model can authorize rights violations or overshoot.

Ecological limits cannot authorize reproductive coercion. When demographic or service pressure appears, sustainability responses address systems: excessive consumption, distribution, efficiency, and sustainable capacity for energy, food, housing, healthcare, and infrastructure. Reproductive freedom remains a constitutional right, and demographic adaptation must use regional evidence, uncertainty, affected-community testimony, rights review, alternatives, public reasons, expiry, and reassessment rather than population targets or individual reproductive controls.

## Why Economies Face Pressure to Grow

### Why Growth Became a Policy Priority

Much of modern economic policy treats GDP growth as a measure of success. This wasn't always the primary focus. Several structural pressures helped make growth a priority:

**1. Debt-Based Money System (Transitional)**

Interest-bearing debt can put pressure on borrowers to earn enough income to make payments. Whether that makes aggregate economic growth unavoidable is debated; defaults, refinancing, changes in prices, and distribution all matter. This Charter proposes reducing harmful dependence on growth during the transition, while distinguishing income growth from growth in physical resource use.

**2. Employment Requirements**

More production can support more employment, but automation complicates this relationship. In a stagnant economy without growth, unemployment can rise and create social instability. Growth has been seen as necessary to maintain social order.

**3. Competitive Pressure**

Nations compete for growth. Companies compete for market share. This creates pressure to produce more, faster, and cheaper. Falling behind competitors can make it harder for a nation or company to choose a slower-growth path.

**4. Financial System Expectations**

Investors expect returns. Stock markets and retirement funds often plan around growth. These expectations put pressure on the financial system to keep expanding.

### The Physical Reality

But physical reality doesn't care about economic assumptions. The Earth has finite resources. Ecosystems have limits.

Several planetary boundaries have been crossed, alongside other resource pressures:[^ch18-planetary-boundaries]
- Continued greenhouse-gas emissions draw down the remaining carbon budget for any chosen temperature limit; the size of that budget depends on the limit and assumptions used.[^ch18-ipcc-budget]
- Biodiversity loss is a measured concern.[^ch18-planetary-boundaries] Species and ecosystems change at different rates.
- Rare-earth minerals and phosphorus raise different supply questions: extraction, processing, access, and recycling for the former; concentrated phosphate-rock reserves and nutrient losses for the latter. Neither is a single global countdown to depletion.[^ch18-usgs-minerals][^ch18-fao-soil]
- Freshwater stress varies by basin and season; some sources are shrinking while others are not.[^ch18-unwater-stress]
- Soil degradation can threaten food production where soils are eroding or losing fertility.[^ch18-fao-soil]

The growth model is running into physical limits. The relevant limits differ by Earth system and region, so this Charter treats them as a revisable evidence-and-governance framework rather than as one fixed threshold.

[^ch18-planetary-boundaries]: The 2023 update of the planetary-boundaries framework assessed all nine boundaries and concluded that six were transgressed. That finding supports the broad statement that human activity is exceeding multiple Earth-system limits; it does not independently verify every resource-specific bullet in this list: https://www.stockholmresilience.org/research/research-news/2023-09-13-all-planetary-boundaries-mapped-out-for-the-first-time-six-of-nine-crossed.html

[^ch18-ipcc-budget]: IPCC AR6 explains that limiting warming requires limiting cumulative CO2 emissions and gives remaining carbon budgets for specified warming levels and probabilities: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/

[^ch18-fao-soil]: FAO's account of its global soil assessment documents erosion, nutrient depletion, and other forms of soil degradation, with consequences for soil functions and food production: https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/Soils-are-endangered-but-the-degradation-can-be-rolled-back/en

[^ch18-usgs-minerals]: USGS's 2025 mineral summaries report limited recycling of rare earths and country-level phosphate-rock reserves; its Morocco country record estimates that Morocco held 68% of world phosphate-rock reserves in 2024. These are supply-chain and reserve-concentration measures, not depletion deadlines: https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/mcs2025 and https://www.usgs.gov/centers/national-minerals-information-center/morocco

[^ch18-unwater-stress]: UN-Water's 2024 water-stress update shows substantial regional and basin-level differences in water stress. UNESCO's 2024 water report describes dwindling and increasingly variable resources where over-extraction and land mismanagement occur; neither supports a claim that all freshwater sources are shrinking: https://www.unwater.org/publications/progress-level-water-stress-2024-update and https://www.unesco.org/reports/wwdr/en/2024/environment

## Alternative Economic Models

### Option 1: Steady-State Economics

**Core Idea:** Developed by ecological economist Herman Daly, this framework proposes that the economy maintains constant size, focusing on quality rather than quantity. Throughput (resource use) is stabilized at sustainable levels.

**Key Principles:**
- **Sustainable Scale** - Economy operates within planetary boundaries
- **Fair Distribution** - Resources distributed equitably
- **Efficient Allocation** - Resources used optimally

**How It Works:**
- GDP is not the primary metric
- Focus on well-being and sustainability indicators
- Resource caps and pollution limits enforced
- Circular economy (reuse, recycle, regenerate)

**Advantages:** Ecologically sustainable, reduces environmental impact, focuses on quality of life

**Challenges:** Requires fundamental system change away from debt-based money (transitional), faces political opposition

---

### Option 2: Degrowth

**Core Idea:** Deliberate reduction of production and consumption in wealthy nations to stay within planetary boundaries. Focus on sufficiency rather than maximization. Downscale to sustainable levels. Importantly, degrowth distinguishes between wealthy and developing nations: it calls for reduction in over-consuming wealthy nations while allowing developing nations to continue growing to meet basic needs for their populations.

**Key Principles:**
- **Reduce Throughput** - Less resource use, less waste
- **Reallocate Wealth** - Reduce inequality
- **Reorient Values** - From consumption to well-being
- **Care Economy** - Value unpaid care work

**How It Works:**
- Reduce GDP in wealthy nations
- Global equity (developing nations can still grow)
- Work sharing (reduce hours, share jobs)
- Universal basic services

**Advantages:** Addresses ecological overshoot directly, reduces inequality, improves well-being

**Challenges:** Politically difficult (contrary to growth ideology), economic disruption during transition, requires international coordination

---

### Option 3: Post-Growth

**Core Idea:** Move beyond growth as the organizing principle. Not necessarily reducing, but not growing for growth's sake. Focus on flourishing within planetary boundaries. Unlike degrowth, post-growth doesn't necessarily require economic contraction in all sectors.

**Key Principles:**
- **Plural Values** - Well-being, ecology, community, not just GDP
- **Sufficiency** - Enough for all, within limits
- **Regenerative** - Systems that restore rather than deplete
- **Distributed** - Local resilience, global coordination

**How It Works:**
- New metrics beyond GDP
- Circular economy
- Regenerative systems
- Democratic governance

**Advantages:** More flexible than degrowth, focuses on positive outcomes, compatible with technology

**Challenges:** Still requires system change, new metrics are hard to implement, faces political opposition

---

### Option 4: Green Growth

**Core Idea:** Green growth proposes continued GDP growth while decoupling it from environmental impact through technology and efficiency.

**Key Principles:**
- **Decoupling** - Economic growth without environmental damage
- **Renewable Energy** - Clean energy transition
- **Circular Economy** - Reduce waste, reuse materials
- **Green Technology** - Innovation for sustainability

**Advantages:** Politically palatable, doesn't require reducing consumption, uses technology

**Challenges:** Decoupling may not happen at the required scale; rebound effects can offset some efficiency gains, with the size varying by technology and context; continued growth in physical resource use has limits.

**The Evidence:** Evidence for absolute decoupling at the scale required remains limited. While some countries have achieved partial or absolute decoupling of emissions from GDP growth, global carbon emissions and material throughput continue rising. Most decoupling observed is relative rather than absolute, and the pace of decoupling is insufficient to meet climate targets.[^ch18-decoupling-review]

[^ch18-decoupling-review]: A systematic review of 835 peer-reviewed articles found that observed decoupling did not achieve absolute global reductions at a rate sufficient to meet environmental targets. A related review found examples of absolute GDP–CO2 decoupling in some countries and periods but no evidence of economy-wide resource decoupling at national or international scale. These findings describe the historical evidence reviewed; they do not prove that future absolute decoupling is impossible: https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ab842a and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7330600/

## Abundance Within Limits

### The Main Point

AI and robotics abundance can be compatible with sustainability IF we design it that way:

**1. Production Meets Needs, Not Unlimited Wants**

Abundance for essentials: food, housing, healthcare, education. Not infinite consumption of luxuries and superfluous goods.

**2. Circular Systems**

Design toward minimal waste and high-value reuse. Materials can be recovered and cycled where practical, but every process uses energy and some material is lost. Regenerative design aims to restore rather than deplete.

**3. Energy from Renewable Sources**

Renewables such as solar, wind, and geothermal can expand low-carbon capacity. Fusion is a separate possible pathway that may add capacity only if it achieves commercial and technical validation; the Charter sets no required schedule. Every pathway must be assessed using full lifecycle emissions, materials, land, maintenance, waste, safety, and ecological effects, so no source is assumed to have zero total impact or to provide unlimited clean energy.

**4. Dematerialization**

Digital over physical where possible. Sharing economy (less ownership, more access). Product longevity and repairability.

**5. Sufficiency Culture**

"Enough" is defined and respected. Quality over quantity. Well-being rather than consumption.

### Traditional vs. Sustainable Abundance

| Traditional Abundance | Sustainable Abundance |
|----------------------|----------------------|
| Infinite production | Production within limits |
| Consumerism (maximizing consumption) | Sufficiency (enough for all) |
| Linear economy | Circular economy |
| Fossil fuels | Renewable energy |
| Growth-focused | Well-being focused |
| Individual ownership | Shared access |
| Planned obsolescence | Durability and repair |

## Defining "Enough"

### The Challenge

In many consumer societies, more is presented as better. Status often comes through consumption. Identity can be tied to possessions. Satisfaction remains elusive.

But how do we define "enough" without creating scarcity? How do we prevent endless desire? How do we measure well-being rather than consumption?

### Three Tiers of "Enough"

**Tier 1: UBR Rights Floor (Universal)**

Chapter 6 defines the canonical scope. Core necessities are nutritious food and safe water (including sanitation where appropriate), safe adequate housing, comprehensive physical and mental healthcare, and education and lifelong learning. Universal enabling services are adequate basic energy, free transportation to essential services and community life, reliable connectivity and communications, an adequate computing device for each individual, necessary assistive technologies, and repair, maintenance, accessibility support, and reasonable replacement.

These guarantees are free at the point of use and establish a floor for survival and dignity. Their real energy, material, labor, maintenance, and ecological costs still require accounting.

**Tier 2: Beyond Basics (Individual Variation)**

Personal choices that vary by individual:
- Hobbies and interests
- Creative pursuits
- Travel and experiences
- Relationships and community
- Personal growth

These should be available by default while staying within planetary boundaries. If a choice uses a demonstrably scarce resource or has a high environmental impact, transparent rules may require trade-offs or limits, with equal rights, appeal and review, and periodic reassessment.

**Tier 3: Luxury (Limited)**

Scarce or high-impact resource use requiring trade-offs:
- Physical space (land, housing size)
- Energy-intensive goods
- Rare materials
- Carbon-intensive activities

These require conscious trade-offs and limits.

### Metrics Beyond GDP

| Metric | What It Measures | Why It Matters |
|--------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI)** | Economic + social + environmental | True well-being |
| **Happy Planet Index** | Well-being / environmental impact | Sustainable happiness |
| **Human Development Index** | Health, education, income | Human flourishing |
| **Planetary Boundaries** | Environmental limits | Ecological safety |
| **Well-being Indicators** | Life satisfaction, relationships | Quality of life |
| **Resource Footprint** | Material/energy use | Sustainability |

## AI and Robotics: Opportunity and Risk

### The Opportunity

With the right implementation and policy frameworks, AI and robotics could enable sustainability:

**1. Efficiency**
- Improve resource use
- Reduce waste through better systems
- Precision agriculture (with caveats about industrial farming impacts)
- Smart energy grids

**Note:** These benefits require supportive policies and systemic changes to realize.

**2. Circular Economy**
- Automated recycling
- Material tracking
- Product lifecycle management
- Design for disassembly

**3. Renewable Energy**
- Improved solar and wind systems
- Smart grids
- Energy storage optimization
- Fusion development assistance

**4. Regenerative Systems**
- Reforestation robots
- Ocean cleanup
- Soil restoration
- Biodiversity monitoring

**5. Dematerialization**
- Virtual over physical where possible
- Digital services
- Remote work and education
- 3D printing for local production

### The Risk

AI and robotics could also worsen sustainability:

**1. Rebound Effect (Jevons Paradox)**
- Some efficiency gains can lower costs and increase use
- Cheaper production can increase waste when total demand rises
- Rebound effects can offset part of an efficiency gain; they do not always erase it
- **Mitigation:** Policy interventions such as caps, taxes, and regulations can counteract rebound effects and help efficiency gains translate to actual reductions
- **Circular economy caveat:** Reuse and recycling can reduce throughput per unit of output, but rising total demand can offset some gains. The size of that rebound has to be measured, not assumed.

**2. Resource Intensive**
- AI requires massive compute and energy
- Many robotics applications currently rely on rare earth metals. Alternatives are being developed.
- Data centers consume enormous electricity

**3. Disposable Culture**
- Easy production leads to disposable goods
- Planned obsolescence
- Waste accumulation

**4. Global Inequality**
- Wealthy nations consume more
- Environmental damage exported to poorer nations
- Climate injustice

### Designing AI and Robotics for Sustainability

**1. Efficiency with Caps**
- Aim to keep efficiency gains from increasing total consumption
- Enforce resource caps
- Use consumption limits to prevent rebound

**2. Circular by Design**
- All products designed for recycling
- Material tracking and recovery
- Design for minimal waste while measuring unavoidable material losses and energy use

**3. Renewable Energy**
- Power AI and robotics with clean energy
- Prioritize energy efficiency
- Aim for carbon-neutral operations

**4. Dematerialization**
- Digital solutions where possible
- Shared resources
- Reduced physical production

**5. Regenerative Impact**
- Use AI and robotics to restore the environment
- Carbon drawdown
- Biodiversity protection

## Economic Systems for Sustainability

### 1. Circular Economy

**Principles:**
- Design out waste and pollution
- Keep products and materials in use
- Regenerate natural systems

**Implementation:**
- Product-as-service (rental access during the monetary transition)
- Design for durability and repair
- Closed-loop recycling
- Industrial symbiosis (waste becomes input)

Guaranteed essentials remain free at the point of use throughout the transition and the mature moneyless phase. In the mature phase, direct provision, borrowing, and reservations provide access under Chapter 6's rules. Scarce or high-impact resource use follows transparent rules with equal rights, appeals, review, and periodic reassessment.

**Examples:**
- Car sharing instead of ownership
- Tool libraries and specialized equipment sharing
- Modular electronics
- Compostable packaging

---

### 2. Doughnut Economics

**Concept:** Developed by economist Kate Raworth, this framework proposes:
- **Inner ring:** Social foundation (minimum standards for all)
- **Outer ring:** Ecological ceiling (planetary boundaries)
- **Doughnut:** Safe space for human flourishing

**Implementation:**
- Guarantee basics for all
- Limit environmental impact
- Measure progress within the doughnut

**Advantages:** Clear visual model, balances social and ecological concerns, actionable framework

---

### 3. Well-being Economy

**Principles:**
- Economy serves people and planet
- Well-being over GDP
- Regenerative and distributive by design

**Implementation:**
- Well-being budgets
- Purpose-driven businesses
- Community ownership
- Ecological accounting

**Precedents:**
- New Zealand Well-being Budget (2019) - Prioritized mental health, child poverty reduction, and indigenous wellbeing in budget allocations[^ch18-new-zealand-wellbeing-budget]
- Scotland's Wellbeing Economy work - An institutional example of putting wellbeing indicators into policy discussion; outcome claims need a separate country-level review
- Wales Well-being of Future Generations Act (2015) - A legal example requiring public bodies to pursue sustainable development goals; claims about improved outcomes need a separate review

[^ch18-new-zealand-wellbeing-budget]: New Zealand Treasury's 2019 Wellbeing Budget identifies mental health, child wellbeing, and Māori and Pacific aspirations among its five priorities. The source supports the 2019 budget and those priorities; it does not by itself establish that the approach has continued unchanged through the present: https://www.treasury.govt.nz/publications/wellbeing-budget/wellbeing-budget-2019

---

### 4. Ecological Economics

**Core Ideas:**
- Economy is a subset of the ecosystem, not separate from it
- Physical limits apply to the whole system. Replacing one material with another can reduce scarcity, and the replacement still has energy, material, and ecological costs.
- Scale matters, not just allocation
- Key figures: Herman Daly (steady-state economics), Robert Costanza (ecosystem services valuation)

**Tools:**
- Resource accounting
- Ecological footprint
- Steady-state targets
- Degrowth where necessary

## Transition Strategies

The sustainability work described here follows Chapter 24's Foundation (Years 0–8), Expansion (Years 8–16), Global Floor (Years 16–30), and Maturity (Years 30+) periods. Early build-out starts in Foundation and continues into Expansion. Circular-economy, renewable-energy, resource, biodiversity, and regenerative work continues through Expansion and Global Floor. Emissions dates and percentages remain proposed targets or scenarios; ecological safety and public review guide each decision. Ecological safety and public review can delay, revise, or stop a workstream.

### Foundation (Years 0–8)

**Actions:**
- Carbon pricing and caps
- Renewable energy transition
- Circular economy pilots
- Well-being metrics adoption
- AI and robotics for sustainability

**Goals:**
- Peak global emissions by 2027-2032
- Reduce waste by 20-30%
- Build renewable energy and circular infrastructure
- 50%+ of major economies adopting well-being metrics

### Expansion (Years 8–16)

Continue the early build-out actions and goals begun during Foundation.

**Actions:**
- Full circular economy implementation
- Renewable energy dominance
- Managed reduction of resource throughput in wealthy nations
- Sustainable AI and robotics
- Regenerative systems

**Goals:**
- Net zero emissions by 2050
- Resource use within planetary boundaries
- Biodiversity recovery (species loss reversed)
- 70-80% reduction in material throughput vs. business-as-usual

### Global Floor (Years 16–30)

Continue the circular-economy, renewable-energy, resource, biodiversity, and regenerative work described above, with its existing targets and ecological review.

### Maturity (Years 30+)

**Intended outcomes:**
- An economic model chosen through evidence and democratic review, with steady-state among the options
- Abundance within limits
- Regenerative systems
- Sustainable flourishing

**Goals:**
- Maintain ecological balance within planetary boundaries
- Continuous improvement in well-being metrics
- Intergenerational equity secured

## The Abundance Within Limits Framework

**Planetary boundaries:** Carbon, Biodiversity, Water, etc.

**Sustainable abundance within these boundaries:**

**UBR summary: both tiers**

Canonical floor; not exhaustive.

- Food, housing, healthcare
- Education, transit, energy
- Connectivity + technology

**Personal choices: Freedom first**

- Autonomy is the default
- Limits only for scarce or high-impact resource use
- Transparent rules, equal rights, appeals, and review
- Reassessed periodically

**Luxury: Scarce, requires trade-offs**

- Large physical space
- Energy-intensive goods
- Rare materials

### Key Principles

1. **Basics Guaranteed** - Universal access to essentials
2. **Choices Available** - Personal autonomy is the default; limits apply only to demonstrably scarce or high-impact resource use, with transparent rules, equal rights, appeals, review, and periodic reassessment.
3. **Luxury Limited** - Scarce or high-impact resource use requires trade-offs
4. **Circular Systems** - Minimal waste and high-value reuse, with losses and energy needs counted
5. **Regenerative** - Systems that restore, not deplete
6. **Sufficiency Culture** - "Enough" is celebrated, not more

## The Chen Family Example

In this illustrative scenario, the Chen family lives the principles of sustainable abundance:

**Housing:** They live in a dense, walkable community with shared resources. Their home is energy-efficient, powered by community solar. They own less but have access to more through sharing networks.

**Food:** Community gardens and automated vertical farms provide abundant nutritious food. Food waste is composted and returned to the soil. Their diet is primarily plant-based, with animal products from regenerative sources.

**Transportation:** They don't own cars. Instead, they use shared autonomous vehicles and excellent public transit. For longer trips, high-speed rail connects cities.

**Consumption:** They prioritize quality over quantity. Items are durable, repairable, and recyclable. During the monetary transition, when they need something, they often rent or borrow rather than buy. In the mature moneyless phase, they get goods through direct provision, borrowing, and reservations, with fair allocation when an item is scarce. Digital alternatives replace many physical goods.

**Leisure:** Their satisfaction comes from relationships, creative work, learning, and community involvement, not from acquiring things. They have abundant time for what matters.

**The Chens** learned about planetary boundaries through their self-directed learning and community projects. They understand that abundance doesn't mean infinite consumption. They're excited about their role in regenerative projects: restoring ecosystems, monitoring biodiversity, contributing to the circular economy.

The Chen family is wealthy in time, community, and purpose. They're not wealthy in stuff. And they're happier for it.

## Addressing Common Concerns

### "Won't this hurt economic development?"

**Answer:** Development is not the same as growth. Development refers to improvements in quality of life, health, education, and capabilities, while growth refers to increases in material/energy throughput. You can develop quality of life without increasing throughput by improving efficiency and access. Examples include: expanding internet access and education (which have low material footprints), improving healthcare delivery, and developing renewable energy infrastructure that allows developing nations to leapfrog fossil fuels. Developing nations can grow sustainably by adopting clean technologies from the start. Wealthy nations should stabilize or reduce consumption while maintaining development gains.

### "Won't people be unhappy without more consumption?"

**Answer:** Once basic needs are met, this Charter proposes putting more emphasis on relationships, purpose, health, and control over time. Additional consumption can provide satisfaction. Nordic countries and Bhutan's Gross National Happiness offer examples for exploring a culture centered on well-being. The goal is deeper and more sustained satisfaction through abundant time, community, and creativity.

### "How do we handle debt-based money during transition?"

**Answer:** Interest-bearing debt can add pressure for enough income to repay it, but whether it forces an entire economy to grow is debated. Reform proposals include sovereign money systems (where money is created debt-free by public institutions), positive money systems, and full reserve banking. Advocates argue these could reduce some pressure for income growth; none would by itself guarantee lower material or energy use. Monetary reform is complex, requires major political will, and would need to be coordinated with other economic changes. This remains an area of active debate and experimentation.

### "Won't this require global coordination?"

**Answer:** Yes, absolutely. Climate change and ecological collapse are global problems requiring international treaties. Under Chapter 5's model, states enter voluntarily through domestic ratification; once they join a specific treaty, its narrowly enumerated climate and cross-border-pollution obligations may be binding. Biodiversity and any other subject outside the currently enumerated scope remain advisory unless members formally amend or replace the relevant treaty, satisfy the required chamber supermajorities, and complete renewed domestic ratification wherever scope or sovereignty changes. Ordinary treaty drafting cannot bypass that scope-change gate. Any consequences require transparent findings, an independent tribunal, proportionality, appeals, review, and protection for ordinary residents and essential services; no general world government is created.

### "What about innovation?"

**Answer:** Innovation continues without growth. Focus shifts to quality, efficiency, and sustainability. Open source collaboration and public good orientation drive progress. Many of humanity's greatest innovations weren't profit-driven.

## Sustainable Abundance and the Path Forward

### What Growth Looks Like Within Limits

**"What about growth? Infinite growth on a finite planet?"**

**Answer:** Material extraction cannot grow exponentially forever within a finite ecological system. The goal is universal access to essentials and better quality of life within ecological limits. The approaches below can help meet that goal:

1. **Economic Models Tested Against Shared Goals**
   - Steady-state, post-growth, and other models remain options to test through evidence and democratic review
   - Not growing for growth's sake
   - Focus on well-being within limits
   - Production can expand where resources and ecological limits allow

2. **Circular, Regenerative Systems**
   - Design toward minimal waste
   - Recover and cycle materials where practical, while counting losses and energy needs
   - Systems that restore

3. **Abundance Within Planetary Boundaries**
   - Guaranteed basics for all
   - Personal autonomy by default; limits for scarce or high-impact use
   - Luxury requires trade-offs

4. **Sustainable AI and Robotics**
   - Efficiency with caps
   - Powered by renewables
   - Designed for circularity

5. **New Metrics**
   - Beyond GDP
   - Well-being, sustainability, equity
   - Measure what matters
