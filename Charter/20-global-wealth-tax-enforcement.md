# Chapter 20: Global Wealth Tax and Enforcement

*Ensuring Compliance Through International Coordination*

---

## Introduction: Wealth Can Move Across Borders

The central problem for public finance is simple: **capital can move; a country cannot.**

For decades, national wealth-tax proposals have faced real questions about valuation, avoidance, administration, and the ability of wealthy people to move assets or residence. France, Sweden, and Germany had different tax designs and histories; they cannot be treated as one experiment that proves a single result. Some people moved assets or residence, but the size of those effects is disputed and not enough here to conclude that every national wealth tax failed.[^ch20-oecd-wealth-taxes]

[^ch20-oecd-wealth-taxes]: OECD, *The Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes in the OECD* (2018), reviews historical taxes, administrative and mobility risks, and how design and the wider tax system affect outcomes. It does not establish that every unilateral tax fails: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_9789264290303-en.html

These difficulties have led some economists to question net wealth taxes, while others argue that design and enforcement can improve them. Tax competition can put pressure on governments to lower rates or shift burdens, but those outcomes are not inevitable.

Humanity's Charter rejects the idea that those difficulties make fair taxation impossible.

This chapter considers two approaches to taxing extreme wealth during the monetary transition:
1. **Multilateral Transparency:** Public, cryptographically auditable beneficial-ownership and control information for corporations, trusts, major public contractors, and public funds, with privacy-preserving limits on everything else.
2. **Physical Point Levies:** During the monetary transition, one option would tax covered private commercial gains or exclusive scarce-resource use linked to physical bottlenecks, such as datacenter power connections, orbital satellite bands, automated mineral extraction sites, and land value. A physical location or scarce use could make some forms of paper relocation harder. Ownership and accounting arrangements could still create avoidance routes.

Chapter 6 governs the base, exclusions, lawful adoption, review, and retirement of any levy. Each option needs an assessment of legality, costs, fairness, avoidance, and ecological effects. Overlapping taxes, fees, and public-ownership returns follow Chapter 6's accounting rules; revenue is counted only once.

Guaranteed essentials, including adequate energy and computing access, remain free at the point of use. Public AI and robotics infrastructure remains publicly owned and operated under Chapter 4. Datacenter power connections and energy inputs could help measure covered private commercial activity permitted under that chapter. Any tax would apply to its separately defined commercial-gain or exclusive resource-use base. Energy and compute accounting continues to support public planning and allocation; scarce public compute above the baseline uses published priorities, queues, or lotteries.

These transition levies may shrink or retire as public provision matures. Once money is no longer required, monetary levies end. Scarce-resource access then follows Chapter 6's stewardship, ecological, demonstrated-need, rotation, queue, and lottery rules. Evidence-based readiness gates govern the change.

## Rights and Due-Process Limits on Financial Enforcement

This chapter applies the **Chapter 8 Rights and Due-Process Matrix** as its canonical rights floor. Financial transparency, tax administration, travel limits, asset freezes, and any delegated enforcement must follow that matrix's notice, reasons, evidence access, representation, independent judicial review, appeal and presumptive stay, automatic expiry, full remedy, and lawful-dissent protections. Tax proposals remain proposals until lawful adoption; a treaty or contractor label cannot create general global jurisdiction or bypass domestic rights review.

### Tiered financial transparency

Public beneficial-ownership and control information may cover corporations, trusts, major public contractors, and public funds. Ordinary personal balances and transactions remain private and data-minimized absent specific legal authorization. People may inspect and correct records, and privacy-preserving proofs may establish a required fact without exposing unrelated personal data. A registry cannot become a general ledger of ordinary residents' finances.

Chapter 9's privacy architecture applies to these records: minimize collection; bind use and retention to the stated lawful tax purpose; restrict access to legally authorized users and log it; require independent audit; and allow correction or deletion where lawful, subject to any legally required record-retention duties.

### Particularized asset freezes

An asset freeze requires a court order and clear and convincing evidence of a nexus between specified assets and a specified unlawful act or enforceable judgment. The order must limit the amount and property covered and preserve housing, food, healthcare, counsel, caregiving, and ordinary living expenses. Every freeze expires automatically; renewal requires current proof and a renewed order. When imminent dissipation creates a specific serious risk, temporary action may precede ordinary notice only with safe notice within 24 hours and judicial review within 48 hours. A missed review ends the freeze, and any later action needs fresh authorization rather than retroactive ratification.

### Narrow travel restrictions and remedies

Travel limits require an individualized court order supported by specific, current evidence of imminent violence, unlawful flight, or a defined quarantine danger. Geography and duration must be narrow; counsel, humanitarian, and medical exceptions remain available. The order expires automatically and may be renewed only on current evidence under the matrix. Full remedies restore movement, status, property, services, and records, notify downstream recipients, reimburse losses and legal costs, compensate material and dignitary harm, and support systemic correction where warranted.

The mechanisms below are bounded-pluralist alternatives for public debate. They are not simultaneous commitments: each requires its own lawful authority, privacy and distributional review, public-status label, and Chapter 24 readiness gate.

### Fiscal portfolio boundary and status rule

Chapter 6 controls whether wealth, land and resource, consumption, public-ownership, financial-transaction, and related mechanisms are complementary, substitutive, phase-specific, or locally selected alternatives. This chapter supplies enforcement detail, legal analysis, valuation issues, objections, and due-process safeguards; it does not create a rival fiscal crosswalk. A mechanism may be used only within a lawful domestic or ratified treaty authority, with notice, evidence access, independent review, appeal and stay, proportionality, expiry, and remedy.

Every rate, threshold, collection fraction, revenue total, cost total, and timetable in this chapter is illustrative, historical, or scenario material unless the text identifies a complete formal-adoption record with the adopting authority, jurisdiction, date, effective period, covered base, evidence, review, expiry, and verifiable source. No number becomes adopted by repetition, and no estimate proves that a mechanism is equitable, lawful, or sustainable. Transition financial mechanisms retire, reverse, or are replaced when their underlying markets or purposes disappear or when a readiness review finds them unnecessary or harmful.

## Why Unilateral Wealth Taxes Face Risks

### Capital Flight

When a country imposes a wealth tax, some wealthy people may be able to change residence, move assets, use legal structures that obscure ownership, or renounce citizenship. Estimates of offshore wealth vary considerably; the EU Tax Observatory cited an estimate of approximately 8% of global household financial wealth held in tax havens (roughly $5.6 trillion in 2007), representing approximately 10% of global GDP. Those are historical, differently defined measures, not a current taxable base. Greater resources can make cross-border planning easier, but the response to any particular tax depends on its design and enforcement.

Unilateral taxes can face capital-mobility and avoidance risks, but the evidence does not show that every country loses investment or that only people unable to leave remain.[^ch20-oecd-wealth-taxes]

---

### Legal Challenges

A wealth-tax proposal needs legal review of constitutional protections for property rights, applicable double-taxation treaties, international-law constraints, and due process requirements. Wealth-tax rules can face court challenges over how different types of assets are valued. In 1995, Germany's Federal Constitutional Court found that applying the same wealth-tax rate to outdated property valuations and current valuations of other assets violated the constitutional requirement of equal treatment.[^ch20-wealth-tax-1995] Legal battles drain resources and create uncertainty.

[^ch20-wealth-tax-1995]: Deutscher Bundestag, Wissenschaftliche Dienste, WD 4 - 3000 - 001/26 (27 February 2026), § 9.3, printed p. 20, especially footnotes 62–63, [official research summary](https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/1158282/WD-4-001-26.pdf); Deutscher Bundestag, Drucksache 13/4839 (11 June 1996), explanatory memorandum, Part II, Article 1, printed p. 43, [official bill document](https://dserver.bundestag.de/btd/13/048/1304839.pdf). The first source summarizes the court decision; the second is a bill's explanatory text that identifies § 10 no. 1 VStG and Article 3(1) GG. Neither link is presented as the full judgment.

---

### Enforcement Costs

Tracking assets across borders can be expensive and complex. It may require specialist staff, legal work, and cooperation with other tax authorities. Whether administration costs more than revenue depends on the tax and its design; the Charter has not established that comparison. International information sharing can help countries track offshore holdings. Domestic enforcement can also achieve some results on its own.

---

### Economic Distortion

Wealth taxes may affect investment choices, entrepreneurship, migration, and economic activity. The size and direction of those effects depend on rates, thresholds, valuation, other taxes, and how the revenue is used. Poor design can cause harm; it is not evidence that every wealth tax does.

---

## The Solution: Multi-Layered Approach

Capital mobility, legal barriers, enforcement costs, and economic distortion can weaken a unilateral wealth tax. The limited evidence does not show that failure is inevitable.[^ch20-oecd-wealth-taxes] This proposal presents bounded layers for comparison, not a requirement to adopt every layer. Each option addresses a different weakness, but every option needs its own legal, privacy, distributional, and cost review under Chapter 6's portfolio rules.

## Layer 1: Global Coordination

This chapter is a proposal for treaty-based cooperation, not a claim of automatic global authority. Under Chapter 5, states may voluntarily enter a treaty through valid domestic ratification. Only members of a relevant treaty are bound, and only for its enumerated subject: cross-border financial secrecy and tax evasion, or governance of a voluntarily pooled fund or shared resource. Domestic tax design remains national; a treaty may coordinate only the cross-border aspects of financial secrecy and tax evasion that its members expressly accept. New subject matter requires formal amendment and renewed ratification.

Any cross-border consequence must follow the treaty's double-majority decision process and the independent-review safeguards in Chapter 5: notice and transparent finding; assistance and a corrective plan; monitored compliance; then, if necessary, a proportionate consequence authorized after an independent treaty tribunal finding. Evidence access, representation, appeal, public reasons, periodic review, sunset or reassessment, remedies for wrongful enforcement, and protections for ordinary residents and essential services are required. Withdrawal with advance notice is available, but cannot erase adjudicated violations or time-limited obligations already accepted.

### Why Coordination Is Necessary

As long as jurisdictions offer different tax treatment and secrecy, people may be able to move assets or residence. A tax in one country can face avoidance and mobility risks, but it does not automatically fail.[^ch20-oecd-wealth-taxes] Coordination among major economies could reduce some gaps. Some havens may remain.

---

### A. Global Minimum Wealth Tax

Similar to the OECD's Global Minimum Tax of 15% for corporations, a global minimum wealth tax would establish baseline standards.

> **Draft policy option; illustrative numbers:** The 2% rate, $10 million threshold, and top-0.1% estimate below are a concrete option for debate. They are not adopted law, a forecast, or evidence that this design would produce a particular revenue or distributional result.

| Component | Specification |
|-----------|---------------|
| Minimum Rate | 2% annual tax on net wealth exceeding $10 million |
| Threshold | Affects only top 0.1% of wealth holders |
| Coordination | G20 (Group of Twenty major economies)/OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development)-led international agreement |
| Enforcement | Participating jurisdictions use domestic rules to apply a top-up tax under the agreed framework; implementation differs by jurisdiction |

Under OECD Pillar Two, the model rules aim for large multinational groups to pay at least a 15% effective tax rate on covered profits in each jurisdiction, with top-up mechanisms where required. As of June 9, 2023, 139 jurisdictions in the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework had joined the wider two-pillar agreement.[^ch20-oecd-pillar-two] That is a precedent for negotiation on corporate taxation, not proof that every participant had implemented the rules or that a global wealth tax would work the same way. Challenges remain:

- Requires buy-in from major economies (US, China, EU)
- Nonparticipating jurisdictions cannot be compelled by an open-ended authority; members may consider only enumerated, ratified, independently reviewed, proportionate measures for covered institutions, with resident and essential-service protections
- Enforcement mechanisms needed to ensure compliance

### B. Global Asset Registry

A proposed public registry of beneficial ownership and control for corporations, trusts, major public contractors, and public funds could make some forms of hidden control harder to sustain. It would not capture every asset or make concealment impossible, and it must not expose ordinary personal balances or transactions absent specific legal authorization.

| Asset Type | Registration Requirements |
|------------|--------------------------|
| **Covered entities** | Beneficial ownership and control for corporations, trusts, major public contractors, and public funds |
| **Public funds** | Control and stewardship interests, with audit trail and lawful access controls |
| **Ordinary personal finances** | Private and data-minimized; access only under specific legal authorization, with inspection and correction rights |
| **Digital and other assets** | Report only where they fall within a covered entity or a particularized lawful proceeding |

This bounded proposal would require reporting by covered entities and lawful information sharing between tax authorities, with penalties proportionate to proven non-compliance. Ordinary personal balances and transactions are not a default registry category. Under the existing CRS (Common Reporting Standard), 111 jurisdictions had commenced automatic exchange of financial account information by 2024.[^ch20-oecd-crs-2024] FATCA in the United States requires covered foreign institutions to report specified U.S. accounts or face withholding on specified payments. Neither system is a complete global asset registry. Challenges include privacy concerns, opposition from some countries, and technical implementation complexity.

[^ch20-oecd-pillar-two]: OECD's two-pillar agreement page records 139 member jurisdictions as of June 9, 2023. Pillar Two concerns minimum taxation of large multinational enterprises, not individual net wealth: https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/announcements/2021/10/statement-on-a-two-pillar-solution-to-address-the-tax-challenges-arising-from-the-digitalisation-of-the-economy-october-2021.html

[^ch20-oecd-crs-2024]: OECD Global Forum reported that 111 jurisdictions had commenced automatic exchanges of financial-account information in 2024. This is a dated measure of exchange participation, not a count of jurisdictions exchanging every possible asset record: https://www.oecd.org/en/networks/global-forum-tax-transparency/news-events/2024/global-forum-celebrates-15-years-of-progress-and-extends-tax-transparency-to-the-crypto-asset-sector.html

- Privacy concerns must be addressed
- Some countries (tax havens) oppose participation
- Technical implementation is complex

### C. Treaty-authorized responses to secrecy

Reduce secrecy incentives through coordinated, treaty-authorized measures; no member or resident is automatically cut off from the global financial system.

| Mechanism | How It Works |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Financial Sanctions** | A ratified treaty may authorize proportionate restrictions on covered institutions after independent tribunal review, while preserving essential payments |
| **Trade Barriers** | A ratified treaty may authorize proportionate, reviewable consequences for covered non-cooperation; ordinary residents and essential services must be protected |
| **Travel Restrictions** | Only an individualized court order based on specific current evidence of imminent violence, unlawful flight, or defined quarantine danger; geography and duration are narrow, with automatic expiry, counsel, humanitarian/medical exceptions, appeal, and full remedy under Chapter 8. |
| **Asset Freezing** | Only a court order for specified assets supported by clear and convincing evidence of a nexus to a specified unlawful act or enforceable judgment; protect housing, food, healthcare, counsel, caregiving, and ordinary expenses, with automatic expiry, proof-based renewal, appeal, and full remedy under Chapter 8. |

Precedents include the EU list of non-cooperative jurisdictions and FATF (Financial Action Task Force) grey and black lists. Under this Charter, those examples do not themselves create authority: any binding measure would require treaty consent, the Chapter 5 decision thresholds, an independent tribunal finding, proportionality, and resident protections. Members are not forced into open-ended isolation.

### D. Citizenship-Based Taxation

Like the U.S. system in one respect, this proposal would let participating countries tax covered citizens on defined income or assets even when they live abroad. Moving could then be less effective as an avoidance strategy, but collection would still depend on domestic law, treaties, and information sharing. A proposed exit rule on renunciation would need separate legislation and due-process protections; it is not automatically triggered in every country or proof that hidden assets would be found. The United States generally taxes citizens abroad on worldwide income, subject to credits and exclusions,[^ch20-us-worldwide-taxation] while any comparison with Eritrea needs a country-specific legal review. Challenges include dual citizenship, privacy, nonparticipating countries, and administrative cost.

[^ch20-us-worldwide-taxation]: The IRS states that U.S. citizens and resident aliens abroad are generally subject to U.S. tax on worldwide income, with exclusions and credits available under specified rules. This supports only the existing U.S. precedent, not the proposed global system or the Eritrea comparison: https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/us-citizens-and-resident-aliens-abroad

- Broad cross-border coverage depends on cooperation from other countries.
- Dual citizenship can complicate tax rules and administration.
- Enforcement requires international cooperation

## Layer 2: National Enforcement

### A. Exit Taxes

An exit tax can apply when a person leaves or renounces citizenship, but existing laws differ. In the United States, section 877A generally treats most property of a covered expatriate as sold at fair market value on the day before expatriation, subject to thresholds and exceptions.[^ch20-us-expatriation-tax] That is not the Charter's proposed rule.

**Charter proposal for debate:** A participating country could consider liability for a defined number of years after departure or asset transfer, with rules for significant assets. Passport or visa restrictions, asset freezes, and treaty-based collection are separate proposals, not features established by the U.S. exit-tax precedent. Each would need a legal basis, due-process protections, proportionality review, and agreement with other countries. Other countries' exit-tax systems must be reviewed individually before being treated as comparable precedents. Challenges include advance planning, administrative cost, and legal disputes.

[^ch20-us-expatriation-tax]: IRS guidance explains that Internal Revenue Code section 877A generally applies a mark-to-market regime to covered expatriates, treating most property as sold on the day before expatriation, subject to thresholds and exceptions. It does not support the chapter's proposed “X years,” passport, asset-freeze, or treaty mechanisms, or verify every international comparison: https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/expatriation-tax

- Requires international cooperation
- Wealthy may plan ahead (renounce early)
- Legal challenges possible

---

### B. Beneficial Ownership Transparency

Revealing who actually controls assets behind legal structures is one possible enforcement tool. This Charter proposes verified ownership reporting for covered companies, trusts, and foundations, appropriate access for tax authorities, penalties for concealment, cross-border cooperation, and whistleblower protections. Existing systems are narrower. The U.S. Corporate Transparency Act began beneficial-ownership reporting in 2024, but FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule exempted entities created in the United States and U.S. persons from its reporting requirement; covered foreign entities registered to do business in the United States remain within the revised scope.[^ch20-fincen-2025] EU beneficial-ownership arrangements and the UK's People with Significant Control register are other examples requiring separate legal review. Challenges include complex structures, privacy, and uneven enforcement.

[^ch20-fincen-2025]: FinCEN's current interim-final-rule FAQ states that domestic U.S. entities and their beneficial owners are exempt from initial and updated BOI reports, while the revised reporting-company definition addresses certain foreign entities registered to do business in the United States: https://www.fincen.gov/boi/ifr-qa

- Complex ownership structures designed to obscure control
- Privacy concerns
- Enforcement varies significantly by country

---

### C. Digital Asset Tracking

Monitoring covered cryptocurrency and digital-asset transactions could make some forms of hidden ownership harder to sustain. This Charter proposes reporting by covered service providers, legally authorized analysis, and cross-border information sharing; universal wallet registration and know-your-customer (KYC) identity checks for every peer-to-peer transaction are proposals, not current global rules. Existing standards include the FATF Travel Rule for covered transfers and the OECD/G20 Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF), which provides a model for tax-information reporting and exchange.[^ch20-oecd-carf] Decentralized finance, privacy-protecting assets, jurisdictional gaps, and technical capacity remain challenges.

[^ch20-oecd-carf]: OECD states that it developed CARF with G20 countries as a global tax-transparency framework for standardized reporting and exchange of crypto-asset transaction information. CARF is not an EU-only framework and must be implemented in participating jurisdictions: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/international-standards-for-automatic-exchange-of-information-in-tax-matters_896d79d1-en.html

- Decentralized finance (DeFi, peer-to-peer financial systems on blockchain) harder to track
- Privacy coins (Monero, Zcash) present difficulties
- Technical expertise required for enforcement

---

### D. Withholding Taxes

Withholding collects covered tax at a defined payment point before money reaches a recipient. This Charter proposes extending appropriate reporting and withholding rules to specified payments and institutions, with penalties and treaty cooperation where agreed. FATCA provides a narrower precedent: generally 30% withholding on specified withholdable payments to nonparticipating foreign financial institutions, subject to exceptions.[^ch20-fatca-withholding] Collecting at source can make some evasion harder and shift some administrative work to institutions. It does not make enforcement automatic or cover every transfer, dividend, interest payment, or capital gain.

[^ch20-fatca-withholding]: IRS instructions state that FATCA generally imposes 30% withholding on specified withholdable payments to foreign financial institutions unless they participate or qualify for an exception, and requires reporting of specified U.S. accounts. That is narrower than 30% on every transaction by any “non-compliant foreign bank”: https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8966

- Tax is collected before a covered payment reaches its recipient, which can make some evasion harder.
- Some administrative work shifts to covered institutions.
- Collection at covered payment points remains subject to audit and enforcement.

## Layer 3: Alternative Approaches

Layer 3 is an alternatives menu, not an additive revenue portfolio. Chapter 6 decides whether a land/resource, consumption, public-ownership, or financial-transaction mechanism complements, substitutes for, or is limited to a particular transition phase alongside wealth enforcement. Local jurisdictions may select a lawful alternative suited to their conditions, but they must disclose the covered base, exemptions, interaction credits, administrative costs, and retirement condition. No projected revenue may be added to another mechanism's projection when the bases overlap.

### A. Land and Resource Taxes

Land and natural-resource sites cannot be moved offshore. That makes land value and resource-use fees possible tax bases even when owners change residence. This Charter proposes considering a Land Value Tax (LVT) and resource-use fees, with locally accountable valuation and collection. Physical immobility does not make assessment easy or collection automatic, and these taxes would not reach financial assets or intellectual property. The effect on different households and communities would depend on design; political opposition is another challenge.

- Doesn't capture all wealth (financial assets, intellectual property)
- Requires accurate valuation
- Political opposition from property owners

---

### B. Consumption Taxes

During the monetary transition, a limited consumption tax may be considered as a fallback for non-essential consumption. Guaranteed essentials remain exempt and free at the point of use; any burden on lower-income people requires offsets and distributional review. A VAT, luxury tax, or progressive rate is an illustrative local option, not a mandate to tax all consumption. Such mechanisms may reduce or retire as direct provision replaces ordinary money-based consumption. Challenges include regressivity without offsets, reduced economic activity, and discouraged spending.

- Regressive without offsets (like UBI, Universal Basic Income)
- Can reduce economic activity
- May discourage spending

---

### C. Sovereign Wealth Fund Ownership

Public ownership is the principal long-term substitute for tax-dependent essential provision. While money remains in use, a public fund could acquire equity stakes in major companies and assets and use audited returns for Universal Basic Resources, maintenance, reserves, and ecological repair. A public fund's stake in an AI or robotics company would not by itself satisfy Chapter 4's requirement for public ownership and operation of covered functions and assets. Private organizations may still build and operate routine, low-risk applications within Chapter 4's public-infrastructure sandbox. As direct public provision and productive capacity mature, monetary returns can give way to direct goods, services, and infrastructure rather than remaining a permanent revenue requirement. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global, the Alaska Permanent Fund, and Singapore's Temasek Holdings are different institutional examples; Norway's fund reports average ownership of about 1.5% of listed companies worldwide.[^ch20-norway-fund] Public ownership does not automatically replace every transition mechanism, eliminate capital flight, investment risk, or capture risk, and it requires lawful acquisition, accountable management, audit, and safeguards against political interference.

[^ch20-norway-fund]: Norges Bank Investment Management states that the fund holds an average of about 1.5% of all listed companies globally. The fund's live value changes with markets and exchange rates; this source supports the ownership share: https://www.nbim.no/en/investments/the-funds-value/

---

### D. Financial Transaction Taxes

Tax the movement of money during the transition, not wealth itself. An illustrative option for public debate would apply a 0.1% charge to stock trades, 0.05% to bond transactions, and 0.01% to currency exchanges; these are not adopted rates or a revenue forecast. Any such mechanism would require a defined base, exemptions or credits for overlapping charges, legal authority, distributional review, and a readiness gate. In a mature post-scarcity condition, direct provision could reduce or change the role of currency and some forms of taxation, but residual scarcity, fiscal institutions, and public choices may retain them.

- May drive transactions offshore
- Requires international coordination
- Could reduce legitimate trading

---

While the alternative approaches in Layer 3 may reduce reliance on direct wealth taxation in a locally selected or phase-specific portfolio, they are not assumed to be additive revenue streams. They must be supported by lawful, reviewable enforcement mechanisms. Layer 4 introduces domestic tools and treaty-compatible options; none is automatic, and cross-border measures must follow Chapter 5's tribunal, appeal, proportionality, and resident-protection safeguards.

## Layer 4: Enforcement Mechanisms

### A. Travel Restrictions

This bounded alternative may be considered only where domestic law supplies authority and a court enters an individualized order supported by specific, current evidence of imminent violence, unlawful flight, or a defined quarantine danger. Geography and duration must be narrow, with counsel, humanitarian, and medical exceptions, automatic expiry, current-evidence renewal, appeal, and full remedy under Chapter 8. The U.S. can deny or revoke passports after certification of seriously delinquent federal tax debt under specified rules; that current-law precedent does not establish a Charter-wide or international travel ban.[^ch20-us-passport-tax-debt] Any treaty-based measure would additionally require ratified scope, independent tribunal review, and resident protections.

[^ch20-us-passport-tax-debt]: The IRS can certify seriously delinquent federal tax debt to the State Department, which generally will deny a passport application and may revoke or limit a passport. This supports the U.S. passport precedent only, not visa restrictions, international agreements, or the claimed behavioral effects: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/revocation-or-denial-of-passport-in-cases-of-certain-unpaid-taxes

- May limit a wealthy tax evader's mobility only under an individualized court order supported by the Chapter 8 grounds described above.
- One intended effect is to encourage compliance.
- May reduce benefits of secrecy for covered actors where lawful, proportionate measures apply; it does not authorize a general travel or financial-system ban

---

### B. Asset Freezing

Court-authorized freezing of specified assets could be considered for a specified unlawful act or enforceable judgment, but only on clear and convincing evidence of the required nexus. The order must be limited in amount and property, protect housing, food, healthcare, counsel, caregiving, and ordinary expenses, and provide notice, appeal, automatic expiry, proof-based renewal, and full remedy. Imminent dissipation permits temporary action only with notice within 24 hours and judicial review within 48 hours; a freeze cannot become a general registry or a substitute for a final judgment.

---

### C. Criminal Penalties

Treating willful tax evasion as a criminal offense remains a domestic-law choice. Extradition or other cross-border cooperation would require a ratified agreement, proof and representation, independent review, proportionality, and appeal. Under U.S. federal law, a willful attempt to evade a federal tax or its payment is a felony punishable by a fine, up to five years in prison, or both. The U.S. offense requires proof of willfulness.[^c20cw-us] Germany is one example of an EU member state with a national tax-evasion offense, punishable by up to five years in prison or a fine; particularly serious cases carry a higher penalty.[^c20cw-germany] Criminal penalties carry serious consequences and are intended to deter evasion.

[^c20cw-us]: 26 U.S.C. §7201, official 2024 U.S. Code, [GovInfo PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/pdf/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleF-chap75-subchapA-partI-sec7201.pdf), printed p. 3850, §7201. It classifies the covered willful attempt as a felony and authorizes a fine, up to five years in prison, or both; five years is the maximum prison term, not mandatory imprisonment. The IRS Criminal Investigation Manual, [§9.1.3.3.2.2](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part9/irm_09-001-003), lines 453–460, lists an additional tax due and owing, an affirmative attempt, and willfulness as the elements; §9.1.3.3.2.2.3, lines 512–516, defines willfulness as a voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty. *Spies v. United States*, 317 U.S. 492, 498–99 (1943), [official U.S. Reports copy](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-317/pdf/USREPORTS-317-492.pdf), interpreting the predecessor federal tax-evasion statute, requires affirmative conduct beyond willful passive omission. These are federal provisions and do not establish the law of every U.S. state or a Charter-wide offense.

### D. Whistleblower Programs

Rewarding people who report tax evasion can help authorities detect violations. An illustrative option would offer 10–30% of recovered taxes as a reward, subject to lawful adoption and review; it is not an adopted rate or collection estimate. National enforcement may be weakened by jurisdictions that offer secrecy or little cooperation. Layer 5 addresses those cross-border gaps. The goal is to reduce opportunities for hidden wealth and avoidance, not to promise that every safe haven can be eliminated.

## Layer 5: Reducing Secrecy Within Lawful Scope

### Treaty-authorized responses to non-cooperation

As long as jurisdictions differ, some wealth may move to avoid tax. National enforcement and international coordination can each reduce that risk, but neither can fully prevent capital flight. Members may consider coordinated, treaty-authorized responses to non-cooperation only within an enumerated ratified instrument and only after independent review. Nonparticipants and their residents are not subject to open-ended global authority; any measure must be necessary, proportionate, time-limited, appealable, and designed to protect ordinary residents and essential services.

---

### A. Financial System Exclusion

One proposed treaty consequence would restrict certain institutions' access to financial messaging or correspondent-banking services after a transparent finding and independent treaty tribunal review. It must be necessary, proportionate, time-limited, appealable, and designed to preserve remittances, humanitarian payments, and essential services. It could raise the cost of non-cooperation, but it would not stop every transaction and could harm people who are not responsible for tax evasion.

### B. Trade Barriers

Participating members could use proportionate trade consequences only where a ratified treaty authorizes them and an independent tribunal finds serious noncompliance. Measures require public reasons, review, sunset or reassessment, and safeguards for ordinary residents and essential goods; they are not automatic punishment of a country or its population.

### C. Public Compliance Reporting

Participating members would publish neutral reports on compliance with obligations listed in a ratified treaty. Reports would explain verified findings, the evidence, and corrective steps, and record progress or unresolved disputes. A jurisdiction could be identified where needed to explain the findings. Before publication, the affected government would have a chance to respond and seek independent review; errors would be corrected publicly. Reports would follow Chapter 5’s treaty limits and Chapter 8’s rights safeguards, protect personal data, and avoid collective blame. Authorities would not use these reports for shaming campaigns or reputational pressure on governments or residents. Lawful journalism, criticism, and public advocacy would remain protected.

### D. Legal Challenges

Challenging tax-haven laws through competent courts and ratified treaty procedures; any consequence for covered non-compliance requires enumerated authority, independent review, proportionality, appeal, expiry or reassessment, and resident protections.

## Political Feasibility Assessment

These country groupings are provisional planning judgments for public debate. Participation depends on each country's lawful decision process and treaty choices.

### Likelihood of Participation by Country Type

**High Likelihood (Most Likely to Participate):**

- **Nordic countries** (Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland) - Norway taxes individual net wealth. Sweden, Denmark, and Finland have administered national wealth taxes in the past. That experience could help them negotiate and administer a new agreement.[^country-nordic]
- **Western European Union members** - The EU has adopted Pillar Two rules for minimum taxation of large multinational groups and rules for beneficial-ownership registers with controlled access. Experience with cross-border tax cooperation and identifying ownership could help support a wealth-tax agreement.[^country-eu]
- **Other developed economies** (Canada, Australia, New Zealand) - Official data show concentrated household wealth. Their tax and statistical institutions could provide a starting point for administration, and wealth inequality gives supporters a case for fairer taxation.[^country-developed]

**Moderate Likelihood:**

- **United States** - Citizenship-based taxation of worldwide income and FATCA financial-account reporting provide precedents for cross-border tax enforcement. Political divisions could make agreement difficult; lasting participation would need broad political support.[^country-us]
- **United Kingdom** - Financial services are a major part of the economy. Rules affecting cross-border finance could meet opposition, making the public case for tax fairness important to negotiations.[^country-uk]
- **Japan** - An aging population puts pressure on public finances. The distribution of wealth and possible political opposition would also shape negotiations.[^country-japan]

**Low Likelihood (Likely to Oppose):**

- **Offshore financial centres** (Cayman Islands, Bermuda, Bahamas, Monaco) - Changes to the treatment of offshore wealth could require these jurisdictions to adapt some financial services. That prospect could make an agreement harder to reach.[^country-offshore]
- **Major emerging economies** (China, India, Brazil) - Concerns about national taxing authority and development priorities could complicate negotiations. All three already participate in OECD/G20 corporate-tax cooperation.[^country-emerging]
- **Resource-rich Gulf states** - In Gulf Cooperation Council member states, oil and gas remain important sources of government revenue, and tax reforms are expanding other revenue sources. Their existing revenue systems could reduce the immediate incentive to join a wealth-tax agreement.[^country-gulf]

### Wealth-Tax and Financial-Cooperation Work Across the Four Periods

#### Maturity (Years 30+)

Continue that review and revise the agreed system as evidence changes.

1. **Early wins** - Demonstrate success with initial coalition members to build momentum
2. **Economic incentives** - Show participating countries benefit from reduced tax competition
3. **Public pressure** - Mobilize public support to counter lobbying from financial interests
4. **Gradual escalation** - Start with information sharing and assistance; consider a proportionate sanction only if a ratified treaty authorizes it and independent review finds it necessary

## Risk Assessment

The probability, impact, and priority labels below are provisional Charter planning judgments for public debate, following Chapter 15's approach to risk assessment.

### Failure Modes and Mitigation Strategies

**Risk 1: Coalition Fragmentation**

*Scenario:* Major economies withdraw from agreement, undermining global coordination.

*Probability:* Medium-High

*Impact:* Catastrophic for global coordination if major withdrawals break the coalition; partial participation may still yield the benefits described below.

*Mitigation:*
- Build redundancy into the system so partial participation still yields benefits
- Create economic incentives for continued participation
- Establish dispute resolution mechanisms to address grievances
- Develop bilateral agreements as backup to multilateral framework

**Risk 2: Tax Haven Countermeasures**

*Scenario:* Tax havens develop sophisticated methods to circumvent covered reporting and treaty-authorized enforcement, including cryptocurrency-based systems, decentralized finance, or new legal structures.

*Probability:* Medium

*Impact:* High - undermines enforcement effectiveness

*Mitigation:*
- Continuous monitoring of emerging financial technologies
- Regular updates to reporting requirements
- International cooperation on cryptocurrency regulation
- Maintain technology advantage in detection capabilities

**Risk 3: Legal Challenges**

*Scenario:* Wealth holders challenge wealth taxation in national and international courts, creating delays and potential invalidation.

*Probability:* High

*Impact:* Medium-High - causes delays and uncertainty

*Mitigation:*
- Ensure constitutional compliance in each jurisdiction
- Build strong legal precedents through careful implementation
- Use existing treaty frameworks (OECD, UN tax conventions)
- Prepare for extended legal battles with adequate resources

**Risk 4: Capital Flight Acceleration**

*Scenario:* Announcement of wealth tax triggers immediate capital flight before enforcement is operational.

*Probability:* Medium-High

*Impact:* High - reduces revenue base and economic activity

*Mitigation:*
- Use only lawfully adopted, publicly labeled measures; no tax, travel limit, or freeze takes effect merely on announcement
- Use phased implementation with Chapter 8 notice, court, appeal, expiry, and remedy safeguards
- Coordinate announcement timing across jurisdictions
- Establish no asset-freeze capability without a particularized court order, clear and convincing nexus, protected living resources, and automatic expiry

**Risk 5: Enforcement Capture**

*Scenario:* Wealthy interests influence enforcement agencies, creating loopholes and selective enforcement.

*Probability:* Medium

*Impact:* High - undermines system integrity

*Mitigation:*
- Transparent enforcement processes with public oversight
- Whistleblower protections and rewards
- Independent audit of enforcement agencies
- Rotation of enforcement personnel
- Criminal penalties for corruption

**Risk 6: Economic Distortion**

*Scenario:* Wealth tax significantly reduces investment, innovation, or economic growth.

*Probability:* Low-Medium (depends on design)

*Impact:* Medium - reduces overall economic welfare

*Mitigation:*
- Set appropriate thresholds to avoid middle-class impact
- Provide exemptions for productive investments
- Design progressive rates that minimize behavioral responses
- Monitor economic impacts and adjust rates accordingly

**Risk 7: Developing Country Exclusion**

*Scenario:* Implementation process marginalizes developing nations, exacerbating global inequality.

*Probability:* Medium

*Impact:* Medium-High - creates geopolitical tensions and reduces legitimacy

*Mitigation:*
- Include developing nations in negotiation process from the start
- Provide technical assistance and capacity building
- Ensure equitable distribution of enforcement costs
- Consider special provisions for least-developed countries

## Practical Implementation Strategy

The proposed stages below follow Chapter 24's four periods. The same conditions apply throughout: participating countries must agree, compatible laws must be in place, and enforcement must follow treaty scope, notice, independent review, court orders where required, appeal, expiry, and remedy.

### Foundation (Years 0–8)

#### Build Coalition

This proposal would ask a coalition of major economies to negotiate minimum wealth-tax standards, information sharing, beneficial-ownership reporting, and exit-tax coordination. G20 and OECD processes could be starting points, but no country has committed to this Charter's wealth-tax framework. Pressure on non-cooperating jurisdictions would need lawful, proportionate design. The following are proposed milestones, not expected results.

- Framework established
- Major economies committed
- Covered secrecy gaps reduced through lawful, treaty-authorized measures where members consent

#### Enforcement Infrastructure

Participating countries would work toward registries, covered digital-asset reporting, and enforceable duties for financial institutions. Automated reporting, analysis tools, and cross-border cooperation could help, but they would not make all assets visible or compliance automatic. The following are proposed milestones.

- Infrastructure operational
- Assets trackable
- Compliance enforced

### Expansion (Years 8–16)

#### Treaty-authorized response to non-cooperation

If agreed rules are breached, participating countries could consider proportionate, treaty-authorized responses. Any travel-related measure must satisfy the Chapter 8 individualized court-order and serious-risk requirements; any asset freeze must be limited to specified assets with a clear and convincing nexus and protected living resources. These measures carry risks for rights and for people who did not evade tax. Their proposed goals are reduced secrecy and more effective collection, not the prevention of all capital flight.

- Covered institutions subject to proportionate, reviewable measures where a ratified treaty authorizes them
- Enforcement effective
- Capital-flight risk reduced where possible

#### Full Implementation

If enough countries agree and pass compatible laws, a coordinated wealth-tax system could begin operating, with collection at covered payment points, legal enforcement, and regular review. Its revenue and sustainability would have to be measured. Some secrecy jurisdictions and avoidance routes may remain.

- Sustainable revenue
- Fewer usable secrecy gaps
- Wealth taxation viable

### Global Floor (Years 16–30)

Continue the conditional implementation described above where participating countries agree, compatible laws remain in place, and readiness review supports it.

### Maturity (Years 30+)

Continue regular review of revenue, sustainability, and remaining secrecy and avoidance routes within the same lawful safeguards.

## The Sovereign Wealth Fund Alternative

Instead of relying only on a wealth tax, a sovereign wealth fund could own part of productive assets and distribute some returns to the public while money remains in use. Public ownership is the principal long-term substitute for tax-dependent essential provision; as direct provision and productive capacity mature, monetary returns can give way to direct services and infrastructure. This might include equity in AI and robotics companies, acquired through a publicly debated and lawful process. A public fund's stake in an AI or robotics company would not by itself satisfy Chapter 4's requirement for public ownership and operation of covered functions and assets. Private organizations may still build and operate routine, low-risk applications within Chapter 4's public-infrastructure sandbox. Public ownership is not an automatic replacement for every transition mechanism and does not guarantee an end to capital flight, investment risk, or capture. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global reports average ownership of roughly 1.5% of listed companies worldwide;[^ch20-norway-fund] the Alaska Permanent Fund and Singapore's Temasek Holdings use different models. A proposal to require new companies to sell stakes to a public fund would need a separate legal and economic review. Initial capital, investment risk, governance, and company opposition remain challenges.

## Enforcing Wealth Taxes Across Borders

### Enforcing a Wealth Tax

**"How do we tax wealth without the wealthy simply leaving?"**

Unilateral wealth taxes can face avoidance, valuation, and mobility risks, but the limited evidence does not show that failure is inevitable.[^ch20-oecd-wealth-taxes] The Charter proposes strengthening them through:

1. **Global coordination** - Most countries participate in minimum standards
2. **Enforcement mechanisms** - Travel, assets, financial system controls
3. **Treaty-authorized responses to secrecy** - Address covered non-cooperation through enumerated, ratified, independently reviewed, proportionate measures intended to reduce secrecy, with safeguards for ordinary residents and essential services.
4. **Alternative models** - Sovereign wealth funds, consumption taxes

### The Path Forward

| From | To |
|------|-----|
| Unilateral wealth taxes | Global minimum standards |
| Secrecy gaps remain | Covered gaps addressed through consent-based, treaty-authorized, reviewable measures |
| Wealth can move across borders | Fewer avoidable gaps, with mobility risk still present |
| Enforcement is difficult | Better information and collection at covered points |
| Revenue is uncertain | Revenue and costs measured before claims of sustainability |

### The Main Point

Global coordination could make wealth-tax enforcement stronger, but a well-designed national tax is not automatically futile. This Charter proposes starting with willing major economies, improving ownership information and lawful collection, reducing secrecy gaps through consent-based treaty measures, and testing public ownership as the principal long-term substitute for tax-dependent essential provision. Success also depends on valuation rules, privacy, due process, administration, participation, and how people respond. The goal is not to punish wealth. It is to ask extreme concentrations of wealth to contribute fairly to the society that made that wealth possible.
