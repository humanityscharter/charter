# Chapter 4: AI and Robotics Infrastructure

*Why technology must be public infrastructure, not private property*

---

## Introduction: Who Controls the Machines?

The biggest question of the automation age is not only whether machines work. It is who owns them, who directs them, and who receives what they produce. If computing clusters, foundation models, and physical robots remain corporate monopolies, a small group can collect the economic rent while most people lose income and a meaningful say over the systems shaping their lives.

There is another direction. Artificial intelligence and robotics can be built as open, decentralized public-purpose infrastructure, governed more like clean water systems, electricity grids, and internet protocols. In that model, accountable ownership or control, enforceable rights, independent oversight, and public remedies are required. Public ownership can support access and accountability, but it is never proof of safety, privacy, competence, democratic legitimacy, or public benefit.

This chapter covers infrastructure ownership, universal access, compute allocation, routine applications, concentration safeguards, and infrastructure transition work. Chapter 17 alone sets AI safety rules. The rules on governance (Chapter 5), rights (Chapter 8), privacy and security (Chapter 9), risks (Chapter 15), and transition planning (Chapter 24) still govern those subjects.

## The Current Reality

### Who Controls AI and Robotics Today?

**Major technology corporations** control much of the capital, data, specialized hardware, and cloud capacity used to develop advanced AI. In 2025, industry produced more than 90% of the notable AI models counted by Stanford's AI Index; OpenAI, Google, and Alibaba were the largest contributors in that dataset.[^ch4-ai-index-2026] In the second quarter of 2026, Amazon, Microsoft, and Google held 28%, 20%, and 15% of worldwide cloud-infrastructure-service revenue, respectively.[^ch4-cloud-market-2026]

**National governments** are developing sovereign AI capabilities, military applications, and surveillance systems. Security competition can pressure institutions to prioritize speed and secrecy over public accountability.

**Wealthy individuals, foundations, and open-source communities** add research and experimentation, but their resources and reach are uneven. Open collaboration can widen participation without by itself solving concentration, misuse, or safety problems.

### The Problem: Concentration is Accelerating

The capital requirements for frontier AI are enormous.[^ch4-ai-index] This creates a dangerous trajectory:

- fewer players control more capability;
- profit motives and national competition can override public benefit;
- proprietary systems make independent scrutiny harder; and
- control over compute, energy, data, and robotics can become control over the economy itself.

This is the risk of techno-feudalism: technological power concentrates in a few hands and extracts value from users and workers instead of supporting shared abundance. The answer is not to assume that any public institution will be virtuous. It is to make ownership, access, authority, records, remedies, and independent review visible and contestable.

## The Vision: AI as Public Infrastructure

### Core Principle: Decentralized Public Infrastructure

AI and robotics should be treated like electricity, internet protocols, and roads: shared enabling infrastructure with universal access, interoperability, maintenance, and public accountability. They should not become private platforms that control essential capabilities through proprietary lock-in.

Public ownership is an ownership boundary, not a safety finding. Public systems still require the independent evaluations, rights safeguards, privacy rules, incident controls, and review described elsewhere in the Charter.

### The Public Ownership Boundary

Public ownership and public operation are required for foundational models; major training and training runs; datacenters and compute networks; high-capability systems; robotics infrastructure; and every consequential or high-risk deployment. The requirement follows the function and effect of the system. Outsourcing, contracting, licensing, shell entities, wrappers, procurement clauses, delegated operators, or a nominally routine label cannot evade it.

Foundational models, major training runs, compute networks, robotics enabling infrastructure, and public safety interfaces are public capabilities. A public compute utility may contract for construction or maintenance, but the public body retains ownership, operating accountability, records, and enforceable remedies. Public ownership supports durable access and makes accountability possible; it does not establish that a system is safe or aligned.

Individuals, cooperatives, nonprofits, and private organizations may build and operate only routine, low-risk applications inside the public-infrastructure sandbox described below. They may not privately own a foundation model, independently train a high-capability system, operate a consequential or high-risk deployment, make a rights-affecting decision, or use a contract to avoid these boundaries.

### The Four-Layer Infrastructure Model

#### Layer 1: Foundation layer (public capability)

Publicly owned foundation models, shared protocols, public datasets where lawfully available, and robot designs provide common building blocks. They use open standards and public interfaces. Openness makes access and scrutiny easier, but it does not substitute for evaluation or rights protection.

#### Layer 2: Infrastructure layer (public utility)

Public utilities own and operate datacenters, compute networks, energy and network interfaces, and robot-manufacturing capacity. Regional and local facilities provide resilience and reduce dependence on a single provider. Utilities publish capacity, maintenance, environmental impacts, outages, access rules, and remedies.

#### Layer 3: Application layer (bounded pluralism)

Local services and applications can differ. Communities, cooperatives, nonprofits, and private organizations may compete on service quality and design within the routine sandbox. The public foundation, safety interfaces, interoperability rules, and accountability requirements remain shared. A routine application's own source may remain private, but its operator, permissions, data practices, monitoring, safety classification, and compatibility/interface information are public.

#### Layer 4: Personal layer (universal individual access)

People may use interoperable personal devices and robots built on public protocols. Personal control must include privacy, portability, accessibility, repair, and the ability to change providers without losing essential data or services. Chapter 9 supplies the privacy and security rules.

## Universal Functional Computing Access

If adopted, every person would receive guaranteed free, functionally adequate computing access for public services, education, communication, creative work, and civic participation. The proposed requirement includes a suitable personal device, connectivity, maintenance, replacement, technical support, accessibility features, and necessary assistive technologies. Adequate community computing would complement individual access for shared work, training, repair, and high-demand tasks; it would never substitute for individual access.

In practical terms, this is free, adequate personal and community compute, with accessible and assistive technology, equipment, connectivity, and support needed to use it.

Chapter 6 is the canonical home for the fiscal portfolio and for the distinction between physical accounting, user-facing payment, and administrative allocation. In this chapter, energy and resource accounting measures capacity, maintenance needs, and ecological burden so that public infrastructure can be planned and allocated responsibly. It is not a household bill, a general compute tax, or permission to auction public capacity to the highest bidder.

Adequacy is defined by the functions people need and by reviewed accessibility needs, not by a frozen hardware specification. Citizen assemblies periodically review the functions, service levels, and support obligations in public. A person can challenge denial, exclusion, inaccessible design, or service failure through the rights and administrative remedies in Chapters 5 and 8.

## Public Compute Allocation Above the Baseline

The universal baseline is a right of access. Scarce compute above that baseline is a public resource allocated under published rules rather than private purchasing power. Standing citizen assemblies enact the criteria; the public compute utility applies them administratively and publishes the record.

Published allocation criteria cover public benefit, scientific value, urgency, ecological capacity, regional equity, and the needs of affected communities. The public compute utility records and publishes requests, decisions, capacity and energy/resource use, conflicts of interest, reasons, conditions, and appeal records. For equal requests, use a lottery or fair queue rather than insider access or discretionary favoritism.

The professional safety authority decides safety eligibility under Chapter 17. It does not allocate compute, set social priorities, or decide which research or service deserves scarce capacity. Compute allocation and safety authorization are separate decisions made by separate bodies.

## The Routine Application Sandbox

Routine, low-risk applications may be built by individuals, cooperatives, nonprofits, and private organizations in a sandbox on public infrastructure. The sandbox is for bounded, reversible uses that stay below every consequential, high-risk, and rights-affecting trigger. It supports pluralism and experimentation without creating a private market in foundational capability.

Every routine operator must use open standards and public safety interfaces; disclose user permissions and material limits; minimize data; maintain monitoring and records; accept liability and accountability for harm; and support portability and accessibility. Public foundation models, protocols, safety interfaces, and enabling infrastructure remain open. The application's own source may remain private only when the operator's identity and the permissions, data practices, monitoring, safety classification, and compatibility/interface information are publicly disclosed and available.

An application performing a covered public function, becoming rights-affecting, or crossing into consequential or high-risk use leaves the routine exception. It then requires public ownership and operation, independent evaluation, and the controlling rules in Chapters 8 and 17. No private organization may evade that result through an API wrapper, subcontract, license, procurement clause, or delegated operator. Contract evasion is prohibited.

## Automatic Upward Review

A material increase in capability, data, network, tool, robot, or infrastructure access; autonomy; scale; affected population; cascade exposure; or potential harm automatically pauses the expanded operation and triggers automatic upward reclassification. The operator must notify the responsible public bodies, preserve records, and provide the information needed for independent evaluation. Contracts, outsourcing, licensing, and a routine label cannot avoid reclassification.

The independent evaluator assesses the changed system. The professional safety authority applies Chapter 17's published authorization rules. The public compute utility cannot decide safety authorization, and no operator can authorize itself. Operation resumes only after the required evidence, rights review, and fresh authorization are complete.

## Concentration Safeguards

Public ownership must be paired with practical anti-capture safeguards:

- **Interoperability:** Open protocols and data portability prevent dependence on one platform.
- **Distributed capacity:** Regional compute and manufacturing reduce single points of failure and concentration.
- **Transparent procurement:** Contracts, beneficial ownership, performance records, and conflicts are public.
- **Public allocation records:** Criteria, queue or lottery results, energy use, reasons, and appeals are inspectable.
- **Independent oversight:** Evaluators, rights offices, courts, assemblies, and complaint channels remain institutionally separate.
- **Privacy and security limits:** Chapter 9 prevents infrastructure from becoming generalized surveillance.
- **Rights supremacy:** Chapter 8 controls whenever an AI system affects liberty, privacy, movement, property, essential services, legal status, livelihood, or meaningful opportunity.

Public ownership can fail through surveillance, political targeting, secrecy, captured evaluation, suppressed complaints, administrative self-protection, or pressure to favor a prestigious project. Those are reasons for stronger records, remedies, independent evaluation, conflict rules, and judicial review. They are not reasons to treat public control as proof of alignment.

## Deployment Context and Readiness

Infrastructure examples include automated farming, modular construction, maintenance, hazardous work, accessible transit, energy management, healthcare support, education tools, environmental monitoring, and household assistance. Each is a proposed application, not an automatic authorization. The responsible authority must establish evidence, reversibility, ecological limits, affected-community participation, and a tested fallback before scale.

### Near-term work: establish the public base

Begin public procurement, open standards, universal device and connectivity pilots, public compute facilities, maintenance and repair capacity, accessibility testing, and regional governance. Existing concentration and labor impacts should be measured rather than assumed. Early projects remain subject to Chapter 17 classification and Chapter 8 rights review.

### Middle-term work: demonstrate and expand

Build resilient regional utilities, open model and robotics capacity, public-interest applications, skills programs, and cross-region interoperability. Expand only where institutional capacity, universal access, ecological limits, independent evaluation, and public accountability are demonstrated. A technical milestone alone is not a readiness finding.

### Long-term work: federate and maintain

Mature systems can coordinate across jurisdictions through consent-based, ratified agreements and shared verification. The work remains revisable: communities may choose different lawful service designs, and no calendar guarantees that a capability, utility, or international agreement will be ready. Chapter 24 is the authoritative transition roadmap; this chapter supplies infrastructure milestones that must fit its readiness gates.

## Conditional Abundance

The Charter's abundance vision is ambitious but evidence-sensitive. Automation may replace much production labor; renewable energy and potentially fusion may reduce energy constraints; and material science, recycling, and circular production may reduce resource scarcity. These are pathways to investigate, not guaranteed forecasts.

Functional post-scarcity means universal material security and greatly reduced dependence on compulsory production labor. It does not mean that every material, location, ecological service, time, attention, or human relationship becomes unlimited. Tradeoffs, maintenance, ecological boundaries, and democratic choices remain. A public infrastructure model is valuable partly because it lets people share gains and negotiate those limits rather than leaving essential capacity to whoever owns the bottleneck.

## What This Chapter Does and Does Not Decide

This chapter decides the infrastructure boundary: ownership, operation, access, allocation, the routine sandbox, concentration safeguards, and infrastructure readiness. It does not create a parallel safety system.

All public systems remain subject to the safety canon in Chapter 17, stable risk IDs in Chapter 15, Layer 3 authority rules in Chapter 5, rights safeguards in Chapter 8, privacy and security rules in Chapter 9, and transition readiness gates in Chapter 24. Chapter 17 contains the technical approaches, safety mechanisms, failure-mode explanations, evidence, citations, examples, and federated international context for AI safety. Its independent evaluation, authorization, incident, stop, appeal, and treaty rules control.

## Addressing Counter-Arguments

**Innovation may slow without private profit.** Public infrastructure can still support vigorous application-layer experimentation, cooperative enterprise, and competition on service quality. The public internet's protocols show how shared infrastructure can support wide innovation, but the analogy is not proof that every public model will work.[^ch4-nsf-internet]

**Public institutions can be inefficient or captured.** This concern is valid. Published allocation, regional capacity, independent evaluation, public records, complaint channels, conflict rules, judicial review, and the ability to replace administrators are safeguards against both bureaucratic drift and private monopoly. They reduce risk; they do not guarantee success.

**Open systems may enable misuse.** Public interfaces and routine applications must be bounded, monitored, and automatically reclassified. Chapter 17 governs high-risk openness, prohibited capabilities, contained safety research, and independent authorization. International verification can help, but a nuclear safeguard or climate-coordination precedent is not a ready-made AI rule.[^ch4-iaea-safeguards][^ch4-paris-ndcs]

**Global coordination is difficult.** Coordination should begin with voluntary, ratified, consent-based cooperation and remain within the narrow treaty architecture in Chapters 5 and 17. It must not become a central global government or an unratified authority over domestic rights.

## Conditions for Shared AI and Robotics Infrastructure

AI and robotics can become shared infrastructure only when the public owns and operates the foundational and consequential layers, every person has functional access, scarce compute is allocated transparently, and routine applications remain bounded. Ownership makes access and accountability possible; independent safety and rights institutions decide whether systems may operate.

This proposal does not promise that technology will eliminate scarcity or harm. The goal is to build public capacity to test, learn, distribute gains, and correct failures while preserving pluralism, ecological limits, and human authority. Chapter 5 explains the governance needed to keep that capacity accountable.
