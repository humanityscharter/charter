# Chapter 3: Human Nature and Purpose

*Why abundance society works with human nature*

---

## Introduction: What Shapes Human Nature?

"What about human nature?"

This question can stop a conversation about abundance. It sits behind many objections to a post-scarcity society:

- "Humans are naturally selfish"
- "Greed drives progress"
- "Competition is natural"
- "Without scarcity, people will become lazy and entitled"
- "You can't change human nature"

These concerns go to what it means to be human. If people are fundamentally competitive, aggressive, and selfish, then an abundance society is impractical and dangerous. It would collapse because it asks people to be something they are not.

But those assumptions do not tell the whole story.

Research suggests that human nature is not fixed in every respect. **The Charter calls this bounded plasticity.** We have biological tendencies and real limits, but our behavior within those limits is flexible and responds to our surroundings. The "bounds" include shared human needs and capacities: social connection, security, meaning and purpose, and the capacity for both empathy and aggression. The "plasticity" is the way those capacities can take very different forms across cultures and living conditions. Change the conditions, and their expression can change too.

This chapter looks at what the research says about human nature, how scarcity shapes behavior, and what may happen when people have the security to develop their better qualities. It also takes up the questions of purpose and meaning that remain when survival no longer drives every decision.

## The Human Nature Debate

### Three Competing Views

Throughout history, philosophers, scientists, and thinkers have proposed three main views of human nature:

| View | Core Claim | Implications |
|------|------------|--------------|
| **Fixed Nature** | Humans have immutable traits that cannot be changed | Society must work with, not against, human nature; scarcity is inevitable |
| **Blank Slate** | Humans are entirely shaped by environment and experience | We can design any society we want; human nature is infinitely malleable |
| **Bounded Plasticity** | Humans have biological tendencies but they're flexible within limits | We can shape expression within biological constraints; some things change, others don't |

Studies find that cooperation and social learning vary across some tasks and cultures, though a large meta-analysis found little cross-society variation in one game-based cooperation measure ([Henrich et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X05000142); [Mesoudi et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2014.2209); [Spadaro et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000389)). Twin research finds both genetic and individual-environment contributions to measured adolescent prosocial behavior, and adult training can change specific brain structures ([Gregory et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2008.00739.x); [Draganski et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1038/427311a)). The Charter calls this balance bounded plasticity: biology sets real limits, while incentives, institutions, and social conditions may shape how capacities are expressed.

### What Science Says

**Evolutionary Psychology:**
Humans evolved in small hunter-gatherer groups where cooperation was necessary for survival. We could not have survived without one another. Competition also existed within and between groups. Both self-interest and altruism are part of us.

**Anthropology:**
Cross-cultural studies show substantial variation in human behavior. The dated homicide findings below illustrate differences across places and periods, along with the limits of comparing them. Some historical datasets show long declines in homicide in parts of Europe. The point here is that violence rates can change; the chapter explores how social conditions may influence behavior.

**Neuroscience:**
The human brain has the capacity for empathy and aggression. Both are neurologically possible, and context affects which response is activated. Neuroplasticity also allows the brain to change throughout life.[^ch3-lifespan-neuroplasticity]

**Psychology:**
Behavior is influenced by environment, incentives, and social norms, though individual variation exists. Research shows that changing incentives and social structures can shift behavioral patterns at scale, though the rate and extent of change vary across individuals and contexts.

**The conclusion:** Human nature includes a range of possibilities that respond to context.

## Aggression: Natural or Contextual?

### The Common Argument

"Humans are naturally aggressive. Look at war, violence, conflict. It's in our nature."

This argument points to something real. Violence exists. War has been part of human history, and our primate relatives show aggression. But that is not the whole picture.

### The Evidence for Contextual Aggression

**Reported homicide findings vary by place and period:**

| Population or dataset | Period | Reported finding |
| ----------------------- | -------- | ------------------ |
| !Kung people in Lee's survey | Reported in 1979 | 29.3 homicides per 100,000 people per year; 41.9 for the narrower 1920–1955 period[^ch3-knauft-homicide] |
| Semai communities described by Dentan | 1955–1977 | At least two reported murders[^ch3-knauft-homicide] |
| European historical jurisdictions in Eisner's dataset | Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries | Mean of 32 per 100,000 across 76 estimates[^ch3-eisner-homicide] |
| European national series in Eisner's dataset | Twentieth century | Mean of 1.4 per 100,000[^ch3-eisner-homicide] |
| United States, national estimate | 2023 | 5.9 homicide victimizations per 100,000 people[^ch3-bjs-homicide-2023] |

**Reading These Findings:** Social norms and institutions can shape how people handle conflict, but no one row proves that a whole society is violent or peaceful by nature. Small-population ethnography, historical court records, and modern national reporting use different definitions and methods. Their rates should not be read as a clean ranking of cultures.

**Some long-term violence trends show change.** Several historical datasets show long declines in homicide in parts of Europe, though the earliest estimates remain disputed. That is evidence that rates can change; it does not establish one worldwide pattern or prove that people everywhere are becoming more peaceful.

### What Triggers Aggression?

Research identifies specific triggers for aggressive behavior:

| Trigger | Mechanism | Can Be Reduced? |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Resource Scarcity** | Competition for survival | Yes, through abundance |
| **Threat Perception** | Fear, defensive response | Yes, through security |
| **Status Competition** | Social hierarchy struggles | Partially, through revaluation |
| **Frustration** | Blocked goals | Yes, through alternatives |
| **Learned Behavior** | Cultural norms | Yes, through culture change |
| **Mental Health** | Psychological factors | Partially, through treatment |

### Aggression in Abundance Society

When material deprivation is reduced:

**What Changes:**
- Reducing material insecurity can reduce some pressures that contribute to conflict and competition
- Basic security is guaranteed
- Status is no longer tied to accumulation
- More outlets for energy (creative, exploratory, playful)

**What Might Remain:**
- Physical competition (sports, games)
- Intellectual debate and challenge
- Playful rivalry
- Protective instincts

**Key Finding:** Aggression does not necessarily disappear. It may move into different outlets. The drive for competition can become sport, play, and creative challenge rather than violence and domination, as seen in some historical transitions from warfare to organized sports.

## Greed: Anxiety or Nature?

### The Common Argument

"Greed is natural. People will always want more. Accumulation is human nature."

This argument assumes that wanting more than you need is an unchangeable part of being human. The evidence suggests that insecurity and living conditions also matter.

### The Evidence for Conditioned Greed

**Insecurity can change what people keep and share.** In specific studies, uncertainty about future pension benefits was linked to higher savings in some households, and perceived shortages during COVID-19 were linked to panic buying ([Murata, 2003](https://www.imes.boj.or.jp/research/papers/english/me21-3-2.pdf); [David et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2021.114062)). Saving or buying extra supplies is not the same as clinical hoarding. In a small experiment, thirsty participants still shared water ([Kause et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0201358)). The Charter proposes greater security so people could share without risking their own needs, if they choose.

**Many cultures practice radical generosity:**
- **Hunter-gatherers** often practice "demand sharing," where resources are distributed on request
- **Potlatch cultures** gain status through giving
- **Modern philanthropy** shows wealthy people giving billions when secure
- **Volunteer work** demonstrates people giving time freely when not struggling

**Inequality can make sharing harder in some settings.** Experiments have found less charitable giving or cooperation under some unequal conditions, but other tests found little effect or results that depended on context ([Duquette and Hargaden, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2021.03.030); [Nishi et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature15392); [Schmukle et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1807942116)). Those studies do not establish that inequality causes greed or hoarding. The Charter proposes that greater security and fairness could make sharing easier, but neither guarantees that people will share.

### The Scarcity Mindset

One possible cycle looks like this:

```
Scarcity → Fear of loss → Hoarding → More scarcity for others → More fear → More hoarding
```

The system around people may help sustain this cycle. Changing those conditions could help weaken it.

**Theoretical Note:** The "paradox" mechanism (hoarding → reduced cooperation → perpetuates scarcity) is a theoretical extension of scarcity mindset research, not a directly empirically-verified finding. It represents a plausible system dynamic consistent with the broader evidence base.

### Greed in Abundance Society

**What Could Change:**
- The Charter aims to secure basic needs so people do not need to hoard for survival
- Status could shift from accumulation toward contribution
- Greater security may reduce anxiety
- Generosity could become easier and more common

**What Might Remain:**
- Desire for unique or exclusive items
- Personal taste and preferences
- Quality preferences
- Curation and collection as hobby (not accumulation from anxiety)

**Key Insight:** Scarcity anxiety can encourage hoarding and make generosity harder. Greater security may ease that pressure, though it will not erase every desire to accumulate. Preference, taste, collecting, and interest in unique items can remain without being driven only by fear.

## Dominance: Response to Competition

### The Common Argument

"Humans naturally seek dominance. Hierarchies are inevitable. Power corrupts."

This argument points to the fact that human societies create hierarchies. But not every hierarchy works the same way.

### Types of Hierarchy

**1. Dominance Hierarchy**
- Based on force, intimidation, coercion
- "I'm better because I can dominate you"
- Creates resentment, resistance, fear
- Common in highly competitive systems

**2. Prestige Hierarchy**
- Based on skill, knowledge, contribution
- "I'm respected because I help others"
- Creates admiration, learning, growth
- Common in cooperative systems

**3. Moral Hierarchy**
- Based on character, ethics, trustworthiness
- "I'm respected because I'm reliable and good"
- Creates trust, community, stability
- Common in close-knit groups

**Key Finding:** Humans naturally seek status, but the *basis* of status varies dramatically by culture. Anthropological research documents prestige-based and dominance-based status systems across different societies. The prestige/dominance distinction was established by Henrich & Gil-White (2001).[^ch3-prestige-dominance] Ferguson's response to Chagnon's account of Yanomami warfare shows how much interpretation of cross-cultural evidence can be debated.[^ch3-ferguson-yanomami] We can have prestige and moral hierarchies without dominance.

### Dominance in Abundance Society

**What the Charter Aims to Change:**
- Status should depend less on wealth accumulation
- Leadership should be valued for service rather than control
- Respect should come more from contribution than dominance
- Social hierarchies should recognize skill and contribution without granting permanent personal power

Under the Chapter 5 proposal, public roles follow different rules: citizen assemblies are selected by lot, senior administrators serve six-year nonrenewable terms, and ordinary civil-service roles are merit-based under transparent rules.

**What Might Remain:**
- Natural leadership abilities
- Desire for recognition and appreciation
- Skill-based prestige
- Role specialization

**Key Insight:** Dominance behavior is often a response to competition for scarce resources. When resources are abundant and status comes from contribution instead of accumulation, that drive can be directed toward service and leadership.

## Cooperation: Equally Natural

### The Case for Natural Cooperation

Aggression and greed are part of the record, but so is cooperation:

**Neurological Evidence:**
- **Oxytocin** has been labeled the "trust hormone," but this claim has not replicated well in controlled studies (Nave, Camerer, & McCullough, 2015).[^ch3-oxytocin-replication] Current research supports the "Social Salience Hypothesis" (Shamay-Tsoory et al., 2015), which reframes oxytocin's effects as context-dependent rather than universally pro-social. The original trust-promotion findings are now considered oversimplified.
- **Mirror neuron systems** have been proposed to play a role in empathy and understanding, but this remains one of the most debated areas in cognitive neuroscience. Gregory Hickok's *The Myth of Mirror Neurons* (2014) argues the empathy connection lacks empirical support, and recent critical reviews note the field is built on a "tenuous foundation" (Cook et al., 2014; Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2022)
- Some studies suggest infants show preference for helpful individuals. The foundational work by Kuhlmeier, Wynn, & Bloom (2003) examined attribution of dispositional states; Hamlin, Wynn, & Bloom (2007) subsequently demonstrated preference for helpers using an adapted paradigm. However, a 2024 large-scale multi-lab replication study found mixed/less robust results, indicating findings vary by methodology and context.[^ch3-infant-helper-replication]
- People are capable of connection and cooperation, but circumstances shape how they act.

**Note on Oxytocin:** While oxytocin is often described as promoting bonding and trust, recent critical reviews indicate these findings have not replicated consistently. The effects appear to be highly context-dependent, with oxytocin potentially amplifying existing social tendencies rather than universally promoting prosocial behavior (Nave et al., 2015; Shamay-Tsoory et al., 2015).

**Evolutionary Evidence:**
- In studied hunter-gatherer communities, food sharing helped reduce the risk of going without.[^ch3-forager-food-sharing]
- Humans are highly social, and cooperation often extends beyond families.
- Coordinating tasks such as hunting can help groups obtain food that would be harder to secure alone.[^ch3-coordinated-hunting]
- People help one another and share resources in many cultures.[^ch3-cross-cultural-helping]

**Cross-Cultural Evidence:**
- Indigenous societies often practice radical sharing
- Religious communities emphasize generosity
- People give to causes they care about.
- People also volunteer their time and skills.

### A Proposed Framework for Cooperation

These four combinations are the Charter's working framework for considering how resource conditions and social relationships may shape cooperation. The patterns are proposals for discussion, not established outcomes.

| Condition | Possible Pattern in the Charter's Framework |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Scarcity + competition | Often competitive, cooperation limited to in-groups |
| Scarcity + community | Mutual aid emerges despite constraints |
| Abundance + competition | Higher cooperation, but status competition may persist |
| Abundance + community | Conditions favorable for broad cooperation |

*Note: Some narrower findings inform this discussion: a task-resource experiment found more in-group bias when resources were insufficient for both recipients to succeed[^ch3-limited-resource-ingroup]; a seasonal field study found lower trust-game investments among Tanzanian farmers in the lean than post-harvest season[^ch3-seasonal-trust]; and reciprocal food sharing among Agta foragers was associated with greater camp stability[^ch3-forager-food-sharing]. These studies examine different behaviors and settings; none tests all four combinations or predicts outcomes in a future abundance society.*

**Key Insight:** The Charter would use abundance to secure basic needs and strengthen the community ties that support cooperation.

## How Scarcity Shapes Behavior

Scarcity and greater material security can shape the conditions in which people make choices. Studies test specific tasks, programs, and populations; the distinctions below separate those findings from possibilities the Charter proposes. A finding in one setting cannot predict how everyone would act in an abundance society.

### The Scarcity Effect

**Psychological Impact:**

*Studied in specific settings*

- **Short-term thinking:** Before payday, low-income U.S. households showed more present-focused choices for money, but not real effort. A Mali transfer study found a modest increase in measured patience ([Carvalho et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481); [Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016)).
- **Increased stress and anxiety:** Lab participants reported more stress under induced scarcity; Mali transfer recipients reported less stress and worry. These self-reports do not establish chronic anxiety ([Huijsmans et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818572116); [Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016)).
- **Reduced cognitive capacity:** Mani et al. found lower performance on selected reasoning and cognitive-control tasks in a financial-scenario experiment and a before/after-harvest study of Indian sugarcane farmers. Later results vary: payday measures showed no difference; Liberian cash-transfer effects on executive-function tasks were small and inconclusive ([Mani et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1238041); [Carvalho et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481); [Szaszi et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2023.07.009)).

*Proposed possibilities*

- **Tunnel vision (focus on immediate needs):** Experiments and an author replication report some attention and borrowing effects; a 2026 reanalysis challenges the tunneling explanation. The mechanism remains contested and task-specific ([Shah et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1222426); [Shah et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2018.12.001); [tho Pesch et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-78074-y)).
- **Increased competition and suspicion:** Tanzanian farmers made lower trust-game investments in the lean season. A small experiment found more cooperation under scarcity with strong prosocial norms, and no difference under weak norms. Effects remain context-dependent ([Agneman et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueac087); [Luo et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15070913)).

**Behavioral Impact:**

*Studied in specific settings*

- **Reduced trust:** Seasonal trust-game investments were lower among Tanzanian farmers in the lean season, consistent with a food-scarcity effect in that setting ([Agneman et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueac087)).

*Proposed possibilities*

- **Hoarding and accumulation:** Protecting or accumulating resources under insecurity is a proposed response; generalized hoarding was not directly tested.
- **Zero-sum thinking:** Scarcity may lead some people to view one person’s gain as another’s loss. The reviewed studies do not establish this as a general effect.
- **Increased conflict:** Conflict incentives increased extraction and resource exhaustion in a common-pool experiment. Field findings on resource decline and support for violence are mixed ([Dannenberg & Schumacher, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2020.11.002); [Tanzania and Kenya study, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpos.2026.1742315)).
- **Less generosity:** A general decline was not established. A Malawi transfer trial found short-run increases in gift giving and a negative long-run spillover ([Cecchi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/jae/ejad017)).

**Neurological Impact:**

*Proposed possibilities*

- **Chronic stress (elevated cortisol):** Cortisol findings vary. A Mexican child program was associated with lower baseline cortisol, especially when mothers had high depressive symptoms; a Kenyan cash-transfer trial found no overall effect ([Fernald & Gunnar, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2009.03.032); [Haushofer & Shapiro, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjw025)).
- **Threat response activation:** A proposed mechanism; direct evidence for generalized neural threat activation was not located.
- **Reduced prefrontal cortex function:** One lab fMRI study found lower left dorsolateral prefrontal activity during bidding under induced scarcity. Activity in one region during one task does not establish broad impairment of prefrontal function ([Huijsmans et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818572116)).
- **Impaired decision-making:** Results vary across choices, bids, trust games, and cognitive tasks. Payday decision-quality measures and overall fMRI bidding effects were unchanged ([Carvalho et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481); [Huijsmans et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818572116)).

**Note on Replication:** Mani and others found that financial strain can affect performance on certain cognitive tasks.[^ch3-scarcity-cognition] Later studies vary by method, population, and measure, including null and small, inconclusive results. A single study does not show that everyone responds the same way ([Carvalho et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481); [Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016); [Szaszi et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2023.07.009)).

**The proposed paradox:** People may protect scarce resources for survival. This can reduce what remains for others and may prompt defensive accumulation. If these responses reduce trust and cooperation, shared solutions can become harder to achieve, which may maintain or worsen scarcity. This is the chapter’s proposed feedback model; Mani et al. did not test the full causal chain.

### The Abundance Effect

Greater material security could ease some pressures and create room for other choices. Studies below measured particular transfers and lab tasks; other outcomes remain Charter possibilities.

**Psychological Impact:**

*Studied in specific settings*

- **Long-term thinking:** Transfers in Mali and Zambia modestly or conditionally changed patience or willingness to wait for money ([Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016); [Handa et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1086/702997)).
- **Reduced stress:** Mali recipients reported less stress and worry; lab participants reported less stress under induced abundance. A Kenyan trial found no overall cortisol effect ([Huijsmans et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818572116); [Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016); [Haushofer & Shapiro, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjw025)).
- **Increased cognitive capacity:** Mani found differences in selected tasks. Other studies found no cognitive-function effect or small, inconclusive effects ([Mani et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1238041); [Carvalho et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481); [Szaszi et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2023.07.009); [Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016)).

*Proposed possibilities*

- **Broader perspective:** A possibility; the reviewed studies did not directly measure a general broadening under greater material security.
- **More creativity and innovation:** A possible aspiration. A financial-concern study measured attention and pleasurable thinking, not creativity or innovation following increased material security ([Wu, Cheek & Shafir, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104449)).

**Behavioral Impact:**

*Studied in specific settings*

- **Sharing and giving:** Malawi transfers increased gift giving in the short run, with a negative long-run spillover ([Cecchi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/jae/ejad017)).
- **Increased trust:** Malawi transfers increased measured trust in the short run, with variation by program and participant; Tanzanian farmers made lower trust-game investments during food scarcity ([Cecchi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/jae/ejad017); [Agneman et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueac087)).

*Proposed possibilities*

- **Positive-sum thinking:** A possibility; the reviewed studies did not directly test positive-sum beliefs under greater material security.
- **Reduced conflict:** A possible aspiration; conflict depends on incentives, institutions, and setting ([Dannenberg & Schumacher, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2020.11.002)).
- **More generosity:** Gift giving is one measured behavior, and Malawi’s effects varied over time ([Cecchi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/jae/ejad017)).

**Neurological Impact:**

*Proposed possibilities*

- **Reduced stress (lower cortisol):** Results are mixed: a Mexican child program was associated with lower baseline cortisol in particular circumstances; a Kenyan trial found no overall effect ([Fernald & Gunnar, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2009.03.032); [Haushofer & Shapiro, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjw025)).
- **Safety response activation:** A possible mechanism for further investigation; the reviewed studies do not directly establish a generalized neural safety response.
- **Enhanced prefrontal cortex function:** One induced-mindset fMRI study found greater left dorsolateral prefrontal activity during bidding. A U.S. infant cash-transfer follow-up found no differences on its preregistered higher-frequency EEG index or frontal gamma measure; an exploratory alpha result needs replication. These activity measures do not establish generally enhanced prefrontal function ([Huijsmans et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1818572116); [Baby’s First Years, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2026.101673)).
- **Better decision-making:** Some transfer studies found gains in patience, trust, or investment; others found no cognitive or decision-quality effect, or uncertain results ([Hidrobo et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2023.10.016); [Cecchi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/jae/ejad017); [Carvalho et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20140481); [Szaszi et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2023.07.009)).

**Key Insight:** Scarcity and greater material security can create different conditions for psychological states and behavior. Responses vary with people, settings, social norms, and the outcome measured. Material security may ease some pressures; community and institutions also shape responses.

## Human Needs and Drives: The Charter's Working View

### Needs and Constraints

For this discussion, the Charter treats the following as enduring needs and constraints:
- Physical needs (food, shelter, safety)
- Social needs (connection, belonging)
- Cognitive needs (curiosity, learning)
- Emotional needs (love, purpose)
- Biological constraints (mortality, physical limitations)

**What Can Change:**
- How needs are met
- Expression of drives
- Cultural values and norms
- Social structures and institutions

### Human Drives and Their Expression

The Charter treats the underlying drives as enduring, while their expression can change:

| Drive | Possible Expression in Scarcity | Possible Expression in Abundance |
|-------|----------------------|------------------------|
| **Security** | Accumulation, competition | Community, stability |
| **Status** | Wealth, power, dominance | Contribution, skill, service |
| **Connection** | Transactional relationships | Genuine community |
| **Purpose** | Survival, provision | Creativity, exploration, service |
| **Growth** | Competition, advancement | Learning, mastery, self-actualization |
| **Autonomy** | Control over resources | Freedom of choice |

**Summary of the Charter's View:** Human behavior can take different forms within real biological limits. The goal is to create conditions where the best of human nature can develop.

## Cultural Evolution

### Humans Change Over Time

Human behavior and values have changed dramatically throughout history:

**Historical Shifts:**
- Hunter-gatherer → Agricultural → Industrial → Information
- Each shift changed human behavior, values, and social structures
- What seems "natural" is often just current cultural norms

**Examples of Profound Change:**
- **Slavery:** Once widely accepted across many societies, now legally abolished in most of the world
- **Women's rights:** Legal rights have expanded in many places
- **Violence:** Long-term homicide declines are documented in parts of Europe, though the earliest estimates are disputed
- **Democracy:** Once a radical idea, now a widely aspired-to governance model
- **Human rights:** Now considered universal and inalienable in international frameworks

**Key Insight:** Parts of life that seem permanent can change within generations. Culture is not fixed.

### The Speed of Cultural Change

- As a planning assumption, some values may shift within a generation
- Institutions take longer to change
- Deep cultural patterns may take much longer; there is no fixed timetable
- But change is possible and ongoing

**The implication:** "Human nature won't allow it" is not enough to end the debate. People have repeatedly shown a capacity to adapt.

## Human Purpose Beyond Survival

### The Purpose Question

When survival is no longer the driving force, what are humans here for?

This may be the deepest question in the Charter. People have offered many answers throughout history:
- Survival and reproduction
- Serving or connecting with something transcendent (divine, ultimate reality, or deeper meaning)
- Creating and building
- Seeking knowledge
- Helping others
- Experiencing and enjoying life
- Achieving enlightenment

**Different people find purpose in different ways.**

### The Hierarchy of Purpose

This hierarchy is an illustrative model of how meeting basic needs may create more room for other sources of purpose. People can move among these levels, pursue several at once, and choose their own priorities.

**Human Purpose Hierarchy**

| Level | Purpose | Includes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Transcendence | Spiritual connection; Unity with something larger; Service to future generations |
| 4 | Creation | Art, science, innovation; Building something that lasts; Contributing to human knowledge |
| 3 | Connection | Deep relationships; Community and belonging; Love and compassion |
| 2 | Growth | Learning and mastery; Personal development; Becoming who you're meant to be |

Meeting basic needs can give people more room to pursue growth, connection, and meaningful work. The Charter hopes greater security will also make space for creation and spiritual exploration, but what people choose will differ.

- Discovery of something new
- Adventure and experience of the unknown
- Understanding the universe
- Expansion of what's possible

- **Intellectual:** Philosophy, theory, ideas
- **Creative:** Art, music, literature, new forms
- **Personal:** Self-discovery, inner journeys
- **Spiritual:** Meditation, mysticism, transcendence

**In Abundance:** People may explore for many reasons, with curiosity playing a larger role and less pressure to claim resources: space for knowledge, science without profit pressure, and art without market pressure.

- Expression of inner experience
- Contribution to the world
- Legacy that lasts
- Joy of the creative act itself

- **Personal:** Self-creation, identity, character
- **Spiritual:** Rituals, practices, traditions

**In Abundance:** The Charter aims to give people more room to create for its own sake: art for art's sake, science for knowledge, and innovation that helps people live well.

- **Humanity:** Connection to all humans
- **Nature:** Connection to the natural world
- **Spiritual:** Connection to something transcendent

**In Abundance:** The Charter aims to give people more time for deep, loving connections and less pressure to form relationships out of necessity. It also aims to strengthen community so fewer people feel isolated.

## Implications for Abundance Society

### Design Principles

**1. Plan for Human Adaptability**
- People can adapt to new conditions
- Don't design only for worst-case scenarios
- Build in flexibility for evolution

**2. Support Positive Tendencies**
- Encourage cooperation and collaboration
- Celebrate generosity and giving
- Value contribution over accumulation

**3. Channel Natural Drives**
- Status through service and skill
- Competition in healthy outlets (sports, art, games)
- Leadership through facilitation and support

**4. Reduce Scarcity Triggers**
- Guarantee basic needs
- Reduce inequality
- Build security and trust

**5. Allow for Variation**
- Not everyone adapts the same way
- Respect individual differences
- Provide multiple paths to fulfillment

### Realistic Expectations

**What to Expect:**
- Some people will adapt quickly
- Some will be reluctant to change or will oppose it
- Some behaviors will persist
- New behaviors will emerge
- Culture will continue evolving

**What NOT to Expect:**
- Everyone becoming perfectly altruistic
- All conflict disappearing
- Human nature being "fixed"
- No one ever being selfish again
- Perfect harmony and agreement

**Design Goal:** Create conditions that support the best parts of human nature while limiting the harm people can do to one another.

## The Transition Pathway

### What to Expect During Transition

These are cultural-change workstream windows nested under Chapter 24's transition periods, not an automatic calendar. Each community may begin later or remain longer, and any scale-up is subject to Chapter 24 readiness gates.
The outcomes below are targets for review, not guaranteed results; evidence and rights-floor performance determine whether a workstream is working.

**Foundation workstream (Years 0-8): Basic Security**
- Material hardship reduced
- Anxiety about basics reduced
- Initial shift in behavior patterns
- Some people may question or oppose the transition because they have concerns about its effects, benefit from current arrangements, or both

**Expansion workstream (Years 8-16): Cultural Shift**
- New norms emerging
- Cooperation becoming easier
- Status systems shifting from accumulation to contribution
- Generosity becoming normalized

**Global Floor workstream (Years 16-30): New Equilibrium**
- Abundance mindset established
- Cooperation and creation become default
- Competition channeled into healthy outlets
- Purpose beyond survival becomes common

**Maturity workstream (Years 30+):** Continued cultural learning and revision; this is a scenario, not a guaranteed endpoint.

### Supporting the Transition

**Individual Level:**
- Practice generosity and sharing
- Challenge scarcity mindset
- Build community connections
- Explore purpose beyond work

**Community Level:**
- Create mutual aid networks
- Build cooperative structures
- Celebrate contribution over accumulation
- Support those struggling with transition

**Systemic Level:**
- Implement universal basic resources
- Restructure status and recognition systems
- Create outlets for creative and exploratory drives
- Support cultural institutions that promote cooperation

## Human Nature Has Bounded Plasticity

### The Answer to "What About Human Nature?"

**The Charter's Synthesis:** Human nature has bounded plasticity. It is neither fixed nor a blank slate.

1. **Aggression** is real but context-dependent. The Charter aims to reduce pressures from scarcity and competition that can contribute to aggression.

2. **Greed** has more than one cause. Scarcity anxiety can encourage hoarding; security may make generosity easier.

3. **Dominance** can respond to competition for resources. The Charter aims to change those conditions so coercive dominance has less room to grow.

4. **Cooperation** is a natural human capacity alongside competition. People can do both, and circumstances influence which has more room to develop.

5. **The environment matters.** Scarcity and greater material security can create different conditions for behavior.

### The Key Insight

**"Human nature" is not an excuse for accepting harmful systems.**

- Humans are adaptable
- Behavior responds to incentives
- Culture shapes expression
- We can change conditions to give better outcomes room to develop

**The goal is to create conditions where the best of human nature has room to develop.**

---

## The Vision

In an abundance society, these possibilities could have more room to develop. Individual outcomes would vary:

- **Aggression** could find expression in play, sport, and healthy competition
- **Greed** could give way in some settings to appreciation, quality preference, and curation
- **Dominance** could be directed toward leadership, service, and mentorship
- **Competition** could include collaboration and friendly rivalry
- **Security** could come more from community than accumulation
- **Status** could come more from contribution than wealth
- **Purpose** could include exploration, creation, connection, and transcendence

**The way human nature is expressed can change.**
