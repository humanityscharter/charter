# Play-Based Development: Supporting Research

This appendix looks at the evidence behind the Charter's case for time to play and learn without coercion. Play takes many forms. A study of an adult-led playful activity, outdoor nature play, or homework time does not by itself tell us what years of free, self-directed play would do. That distinction matters when people are being asked to trust the argument.

## What evidence supports play in development?

The [American Academy of Pediatrics report by Yogman and colleagues (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30126932/) brings together developmental research and makes a pediatric case for protecting play. It is a clinical synthesis, not an experiment proving that one play policy causes a lifetime outcome. The [Harvard Center on the Developing Child's executive-function guide](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Enhancing-and-Practicing-Executive-Function-Skills-with-Children-from-Infancy-to-Adolescence-1.pdf) explains skills such as working memory, self-control, and flexible thinking, and suggests activities to practice them. It does not show that free play alone builds a particular brain region.

[Blair and Raver's 2014 cluster-randomized kindergarten study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0112393) tested a classroom approach that embedded self-regulation support in literacy, mathematics, and science activities. The reported improvements in academic outcomes are evidence for that *combined curriculum*, not a randomized test of unrestricted free play or direct proof of a prefrontal-cortex mechanism.

[Yaffe and colleagues (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-07028-z) compared a brief playful interaction with an adult against a physical-activity interaction. The final sample was **62 children aged 6–10**, split **31 and 31**. The playful group improved on the study's immediate attentional measure, particularly Flanker-task response time, and reported more positive mood and connection to the adult. This is a useful short-term result. It does not establish lasting executive-function development, compare school systems, or measure self-directed free play over time.

[Canepa and Ramenghi's narrative review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2025.1729411/full) was published **6 January 2026** (in a volume labeled 2025). It discusses animal and human work on play and neurorehabilitation. Animal mechanisms and theories about reward or social circuits should not be stated as measured human effects of free play. Claims about cortisol, social circuits, dopamine pathways, or default-mode-network development require directly relevant human studies that measure those mechanisms. The Charter's practical case for play does not depend on claiming those mechanisms are settled.

## What happens when children have more free play or more structured time?

[Caetano, Caetano, and Nielsen (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2023.102503) studied time spent on enrichment activities—including homework, reading, and organized activities—and cognitive and non-cognitive outcomes in a U.S. child panel. Their model estimates that the **last hour of enrichment** had an effect close to zero on measured cognitive skills and a negative effect on measured non-cognitive skills, with the negative finding **concentrated among high-school students**. The study uses a control-function approach to address selection, not random assignment. Its activity table reports homework as **66% of enrichment time across all grades**, and **79% in grades 9–12**; those are sample compositions, not universal rates. The exposure is **enrichment time**, not the amount of free play, and the comparison is not “free-play-rich childhood versus overscheduled childhood.” It does not establish that every structured activity harms mental health or that replacing one with free play would reverse the estimated effect.

[Dankiw and colleagues' 2020 systematic review](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0229006) included **16 studies** of unstructured nature play. It found more consistent positive findings for physical activity and imaginative or dramatic play than for other outcomes, while stressing heterogeneous definitions, outcomes, and study quality. Nature play is worth investigating, but this review does not prove broad health or lifelong cognitive gains.

Claims about play, structured activities, and development should be supported by identifiable research with a clear publication date, population, design, and outcome. Identify whether the source reports an original study or summarizes studies in a review or synthesis. The evidence summarized here does not establish the effects of every form of free play or structured activity.

### Alternative education is a separate evidence question

[Gray and Riley's survey of 75 unschooled adults](https://weareborntolearn.org/research/) records participants' positive self-reports. The small, self-selected adult sample cannot establish average outcomes for children assigned to unschooling; retrospective accounts also do not make it longitudinal follow-up evidence. [Kunzman and Gaither's 2020 homeschooling research review](https://crhe.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/hel1Kunzman_Gaither_9_1_2533336_2020-1.pdf) covers a broader literature, but homeschooling is not one method, and study selection and family differences complicate comparisons. A separate [2024 review of alternative schools](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0190740924000525) describes mixed findings and methodological limits; alternative schools should not be treated as the same intervention as unschooling or free play.

## What the Current Evidence Suggests

| Question | What can be said now | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Early play and development | Pediatric and developmental syntheses support making room for play; a small experiment found a short-term attention benefit from a playful adult interaction. | Different kinds of play and few long-term causal comparisons. |
| Nature play | A systematic review found encouraging physical-activity and imaginative-play results. | Heterogeneous, often limited-quality studies. |
| Structured self-regulation curricula | A randomized kindergarten study reported academic benefits from an integrated classroom approach. | It did not isolate free play. |
| Enrichment time | Marginal-hour research raises questions about the balance of homework and organized activities. | It did not directly compare years of free play with years of structured activities. |
| Homeschooling | A broad research literature exists with mixed methods and outcomes. | Selection differences and varied educational approaches. |
| Unschooling | Adult accounts can reveal valued experiences and problems to study. | Small self-selected samples cannot show population-wide causal effects. |

Children need time, safety, relationships, and room to direct some of their own lives. To test the stronger claims, we need larger, diverse longitudinal studies with clear definitions of free play, structured activity, and coercion; measured academic, social, health, and later-life outcomes; and careful attention to family circumstances and children's own views.
