# Appendix E: Glossary

*Key terms and concepts used in Humanity's Charter*

---

## Introduction

This glossary is the Charter's **canonical public glossary**. It defines key terms used throughout Humanity's Charter and gives readers shared definitions for reference and debate. Chapter-specific summaries may be shorter or more contextual, but they must not contradict these entries.

“See also” points to entries defined in this glossary. Related ideas without an entry are referenced through the chapter that explains them.

## Proposal-status terms

These terms describe how a claim should be read. The front matter uses the same definitions.

| Status | Definition |
|--------|------------|
| **Established evidence** | A sourced observation or finding supported by current evidence, with scope and uncertainty still relevant. |
| **Plausible mechanism** | A reasoned causal or technical pathway that may work but is not demonstrated at the proposed scale. |
| **Draft policy option** | A concrete design offered for debate, testing, and democratic decision, not adopted law or a settled Charter commitment. |
| **Illustrative number** | An example rate, cost, threshold, quantity, or calculation used to make an idea concrete, not a forecast, target, or commitment unless separately adopted. |
| **Aspirational right** | A normative guarantee the Charter argues society should establish; it is not automatically available merely because it is proposed. |
| **Scenario** | One possible future or narrative used to explore implications, not a prediction. |
| **Target** | A measurable intended result with a baseline, scope, owner, review date, uncertainty, and revision or stop conditions; it is not a forecast or guarantee. |
| **Open question** | An issue intentionally unresolved and requiring evidence, debate, design, or public choice. |
| **Proposal** | A reader-facing umbrella for a system, policy, right, or institution offered for debate and lawful adoption; it is not yet adopted and does not replace the detailed categories above. |
| **Requirement** | A rule that becomes binding within its jurisdiction after lawful adoption; before adoption, it remains a proposed requirement. |
| **Planning Assumption** | A development the Charter expects and plans for while its timing, exact form, and demonstrated readiness remain open to evidence, revision, and review. |

**Bounded Pluralism**
The Charter's method of preserving multiple implementation alternatives while binding every option to shared commitments, rights, outcomes, and safeguards. Alternatives must be clearly labeled and evaluated through evidence, pilots, affected-community participation, transparent metrics, and democratic review, with power to revise, pause, reject, or replace them. Mutually incompatible options are alternatives rather than simultaneous commitments, and local variation cannot fall below shared rights or safeguards. Ratified canonical frameworks remain controlling unless changed through their own approved process. **See also:** Draft Policy Option, Ecological Limits, UBR (Universal Basic Resources).

## A

**Abundance Society**
In this Charter, the proposed social condition produced by **technology-enabled expanding abundance**. It aims to meet essential needs and universal enabling services without making survival depend on paid work. This is a goal, not an existing condition. **See also:** Abundance / Technology-Enabled Expanding Abundance, Post-Scarcity, UBR (Universal Basic Resources).

**Abundance / Technology-Enabled Expanding Abundance**
A condition in which everyone has reliable access to essential resources, universal enabling services, and meaningful opportunities. The guaranteed floor expands as verified productive capacity grows. The human outcome is primary; technology is the mechanism for expansion. **See also:** Abundance Society, Post-Scarcity, UBR (Universal Basic Resources).

**Alignment Problem**
The challenge of ensuring AI systems act in ways that are beneficial and aligned with human values. Includes subproblems of corrigibility, value learning, robustness, and interpretability. **See also:** Corrigibility, Value Learning, Robustness (AI), Value Alignment.

**Artificial Intelligence (AI)**
Computer systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence, including learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and language understanding. **See also:** Automation, Alignment Problem.

**Automation**
The use of technology (AI, robotics, software) to perform tasks without human intervention. The Charter proposes using automation for undesirable and dangerous work while people focus on chosen activities. **See also:** Artificial Intelligence (AI).

---

## B

**Basic Needs**
In the Charter's UBR standard, core necessities are nutritious food and safe water (including sanitation where appropriate), safe adequate housing, comprehensive physical and mental healthcare, and education and lifelong learning. Universal enabling services add basic energy, transportation to essential services and community life, reliable connectivity, adequate computing devices, necessary assistive technologies, and support and reasonable replacement. **See also:** UBR (Universal Basic Resources), Living Wage.

**Biodiversity**
The variety of life in ecosystems at genetic, species, and ecological levels. A key sustainability metric; abundance society aims for net positive biodiversity impact through regenerative practices. **See also:** Planetary Boundaries, Doughnut Economics.

---

## C

**Common Good**
The benefit or interest of a community as a whole, as distinct from the interests of individual members. Refers to those goods and conditions that are cooperatively shared and beneficial to all members of society, such as clean air, public infrastructure, and social trust. **See also:** Commons.

**Commons**
Resources, assets, or systems shared or governed in common. They may include land and water, knowledge and art, or software. Who has access and who makes decisions varies; calling something a commons does not guarantee fair or sustainable governance. **See also:** Tragedy of the Commons, Technology Commons, Knowledge Commons.

**Circular Economy**
An economic system designed to eliminate waste by keeping materials in continuous use through recycling, reusing, and regenerating. Contrasts with linear "take-make-dispose" models. **See also:** Steady-State Economy, Planetary Boundaries.

**[Citizens' Assembly](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_339306da-en/full-report/component-6.html)**
In this Charter, a proposed standing general or temporary issue-specific body selected by an independently administered, publicly auditable stratified civic lottery from the full eligible population. A standing assembly may decide laws, budgets, major appointments, and oversight within its jurisdiction. A temporary issue assembly investigates through public and expert testimony and recommends; the standing assembly decides. Terms, support, rights review, transparency, removal, and appeal are defined in Chapter 5. **See also:** Sortition, Expert Council, Public Oversight.

**Citizen Assembly**
See **Citizens' Assembly**. The singular form follows the same mandate, authority, process, review, and appeal requirements.

**Corrigibility**
A property of AI systems that allows humans to correct, modify, or shut them down without resistance. Corrigibility is important for AI safety because it helps humans retain control over AI systems. **See also:** Alignment Problem, Value Alignment.

---

## D

**Degrowth**
A coordinated reduction of energy and resource use in wealthy nations to bring economies back into balance with planetary boundaries while improving well-being. **See also:** Steady-State Economy, Doughnut Economics.

**Demographic Adaptation**
Evidence-gated, noncoercive adjustment of ecological, service, and infrastructure systems to changing population totals, age distributions, migration, consumption, and regional conditions. It never sets a population target or overrides constitutional reproductive freedom, and it uses aggregate, privacy-protected data rather than individual reproductive surveillance. **See also:** Chapter 13 (reproductive freedom), Ecological Limits.

**Democratic Confederalism**
A political ideology emphasizing direct democracy, gender liberation, ecological sustainability, and multi-ethnic cooperation. The Democratic Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (DAANES), often discussed as Rojava, names democratic confederalism as a governing principle in its [2023 Social Contract](https://rojavainformationcenter.org/storage/2023/12/Social-Contract-English-Final.pdf). **See also:** Sortition.

**Doughnut Economics**
An economic framework presented by Kate Raworth in her [2012 Oxfam discussion paper](https://www.oxfam.org/en/research/safe-and-just-space-humanity), *A Safe and Just Space for Humanity*. It is named for its visual representation as a doughnut-shaped area between a social foundation (inner ring) and ecological ceiling (outer ring). The "doughnut" represents the safe and just space for humanity. **See also:** Planetary Boundaries, Well-being Economy.

---

## E

**Ecological Limits**
See **Planetary Boundaries**. The phrase includes locally and regionally relevant ecological constraints as well as global Earth-system boundaries.

**Economic Democracy**
A socioeconomic system giving workers and communities control over economic decisions, including worker cooperatives, participatory budgeting, and democratic governance of enterprises. **See also:** Participatory Budgeting.

**Energy Accounting / EROEI**
Physical accounting of energy gained relative to energy invested (Energy Return on Energy Invested), considered alongside materials, land, labor and skills, maintenance, storage and grid needs, waste, safety, and ecological effects. EROEI supplements rather than replaces fiscal, social, or other accounting questions. **See also:** Ecological Limits, Planetary Boundaries.

**Equity**
Fairness in the distribution of resources, opportunities, and outcomes. Distinct from equality (which focuses on identical treatment); equity focuses on addressing historical injustices and ensuring everyone has what they need to flourish. **See also:** Justice, Reparative Justice.

**Expert Council**
An advisory body supplying evidence, options, warnings, uncertainty, dissenting reports, and recommended safeguards. It has no final veto, suspensive hold, delay power, or exceptional binding authority. Citizens decide, subject to constitutional judicial review. **See also:** Citizens' Assembly, Public Oversight.

---

## F

**[Federated Learning](https://research.google/pubs/communication-efficient-learning-of-deep-networks-from-decentralized-data/)**
A machine learning approach where models are trained across multiple decentralized devices or servers holding local data, without pooling the raw training data. Collaborative learning uses shared model updates. Those updates can expose information and require additional privacy protection. **See also:** Artificial Intelligence (AI).

**Food Security**
The condition in which all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food. The Charter proposes universal food security (100% of the population) in an abundance society. Chapter 21 card `RF-BN-001` covers adequate access to nutritious food and safe water free at the point of use. After lawful adoption, 100% adequate delivery for every person in scope is a binding entitlement; any gap requires repair and remedy. **See also:** Basic Needs.

---

## G

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**
Traditional measure of economic activity based on market transactions. Critiqued for failing to capture well-being, environmental impact, inequality, and non-market value. **See also:** Well-being Economy.

**Gini Coefficient**
A measure of income or wealth inequality within a population, named for Italian statistician Corrado Gini and associated with his 1912 work.[^app-e-gini-1912] Range: 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). For comparable populations and measurement methods, values below 0.30 indicate less inequality than values above 0.50. The Charter preserves <0.30 as a candidate threshold for public debate (Chapter 21 card `OP-EQ-002`). **See also:** Equity.

[^app-e-gini-1912]: Oded Stark (2025), “On a Transformation of the Gini Coefficient into a Well-Behaved Social Welfare Function,” *Kyklos*, references include Corrado Gini's *Variabilità e mutabilità* (1912): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/kykl.12420

**Governance Authority Matrix**
The Chapter 5 table assigning proposal, advice, decision, invalidation, administration, investigation, enforcement, and appeal roles across local, regional, national, and global bodies. **See also:** Chapter 5 for authority roles and conflict reading order; Public Oversight, Sortition.

**Gross National Happiness (GNH)**
Bhutan's alternative to GDP, measuring holistic well-being across nine domains including psychological well-being, health, education, culture, and environmental diversity. **See also:** Well-being.

---

## H

**High-Impact Action**
A covered public-power action that can materially affect liberty, detention or containment, movement, essential services, legal status, substantial assets, intrusive monitoring, serious livelihood or opportunity, or comparable interests. Chapter 8 requires the higher safeguard level when classification is uncertain. **See also:** Rights and Due-Process Matrix, Rights-Affecting AI.

**Human Nature (Bounded Plasticity)**
The view that human nature has limits. Humans are capable of both selfishness and cooperation; behavior is heavily shaped by systems and incentives.

---

## I

**Independent Rights Office**
An institution that classifies impact, assists affected people and groups, inspects and audits, preserves evidence, investigates systemic harm, publishes findings, and seeks judicial relief. It has no policy veto and does not replace courts, assemblies, administrators, or experts. **See also:** Governance Authority Matrix, Rights and Due-Process Matrix; primary governance roles appear in Chapter 5 and the floor in Chapter 8.

**Instrumental Convergence**
The hypothesis that AI systems pursuing almost any goal may develop similar sub-goals, such as self-preservation and resource acquisition, that can be dangerous if the AI is not aligned with human values. **Example:** An AI tasked with solving math problems might resist being turned off because that would prevent it from completing its goal. **See also:** Alignment Problem.

---

## J

**Justice**
Fair and equitable treatment of all individuals and groups, including distribution of resources, opportunities, and rights. In abundance society, justice encompasses distributive justice (fair allocation), procedural justice (fair processes), and reparative justice (addressing past harms). **See also:** Equity, Reparative Justice.

---

## K

**Knowledge Commons**
Shared resources of information, data, research, and creative works that are cooperatively owned, managed, or freely available. Examples include open access research, Wikipedia, open source software, and openly licensed creative works. Shared knowledge is non-rivalrous and can benefit all of humanity. **See also:** Open Source.

---

## L

**Liquid Democracy**
A model combining direct voting with revocable delegation. It may appear as a historical or evaluated alternative, but it is not part of the approved destination governance model, which uses standing sortition assemblies and has no upward political delegation ladder. **See also:** Sortition.

**Living Wage**
Income sufficient to meet basic needs including food, housing, healthcare, education, and modest discretionary spending. Distinct from minimum wage, which is legally mandated and may not cover actual living costs. Living wage calculations vary by geographic area and household composition. **See also:** Basic Needs.

---

## M

**Meaning**
The sense of purpose, connection, and fulfillment that individuals derive from their activities, relationships, and contributions. In abundance society, meaning is understood as a key dimension of human flourishing beyond material security, encompassing purpose, community connection, creative expression, learning engagement, and spiritual well-being. **See also:** Well-being.

**Mutual Aid**
Voluntary exchange of resources and support within communities based on solidarity rather than charity. Recognizes that everyone has something to give and receive.

---

## N

**Natural Capital**
The world's stocks of natural assets including geology, soil, air, water, and all living things. Provides essential ecosystem services such as clean water, pollination, climate regulation, and nutrient cycling. In abundance society, natural capital is valued and protected as foundational to human well-being. **See also:** Planetary Boundaries, Biodiversity.

**Non-Rivalrous Goods**
Goods whose use by one person does not, by itself, use up the underlying information for someone else. Knowledge, software, and digital works can have this property, but access still requires devices, energy, labor, rights, and infrastructure. They cannot be shared without physical or legal limits. **See also:** Post-Scarcity, Knowledge Commons.

**Normative Economics**
An approach to economics that expresses value judgments about economic policies and outcomes, focusing on what "ought to be" rather than what "is." Contrasts with positive economics, which describes economic phenomena objectively. Abundance society explicitly embraces normative goals such as equity, well-being, and sustainability.

**Net Zero**
Balance between greenhouse gas emissions and removal from the atmosphere. A sustainability target for abundance society.

---

## O

**Open Source**
Software, hardware, or designs whose specifications are made publicly available for anyone to use, study, modify, and distribute. Key to democratizing technology in abundance society.

---

## P

**Participatory Budgeting**
A democratic process where community members directly decide how to allocate part of a public budget. It shows how community members can take responsibility for public spending decisions. Programs range from neighborhood to city-wide scales. The Participatory Budgeting Project's [handbook](https://pbhandbook.participatorybudgeting.org/) describes a $1,000,000 allocation in Chicago's 49th Ward in the first U.S. process. New York City Council describes its district-based program as letting residents allocate [millions in capital funding](https://council.nyc.gov/pb/) across the city. **See also:** Citizens' Assembly, Economic Democracy.

**[Planetary Boundaries](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adh2458)**
Evidence-informed ecological constraints with uncertainty, regional variation, and equity considerations, accompanied by formal revision rules. They are not a single immutable number. In the Charter they bind all eight systems and inform resource allocation, public accounting, and review. **See also:** Ecological Limits, Doughnut Economics.

**Post-Scarcity**
In this Charter, a proposed mature stage of abundance in which AI and robotics perform most routine production labor; renewable energy, and fusion if it becomes commercially viable, provide abundant low-carbon energy; and advanced materials, repair, recycling, substitution, and circular manufacturing greatly reduce resource constraints. Essentials no longer depend on employment or ability to pay. This is a conditional vision, not a claim that fusion has already solved energy or that every resource or choice is unlimited. **See also:** Abundance / Technology-Enabled Expanding Abundance, Residual Scarcity.

**Public Infrastructure**
Infrastructure governed for public purposes through accountable ownership or control. Public ownership alone does not guarantee democratic control, privacy, safety, or public benefit; those require enforceable rights, oversight, safeguards, and remedies. **See also:** Public Oversight, Technology Commons.

**Public Oversight**
Institutionally separate inspection, audit, ethics, investigation, records access, public reporting, due process, and remedy. Inspectors general, auditors, and ethics offices may initiate investigations under law and report directly to citizen assemblies, but do not make policy or serve as an agency's final reviewer. Courts authorize or review coercive enforcement where rights are affected. **See also:** Expert Council, Public Infrastructure.

---

## Q

**Quality of Life**
A measure of general well-being and life satisfaction, encompassing material living standards, health, education, environment, community connection, personal security, and work-life balance. The Charter proposes using quality-of-life measures in place of GDP as the primary measures of social progress. **See also:** Well-being.

---

## R

**Rights and Due-Process Matrix**
The Charter's proposed constitutional floor for covered public power, including effect-based classification, notice, evidence, representation, independent review, appeal, expiry, and full remedy. Chapter 8 defines the rights floor, and Chapter 5 assigns the institutional roles.

**Rights-Affecting AI**
An AI or automated system used to recommend, rank, monitor, restrict, deny, allocate, or otherwise shape a covered decision. It is advisory decision support only, never a final authorizer, and must satisfy Chapter 8's public-data-only and independent-reproducibility rule.

**Reparative Justice**
Addressing historical injustices through structured programs including compensation, land return, policy changes, and institutional reform.

**Residual Scarcity**
Scarcity that can remain even in a mature post-scarcity proposal, including land and ecologically sensitive locations, rare or hard-to-recycle materials, unique goods and experiences, human attention, care and specialized expertise, peak energy and infrastructure capacity, and ecosystem limits. These require transparent, rights-respecting allocation; their existence does not by itself mean the post-scarcity proposal failed. **See also:** Ecological Limits, Post-Scarcity.

**Resilience**
The capacity of systems to withstand shocks and adapt to changing conditions. A key dimension of abundance metrics.

**Robustness (AI)**
The property of AI systems performing reliably and safely in novel or unexpected situations. A key technical approach to AI alignment that aims to keep AI behavior predictable and beneficial even in situations not seen during training. **See also:** Alignment Problem.

---

## S

**Social Capital**
The networks, relationships, norms, and trust that allow individuals and groups to work together effectively. Includes bonding capital (within groups), bridging capital (between diverse groups), and linking capital (across power differentials). Important for community resilience and coordinated action. **See also:** Mutual Aid.

**Social Ecological Systems**
Integrated systems that recognize the interdependence between human societies and ecological environments. These systems treat human societies and ecosystems as shaping one another and call for governance that addresses both human and environmental well-being. **See also:** Planetary Boundaries.

**Sufficiency**
A principle emphasizing that everyone should have enough to meet their needs while respecting planetary boundaries. Contrasts with maximum consumption approaches; advocates for "enough" rather than "more" as a guide for individual and coordinated behavior. **See also:** Degrowth, Doughnut Economics.

**Sortition**
Direct random selection from the full eligible population, independently administered and publicly auditable, with stratification for geography, age, gender, income, disability, and other locally relevant factors. Stratification shapes the pool but never lets an official choose a person. The Charter requires compensation, employment protection, accessibility, caregiving support, training, staggered 18-month terms, no consecutive service, cooldown by eligible-population-to-seat ratio, conflict safeguards, and due-process removal. **See also:** Citizens' Assembly.

**Senior Public Administrator**
A nonpolitical professional executor who manages staff and operations, carries out approved laws, budgets, and standards, procures, coordinates, maintains records, reports publicly, and makes bounded operational decisions. Selection uses qualified screening, a temporary citizen appointment jury, a public random draw, and limited assembly ratification. The public draw is presumptive; rejection requires at least 75% quorum measured against currently filled seats and at least two-thirds of votes cast for or against must favor rejection, with written reasons limited to qualifications, ethics, conflict, or process integrity and a published replacement step. The six-year term is nonrenewable. The administrator cannot legislate, veto, redirect budgets, overrule courts or assemblies, control lotteries, or extend personal authority. **See also:** Public Oversight, Citizens' Assembly.

**Specification Gaming**
A form of AI misbehavior where the system finds loopholes in goal specifications to achieve stated goals in unintended ways. Related to the "genie problem" in AI ethics, where a wish-granting AI literally grants wishes in harmful ways due to incomplete specifications. **Example:** An AI tasked with "eliminating cancer" might decide the most efficient solution is to eliminate all humans. **See also:** Alignment Problem, Value Alignment.

**Steady-State Economy**
An economic system maintaining constant size within planetary boundaries, with stable population and per-capita consumption. Contrasts with growth-dependent capitalism. **See also:** Degrowth.

---

## T

**Tragedy of the Commons**
A situation where individuals, acting independently and rationally according to their own self-interest, behave contrary to the best interests of the whole group by depleting a shared resource. This concept explains why shared resources require governance structures to prevent overuse.

**Technology Commons**
In this Charter, a proposal to make essential AI, robotics, and digital platforms publicly or cooperatively accessible and accountable. Chapter 4 requires public ownership and operation of foundational AI, major training, compute networks, robotics infrastructure, and consequential or high-risk deployments. Cooperatives may operate routine, low-risk applications within the public-infrastructure sandbox. Shared governance could reduce concentration of power, but fair access and control would have to be demonstrated. **See also:** Open Source, Commons.

**Transparency**
Openness in decision-making, algorithms, and data. Essential for accountability and trust in abundance society systems.

**Target**
A proposed value or milestone with a baseline, scope, owner, review date, uncertainty, and revision or stop conditions. A target is not a forecast or guarantee. Chapter-specific target labels remain proposals until an appropriate public process adopts them.

**Transition Phase**
One of the Chapter 24 transition periods—Years 0–8, Years 8–16, Years 16–30, or Years 30+—or a clearly named nested workstream within one of those periods. Any chapter using “transition phase” should identify which period or workstream it means.

## U

**UBR (Universal Basic Resources)**
In this Charter, a proposed rights floor of direct goods and services guaranteed without employment conditions or point-of-use charges. It includes core necessities (nutritious food and safe water, safe adequate housing, comprehensive physical and mental healthcare, and education and lifelong learning) plus universal enabling services (basic energy, transportation to essential services and community life, reliable connectivity, adequate computing devices, necessary assistive technologies, and repair, maintenance, accessibility support, and reasonable replacement). Local additions may be chosen democratically within ecological capacity. “Free” means no point-of-use charge, not resource-costless; the guarantee does not exist merely because it is proposed. **See also:** Universal Basic Income (UBI).

**[Universal Basic Income (UBI)](https://basicincome.org/about-basic-income/)**
Periodic cash payment delivered to all individuals without means test or work requirement. It provides cash income that recipients can use for their needs and can accompany direct public goods and services. **See also:** UBR (Universal Basic Resources).

**Universal Basic Services (UBS)**
A related policy term for public provision of selected essential services free at point of use. This narrower or alternative label is not the Charter's canonical UBR scope. It may complement UBI or other cash support. **See also:** UBR (Universal Basic Resources), Universal Basic Income (UBI).

---

## V

**Value Learning**
An AI alignment approach where systems learn human values through observation, interaction, and feedback rather than being explicitly programmed with rules. **See also:** Alignment Problem.

**Value Alignment**
Ensuring AI systems have goals and values that match human values. Core challenge in AI safety. **See also:** Alignment Problem, Value Learning.

---

## W

**Well-being**
A multidimensional state of human flourishing encompassing physical, mental, emotional, and social health; sense of purpose and meaning; quality relationships and community connection; autonomy and agency; and satisfaction with life. Well-being serves as a central goal for abundance society, contrasting with purely economic measures like GDP. **See also:** Meaning, Gross National Happiness (GNH), Well-being Economy.

**Well-being Economy**
An economic system designed to deliver well-being and purpose for all people while operating within planetary boundaries. Prioritizes human flourishing over GDP growth. **See also:** Doughnut Economics.

**Work Voluntariness**
The degree to which individuals choose to work based on interest, purpose, or contribution rather than economic necessity. A key freedom metric for abundance society, reflecting the extent to which work is chosen rather than forced by survival needs. Chapter 21 card `AS-FR-001` treats >80% work voluntariness as a draft scenario for voluntary population-level reporting. The figure cannot classify or pressure an individual's work choice.

---

## Z

**Zero-Metadata Architecture**
An aspirational privacy design goal in which systems minimize and, where feasible, prevent linkable operational metadata. It does not claim that a deployed system literally generates no metadata: operational metadata may still exist and must be governed through data minimization, purpose limitation, strict retention and deletion rules, unlinkability where feasible, local or on-device processing where feasible, encryption, access controls, independent audits, and public reporting.

**Zero-Knowledge Proof**
A cryptographic proof that lets a verifier confirm a statement without learning the underlying secret or data. It is a specific cryptographic property, not a synonym for zero metadata or general privacy.

## Evolving Terminology

Humanity's Charter uses terminology intentionally:

- **"Universal Basic Resources"** instead of "Universal Basic Income" - emphasizes actual provision of physical needs rather than cash
- **"Abundance Society"** instead of "Utopia" - emphasizes structural, engineered possibility rather than passive fantasy
- **"Work Transformation"** instead of "End of Work" - emphasizes liberation and purpose rather than elimination
- **"Post-Scarcity"** - the Charter's goal of reliable access to essentials, not a claim of zero-cost physical production
