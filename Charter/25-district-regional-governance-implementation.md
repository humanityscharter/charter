# Chapter 25: Local & Regional Governance Implementation

*A lawful local playbook for moving from present institutions to sortition-led civic authority*

## Purpose and legal boundary

Chapter 5 defines the destination governance model. This chapter explains how a municipality, district, city-region, or other local or regional jurisdiction can move toward it. It is a civic implementation guide, not legal advice. Home-rule authority, delegation rules, charter amendments, budgeting, ballot procedures, labor law, public records, and court jurisdiction vary by place. Obtain local legal review before promising binding authority, changing a charter, spending public funds, or changing a public employee's duties.

During transition, present-day elected councils, commissions, legislatures, and executives may lawfully authorize pilots, appropriate funds, appoint temporary staff, and place charter or constitutional changes before the voters or other body current law requires. Those institutions are transition mechanisms. They must not be described as the mature Charter model, and a pilot does not itself replace them.

The destination is a standing general citizen assembly selected by an independently administered, publicly auditable stratified civic lottery from the full eligible local population. It decides local laws, budgets, major appointments, and oversight. A temporary issue-specific assembly investigates a major proposal through public and expert testimony and recommends; the standing assembly decides. Constrained professional administrators execute. Experts advise. Independent courts and oversight bodies review.

## Readiness and sequence

The local and regional windows below are nested workstreams under Chapter 24's transition timeline. They are not automatic deadlines or a new transition phase. A jurisdiction may overlap, pause, revise, or stop a window.

**Foundation: lawful pilots and infrastructure**

- legal review
- public records
- petition intake
- advisory deliberation
- accessibility and compensation
- participatory budgeting
- oversight baseline

**Expansion: delegated authority where law permits**

- standing-assembly design
- public lottery audit
- issue assemblies
- appointment-jury pilots
- professional administration
- lane testing

**Institutionalization: valid constitutional or charter replacement**

- rights and judicial protections
- full assembly authority
- administration
- independent oversight
- regional and treaty coordination

Each gate must publish: legal authority; affected communities; inclusion and accessibility plan; selection and conflict rules; cost and ecological impacts; service-continuity plan; evidence and uncertainty; rights and due-process review; sunset or stop conditions; and appeal route. “Success” recommends a gate review; it never transfers authority automatically.

## Canonical measurement crosswalk and ownership

Chapter 21 owns the shared metric definitions, registered IDs, category and status labels, card fields, privacy safeguards, uncertainty, correction and appeal routes, and prohibited-use rules. Chapter 25 retains ownership of lawful local implementation: pilots, local evidence, jurisdiction-specific baselines and legal assumptions, accessibility, testimony, appeals, service continuity, and stop or revision decisions. Chapter 5 retains governance roles, selection and appointment rules, quorum and approval rules, and citizen-assembly authority. Chapter 8 retains rights and due-process authority. Chapter 24 retains the transition timeline and readiness gates.

The local crosswalk is:

These references require a local namespace, baseline, scope, owner, auditor, uncertainty statement, testimony, review or expiry date, appeal route, and adoption record for any jurisdiction-specific measure. Metric warnings inform accountable human review and repair; they do not transfer authority, create a policy veto, advance a Chapter 24 gate, punish a resident, deny a service, or change a Chapter 5 governance threshold. The standing assembly remains selected through an independently administered, publicly auditable stratified civic lottery. Temporary issue-specific assemblies investigate and recommend, while the standing assembly decides. Senior administrators remain subject to independent qualification review, a citizen appointment jury, a public random draw, and standing-assembly ratification or rejection, and execute delegated law without becoming a policy-making layer. Quorum continues to use currently filled seats as its denominator, with seated abstentions counting toward quorum; the existing `75%`, `85%`, and `90%` levels and approval thresholds remain governance rules. Emergency review, expiry, renewal, court access, rights, rotation, dissent, and the source-population-based reselection cooldown remain unchanged. These safeguards remain separate from metric-review cadence.

## Local Rights and Due-Process Operations

Every local pilot and mature local service that exercises covered public power must apply the **Chapter 8 Rights and Due-Process Matrix** as its canonical rights floor. A local rights office provides operational capacity without changing Chapter 5 roles or thresholds. It classifies impact (with uncertainty defaulting to high impact), assists affected people and groups, inspects and audits records and systems, preserves evidence, investigates systemic harm, publishes findings and anonymized corrections, and may apply to a court for an emergency pause or other binding relief. It has no policy veto, cannot make final rights rulings, and cannot create a permanent restriction; courts issue binding rights decisions, while assemblies set policy and administrators execute it.

The local office and service operators must provide accessible notice and reasons, translation, disability and communication support, publicly funded counsel or an independent advocate as the matrix requires, and immediate access to an independent court. Each covered action has a complete record available to the affected person subject only to narrow, court-approved redaction; public reporting protects identifiers while showing governing law, criteria, aggregate outcomes, errors, reversals, and audit results. Individual and collective standing are preserved, and group assistance never displaces an individual's independent review.

The local public remedy fund pays restoration and compensation promptly, without waiting for agency or budget disputes, and recovers from responsible agencies, contractors, or insurers. Remedies restore rights, access, status, property, and essential services; correct or delete records; notify downstream recipients; reimburse losses and legal costs; compensate material and dignitary harm; publish an anonymized correction; and require systemic reform where warranted. Deliberate abuse, concealment, or corruption may lead to personal discipline or liability only after due process.

After lawful adoption of the implemented matrix, local operators review existing restrictions on the Chapter 24 schedule: detention or containment within 7 days; travel restrictions, substantial asset freezes, or essential-service deprivation within 30 days; intrusive monitoring or high-impact AI decisions within 90 days; and all other covered actions within one year. Missed review causes expiry and cannot grandfather or retroactively ratify the old action. A later restriction requires fresh lawful authorization and current evidence. Existing emergency actions follow the shortest applicable deadline, and essential services remain protected while a gate is revised, paused, or replaced.

## Foundation: what can be done under present law

### Start with what is already lawful

Identify which public meetings, advisory committees, participatory budgeting, planning hearings, local initiatives, public-records processes, ombuds services, and jury administration are already available locally. These are useful transition infrastructure, but their legal powers differ. Identify and record the exact enabling statute, charter section, budget authority, delegation rule, labor agreement, records duty, and judicial review route before describing a pilot as binding.

An existing body may authorize an advisory assembly or deliberative process. It should not promise that an advisory recommendation will automatically become law. Its written response should say which recommendations it accepts, changes, or declines and why. If the body wants binding authority, it must identify the ordinance, charter, constitutional amendment, or other lawful route that can provide it.

### Citizen advisory and issue assemblies

An advisory or issue assembly is a practical first test of lottery administration, evidence quality, compensation, facilitation, accessibility, public records, and response quality. Use a defined question rather than an invitation to redesign every institution at once. Recruit from the full eligible population through the independent lottery, not through the organizer's mailing list. Publish the invitation, response rate, stratification, replacements, and exemptions.

The authorizing instrument should include a neutral facilitator, an independent evidence review, balanced witness rules, public and private session boundaries, conflict disclosures, a code of conduct, a process for requesting additional evidence, and a written response deadline. A pilot can be small in scope without being casual about rights or inclusion.

### Participatory budgeting

Participatory budgeting can test whether residents can deliberate over real tradeoffs. A lawful transition institution may dedicate a defined portion of a parks, housing, transport, or other budget. State whether residents propose projects, rank them, or make a binding allocation under existing law. Protect core services, disclose maintenance and lifecycle costs, and publish rejected proposals with reasons.

Existing examples vary by place and scale. New York City describes participatory budgeting as a process in which community members directly decide how to spend part of a public budget.[^ch25-pb-nyc] Paris reported residents selecting 121 projects totaling nearly €83 million in its 2024 cycle.[^ch25-pb-paris] A Portland, Oregon, proposal on the November 2026 ballot would dedicate an amount equivalent to at least 2% of adopted General Fund discretionary ongoing expenses to participatory budgeting. It is a proposal, not an enacted allocation.[^ch25-pb-portland] These examples show possible process designs, not proof that every jurisdiction has authority or that a particular budget size is transferable.

### Deliberative polling and evidence pilots

Deliberative polling can be useful when a jurisdiction first needs evidence about whether residents' views change after balanced information and facilitated discussion. It is not the same as a standing assembly and does not create binding authority. Measure the before-and-after sample, attrition, information quality, subgroup participation, and whether the responsible authority used the findings.

Universities, libraries, civic organizations, and public agencies can support evidence and facilitation, but funders must disclose conflicts and cannot select the participants or edit the public findings. Expert councils remain advisory and must publish competing interpretations.

## Expansion: the local destination architecture

When a foundation pilot meets a Chapter 24 readiness gate, a jurisdiction may seek a lawful charter or constitutional change establishing this standing assembly. The proposal should specify jurisdiction, lane thresholds, filled-seat quorum, terms, compensation, lottery administrator, stratification, cooldown, no-consecutive-service rule, deferrals, removal, ethics tribunal, citizen adjudication panel, records, appeals, emergency review, administration, and judicial rights review. A charter amendment should identify which policy powers move, when they move, and what service obligations continue; it must not silently transfer enforcement, procurement, judicial, or budget powers it does not describe.

### Standing general citizen assembly

When valid law authorizes it, establish the standing local assembly as the general decision body. Its mandate covers ordinary laws and budgets, major structural policy, major appointments, and oversight responses. A standing assembly may create temporary issue-specific assemblies, but it cannot delegate away its final policy decision on a major proposal.

The lottery is direct, stratified, independently administered, and publicly auditable. Stratification may include geography, age, gender, income, disability, and other locally relevant factors. It shapes the pool but cannot allow an official to pick a participant. Service is a presumptive civic duty like jury duty, with full compensation, employment protection, accessibility and caregiving support, training, translation, and neutral health, disability, hardship, safety, and conflict deferrals.

Terms are 18 months and staggered. No consecutive service or upward political career ladder. Cooldown at the same local layer is three years when fewer than 20 eligible people exist per seat, six years at 20–100, and 12 years above 100. Removal is limited to corruption, concealed conflicts, serious misconduct, incapacity, persistent nonattendance, or unlawful disclosure. An independent ethics tribunal investigates and a separately selected citizen adjudication panel decides after due process. Political disagreement is never a removal ground.

### Constrained professional administration

Use professional public administration rather than an administrative assembly. A senior local administrator manages staff and operations, executes approved laws, budgets, and standards, handles procurement and coordination, maintains records, reports publicly, and makes bounded operational decisions. The administrator cannot enact or veto laws; change rights or selection rules; create or materially redirect budgets; overrule the assembly or courts; extend authority or emergency powers; control lotteries; or conceal decisions or conflicts.

The proposed hybrid selection process works as follows: an independent civil-service office verifies qualifications, ethics, and conflicts; a temporary citizen appointment jury uses a public rubric and structured interviews to identify 3–5 equally qualified finalists without ranking; a public random draw chooses one; and the standing assembly ratifies or rejects the draw. Rejection requires at least 75% quorum measured against currently filled seats (vacancies are excluded from the denominator and seated abstentions count toward quorum) and at least two-thirds of votes cast for or against must favor rejection. Written reasons are limited to qualifications, ethics, conflict, or process integrity, and the published replacement draw or next lawful step must follow a rejection. The term is six years and nonrenewable, with annual public performance reporting, an independent midterm review, and defined-cause or no-confidence removal with due process. Ordinary rank-and-file civil service remains merit-based under transparent rules. This special lottery-and-ratification process governs senior administrators; the ordinary appointment lane does not replace it.

Existing public employees are not a political obstacle to be bypassed. During transition, they can explain systems, maintain services, identify operational risks, and carry out lawful pilot decisions. Their participation must not let an agency choose the citizen assembly, suppress records, or turn implementation advice into policy.

### Agenda and decision lanes

Residents by petition, assembly members, issue assemblies, administrators, expert councils, and lower-level assemblies may propose. A neutral agenda office applies the Chapter 5 intake checks without screening political content (see ‘Governance Authority Matrix,’ ‘Decision lanes’).

| Lane | Quorum against filled seats | Approval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ordinary local laws, budgets, appointments, oversight | 75% of currently filled seats | More than 50% of votes cast for or against |
| Major structural local policy | 85% of currently filled seats | 60% of votes cast for or against |
| Emergency-power renewal | 90% of currently filled seats | Two-thirds of votes cast for or against |
| Local pilot | Lane matching its scope | Same threshold, plus sunset, evaluation, rights review, and appeal |

Quorum is a percentage of currently filled seats. Vacant seats are excluded from the denominator and cannot count as participation. Approved remote and accessibility participation counts. Abstentions by seated members count toward quorum but not affirmative votes. Ordinary policy referendums and political recalls are not destination-model routes. Direct referendums are reserved for constitutional ratification.

## Regional coordination

Cross-local issues such as watersheds, housing, transport, energy, and public health may require a regional standing assembly or, during transition, an inter-local agreement. In a transitional inter-local agreement, each participating local assembly may instruct a rotating randomly selected delegation; that delegation has no independent policy mandate. The mature regional standing assembly is itself directly selected through a regional stratified civic lottery, not through upward delegation. Regional bodies remain subject to rights, ecological limits, independent judicial review, and the same transparent lottery, compensation, accessibility, conflict, and appeal rules.

Regional coordination may set interoperability standards, pool resources, and address effects that cross boundaries. It may not silently convert a service standard into a new policy power, or let a professional administrator become a regional legislator.

## Emergencies at local level

Professional incident specialists may take immediate, narrowly defined life-safety actions under preapproved protocols. A rotating emergency committee drawn from the standing local assembly reviews within 24–48 hours; the full assembly reviews within seven days; courts remain immediately available. Extraordinary powers expire after 30 days. Renewal requires 90% quorum of currently filled seats, two-thirds approval by votes cast for or against, and a new public justification. Powers cannot permanently change laws, suspend core rights, delay lottery rotation, suppress lawful dissent, or make themselves permanent. Publish decision, evidence, expenditure, and conflict records and complete an independent after-action audit.

## Lawful transition playbook

| Step | Transition action | Destination safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Map authority | Identify current elected bodies, agencies, courts, public employees, and legal limits. | Label every present institution as current or transitional. |
| 2. Publish a proposal | File a complete petition or pilot proposal with cost, rights, ecological, and accessibility materials. | Neutral agenda office cannot reject political content. |
| 3. Test deliberation | Run a compensated, stratified advisory or issue assembly with independent lottery administration. | Publish the draw, evidence, conflicts, dissent, and response. |
| 4. Test execution | Use existing professional staff under a written delegation and independent review. | Do not create an administrative assembly or grant staff policy power. |
| 5. Review readiness | Apply Chapter 24 go, revise, pause, or stop gates. | Evidence does not automatically transfer authority. |
| 6. Change the charter | Use the legally required amendment or constitutional process. | Apply the constitutional lane, independent rights review, and required referendum. |
| 7. Institutionalize | Establish the local assembly, administration, oversight, courts, and records. | Audit the lottery and appointments; maintain service continuity and appeals. |

## Concerns, opposition, and legal transition

### The reality of opposition

Any meaningful transfer of authority will face objections. Some are principled, such as concerns about competence, minority rights, continuity, cost, or legal limits. Others may reflect the loss of a current office, contract, or informal influence. Treat both as information, but do not promise existing officeholders a permanent role in the destination model.

### Legal challenges

If opponents argue that a local institution lacks authority, obtain a written legal opinion and identify a narrower lawful pilot or the required state, national, or constitutional route. Do not assume home rule applies everywhere. Preserve records, publish the authorizing text, provide standing and appeal information, and pause any power a court enjoins. A legal challenge is not evidence that the model is invalid everywhere, nor is a favorable ruling proof that every future expansion is lawful.

### Funding cuts and capacity constraints

Protect pilot funds through a transparent appropriation, a clear cost boundary, and a continuity plan. Grants or philanthropy may fund evaluation or accessibility during transition, but should not buy selection influence or control the public record. Crowdfunding can support independent civic education, but it cannot substitute for public compensation or stable administration. A pilot should state what happens if funding ends before the decision or service is complete.

### Political opposition

During transition, elected officials may question or oppose a proposal because they believe it threatens accountability, or may be unable to adopt it because their institution lacks the necessary authority. Engage them as lawful transition actors, provide precise legal and service information, and make the public route clear. Do not frame the mature model as keeping elected officials in office under a new label. The goal is to establish citizen authority through valid public processes, not to win a personality contest.

### Media narratives and misinformation

Claims that citizens cannot understand complex questions should be answered with the public evidence record, not with unverified success stories. Publish the agenda, briefings, witness selection, uncertainties, dissent, costs, and implementation outcomes. Do not use one assembly's recommendation as proof that all assemblies will agree or that every policy outcome will improve.

### The “too radical” narrative

Use plain language: residents serve like jurors, experts advise, professionals execute, courts protect rights, and the public can see the record. Explain the transition sequence and existing legal precedents without claiming that an advisory committee is already a constitutional replacement. Emphasize reversibility, stop conditions, service continuity, and appeal routes.

## Coalition building

Local implementation needs more than a democracy organization. Potential partners include labor and worker organizations, tenant and neighborhood groups, disability advocates, rural and Indigenous communities where applicable, youth and older-adult organizations, faith communities, public-interest technologists, researchers, civil-service associations, small businesses, environmental groups, and public defenders or legal-aid organizations. Each has different reasons to care: voice, service quality, dignity, evidence, accessibility, ecological protection, or legal accountability.

Recruitment should not be confused with selection. A coalition can advocate for a process; it cannot choose the assembly. Organizers should publish funding, conflicts, paid roles, and moderation rules. The coalition should preserve minority positions and unresolved questions rather than claiming unanimous support.

Current officials, public employees, and moderate residents should receive accurate transition information. They are not automatically opponents, but neither are they entitled to a permanent governing role. The destination rule remains citizen assemblies plus constrained administration.

## Messaging strategy

Useful messages include:

1. **More voice for ordinary residents:** service is compensated and supported rather than reserved for people with spare time.
2. **Informed decisions:** citizens hear evidence and affected communities before voting on structural choices.
3. **Competent execution:** professional administrators keep services running without becoming lawmakers.
4. **Rights that remain enforceable:** courts can invalidate unlawful action, and independent oversight can investigate it.
5. **Local control with coordination:** the lowest effective level decides, while regional or treaty bodies handle spillovers.
6. **A lawful path:** pilots, charter changes, and constitutional changes follow the authority current law provides.

Avoid messages that promise instant transformation, perfect representation, universal consensus, automatic savings, or a guaranteed Year 30 endpoint. Avoid portraying elections, current officials, or present-day agencies as inherently illegitimate; the transition must be lawful and factually grounded.

## Chen family and local implementation

This is one possible local implementation sequence. Year 1 and Year 2 are scenario-local labels, not Chapter 24's transition timeline years.

In Year 1, David attends a public session about a compensated housing issue assembly. An independent lottery selects members from the full eligible local population, including rural residents, renters, disabled residents, caregivers, and people who do not usually attend meetings. The authorizing council remains a current transition institution and publishes its legal authority.

In Year 2, the issue assembly hears residents, housing providers, builders, accessibility advocates, and environmental experts. Its report recommends a limited pilot with a cost boundary, a sunset, and an evaluation plan. The council accepts part of the recommendation and explains the rest in writing. Maria serves on the oversight panel that checks whether the public record and compensation were delivered.

After a readiness review, the local authority places a charter amendment before the legally required public process. If validly adopted, a standing citizen assembly takes the destination-model policy role. David may be selected later only if the lottery and cooldown rules allow it; he cannot nominate himself or campaign for a seat. A professional administrator is selected through the qualified-finalist, citizen-jury, random-draw, limited-ratification process and manages implementation. If a court finds a rights violation, the local jurisdiction revises the policy or follows appeal; it cannot simply vote the judgment away.

## Implementation plan and protections

### Foundation window within Years 0–8

- Map legal authority, current agencies, service dependencies, labor obligations, and courts.
- Establish an independent lottery and public-records baseline.
- Run one or more compensated advisory or issue assemblies.
- Test accessibility, caregiving, protected leave, translation, remote participation, and deferral rules.
- Pilot participatory budgeting with a defined and safe budget share.
- Publish evidence, conflicts, dissent, outcomes, and a written response.

### Expansion window within Years 8–16

- Seek valid charter or constitutional authority for standing assemblies where readiness gates pass.
- Test ordinary and major-policy decision lanes with filled-seat quorum.
- Establish appointment juries and professional administration.
- Build independent inspector, auditor, ethics, records, and appeal capacity.
- Coordinate with neighboring jurisdictions on cross-boundary needs.

### Global Floor work within Years 16–30

- Participate in regional, national, or treaty-level arrangements only where consent and legal authority exist.
- Use instructed delegations and direct global sortition for the Peoples' Assembly.
- Preserve withdrawal, tribunal review, resident safeguards, and essential services.
- Expand only when evidence, rights, ecological, and administrative capacity gates pass.

Five protections should accompany every window:

1. **Build broad support without capture:** disclose coalition funding and preserve dissent.
2. **Demonstrate success without overclaiming:** publish baselines, uncertainty, and failures.
3. **Maintain legal compliance:** obtain local review and respect injunctions and appeals.
4. **Keep services running:** never make a governance experiment a reason to interrupt essential services.
5. **Protect minority rights:** use the constitutional floor, independent courts, accessibility, and due process rather than an assembly veto.

## Handling failure and setbacks

### Low participation or skewed selection

Publish response and replacement rates, examine which groups declined or could not serve, and provide better compensation, safety, access, and caregiving support. If the panel is not credible, pause the decision rather than treating a skewed sample as representative.

### Legal injunction or invalid authority

Stop the affected power, preserve records, communicate the service impact, and use appeal or a narrower lawful design. Do not move the same authority to a contractor or advisory label to evade the ruling.

### Political pushback or administrative noncompliance

Use public reasons, oversight investigations, and lawful court remedies. Build coalition support, but do not use political recall as a substitute for defined-cause due process. If an administrator exceeds delegated authority, the independent ethics or judicial route applies.

### Implementation failure

Publish missed milestones, cost overruns, service effects, and corrective options. A standing assembly may revise, pause, or stop a pilot under the readiness gate. A recommendation being ignored is a governance result to document, not evidence that citizens lack capacity.

### Lessons from precedents

Ireland's Citizens' Assembly archive shows how randomly selected members can study constitutional and climate questions, but its recommendations were advisory and its later public referendum was a separate process.[^ch25-ireland] France's Citizens' Convention on Climate shows an extended deliberative process, but proposal submission must be distinguished from enactment.[^ch25-france] Oregon's Citizens' Initiative Review demonstrates a statutory citizen-panel model. Oregon's 2026 State Voters' Pamphlet Manual reports that the commission has been on hiatus since 2018.[^ch25-oregon] OECD's comparative work documents many representative deliberative processes but also cautions that implementation evidence is limited.[^ch25-oecd]

## Budget considerations

The categories below identify costs to include when planning a local budget. A local budget must state geography, price year, staffing, member count, meeting frequency, accessibility, caregiving, translation, technology, records, legal review, evaluation, audit, and contingency assumptions. Do not treat a sample stipend or a participatory-budget allocation as the total cost of governance.

| Cost category | Foundation pilot | Expansion and standing system |
|---|---|---|

| Member compensation and support | Stipends or wages, protected leave, access, care, travel | Recurring compensation, employment protection, translation, and accessibility |
| Facilitation and records | Issue assembly facilitation, evidence, transcripts, public portal | Permanent deliberation, agenda, archives, and public information capacity |

| Independent oversight | Pilot audit and ethics review | Inspectors general, auditor, ethics office, records, and appeals |
| Evaluation | Baseline, outcomes, uncertainty, and stop review | Continuous performance, rights, equity, ecological, and service review |

Funding may come from lawful public appropriations, grants, or philanthropy for bounded evaluation and access. No funder may select participants, suppress findings, or purchase agenda priority. Public budgets should show ongoing operating costs separately from one-time pilot costs.

## Demographic and geographic considerations

### Urban jurisdictions

Urban systems may need neighborhood-level recruitment, multilingual outreach, accessible transit, safe evening schedules, and careful coordination across dense service networks. A large population can make the lottery pool robust while making accessibility and subgroup contact more complex.

### Suburban jurisdictions

Suburban jurisdictions may need cross-municipal agreements, attention to renters and unincorporated areas, and accessible remote participation. Do not let homeowner associations or high-response neighborhoods become a substitute for the full eligible population.

### Rural jurisdictions

Rural areas may face long travel distances, smaller eligible populations, limited facilitation capacity, broadband gaps, and higher confidentiality risks. Use regional meeting sites, mileage and caregiving support, secure remote participation, and transparent rules for when a small population changes the cooldown ratio. Combining jurisdictions requires consent and a clear mandate.

### Small towns and districts

Small communities may know one another personally, which can increase trust but also raise conflict and safety concerns. Independent selection, confidential deferral, conflict disclosure, and a citizen adjudication panel matter especially where a member may have a direct relationship with a local institution.

Stratification should include geography, age, gender, income, disability, language, and other locally relevant factors. It should improve access, not permit officials to hand-pick favored residents.

## Putting the local strategy together

The local sequence is:

1. Map current authority and services.
2. Form a coalition that discloses funding and preserves dissent.
3. Publish a legally reviewed, fully costed pilot.
4. Select participants through an independent, auditable lottery.
5. Compensate and support service, including access and caregiving.
6. Hear affected residents and competing expert evidence.
7. Publish the issue assembly's recommendation and the current institution's written response.
8. Evaluate rights, inclusion, cost, service, ecological, and implementation outcomes.
9. Apply Chapter 24's GO, REVISE, PAUSE, or STOP/REPLACE gate.
10. Use the valid charter or constitutional route before transferring binding authority.

Establish a credible path from lawful transition work to a constitutional destination in which citizens decide, professionals execute, experts advise, and independent review remains real. Do not try to replace every institution in one announcement.

Local governance puts rules about authority into daily practice. A credible local program gives residents a fair chance to serve, professionals clear limits, experts a useful but nonbinding role, courts a real rights mandate, and the public enough records to decide whether to continue. It treats every pilot as a proposal under review until a lawful public process makes it part of the governing system.
