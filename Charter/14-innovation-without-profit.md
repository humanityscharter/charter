# Chapter 14: Innovation Beyond Profit

*How creativity and progress flourish when driven by purpose rather than value capture*

---

## Introduction: Profit Is Not the Only Reason People Innovate

In 1955, journalist Edward R. Murrow asked Jonas Salk who owned the patent to the polio vaccine. Salk answered: *"Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?"*[^ch14-salk-interview]

[^ch14-salk-interview]: A transcript of the April 12, 1955 CBS interview records Murrow's question and Salk's answer. The exchange documents Salk's public statement; it does not by itself settle the vaccine's patent history or the role of funding and manufacturing partners: https://retroreport.org/transcript/transcript-could-you-patent-the-sun/

One common argument says that without the chance to become very wealthy or the fear of poverty, people would stop inventing and progress would stall.

Economic history gives us many examples of major breakthroughs built through public funding and open collaboration.

Many foundational breakthroughs drew on public research and shared work. Internet protocols (TCP/IP), GPS, the Human Genome Project, mRNA vaccine platforms[^ch14-mrna-nidcr], and Linux each have different histories and mixes of public, private, and volunteer contributions. Funding can be traced more reliably than the private motives of every person involved.

The Charter's criticism concerns specific investment practices. Too much venture capital directs talent toward rent extraction, closed ownership, ad-click optimization, and financial products. Profit can fund useful development. It can also turn shared discovery into a private asset.

When society guarantees material security and treats scientific knowledge as something we share, innovation need not disappear. Public research and shared knowledge could give more work room to focus on clean energy storage, ecological repair, material recycling, and ending disease rather than quarterly earnings.

Public research, open licensing, challenge prizes, grants, recognition, cooperative enterprise, and carefully regulated private investment are evaluated incentive mechanisms. They may complement one another where compatible, but none is automatically required in every field and no list here is a cumulative mandate. Their use should be tested against safety, access, public purpose, worker and community rights, and measurable innovation outcomes, with democratic power to revise or stop mechanisms that fail.

## The Profit Myth

### The Common Assumption

The belief that "without profit, no innovation" is deeply embedded in our economic thinking. The logic seems straightforward:

- Profit provides the incentive to innovate
- Competition drives continuous improvement
- Market signals guide innovation toward what people value
- Without profit, there's no motivation to take risks or create something new

This assumption shapes economic policy, business strategy, innovation funding, intellectual property rights, venture capital, and the structure of corporate research and development.

**But is this assumption true?**

### Evidence Against the Profit Myth

The historical record tells a different story.

**Open Source Software**

Open-source software is a striking example of shared development. Consider these projects:

| Project | Economic Value | How it is organized |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| **Linux** | Server, Android, and cloud infrastructure | Open-source project with company contributions[^ch14-linux-kernel-report] |
| **Wikipedia** | Global knowledge resource | The nonprofit Wikimedia Foundation hosts and protects the site; volunteer editors create and curate its content.[^ch14-wikimedia-roles] |
| **Firefox** | Open-source web browsing | The nonprofit Mozilla Foundation oversees governance; its wholly owned taxable Mozilla Corporation develops Firefox with the community.[^ch14-mozilla-roles] |
| **Python, JavaScript** | Web applications, scientific computing, and other software | The Python Software Foundation produces core Python and manages contributions; Ecma International's TC39 maintains JavaScript's definition with community contributions.[^ch14-python-js-roles][^ch14-python-js-uses] |
| **Cryptocurrency protocols** | Enabled decentralized finance and blockchain technology | Open source development |

These projects created enormous value through shared work. Linux supports web servers and cloud deployments, and provides Android's kernel.[^ch14-linux-webserver-example][^ch14-android-linux-platform] Wikipedia offers a free encyclopedia written and maintained by volunteers around the world.[^ch14-wikimedia-roles] Python is used in web development, scientific computing, and other software; JavaScript is used in web pages and server-side applications.[^ch14-python-js-uses] Community, curiosity, and shared purpose can motivate people to contribute to this work.[^ch14-linux-kernel-report]

A 2024 study estimated that companies would spend about $8.8 trillion if each firm rebuilt the widely used open-source software it uses.[^ch14-oss-value-2024]

**Government and Academic Research**

Public research helped develop major technologies:

| Discovery | Research and development | Impact |
|-----------|----------------|--------|
| **Internet (TCP/IP)** | ARPA-supported research and contracted engineering[^ch14-darpa-tcpip] | Global communication revolution |
| **GPS** | U.S. government-provided service; commercial applications[^ch14-gps-context] | Navigation, timing, location services |
| **Touchscreen** | CERN capacitive-touchscreen work published openly; industrial production followed[^ch14-cern-touchscreen] | Modern smartphones and devices |
| **MRI Technology** | Mixed (academic research + commercial)[^ch14-mri-funding-context] | Medical imaging breakthrough |
| **Human Genome Project** | U.S. public funding from DOE and NIH; charitable support from the Wellcome Trust; Celera's separate private sequencing effort[^ch14-hgp-funding][^ch14-hgp-2000-announcement] | Medical and biological revolution |

Government and academic research helped develop the internet, GPS, touchscreen technologies, and MRI.[^ch14-mri-funding-context] Public investment in knowledge and capability helped lay the foundations for these technologies. They spawned entire industries and transformed daily life.

**Hobbyist and Maker Innovation**

People innovate for fun, curiosity, and community recognition:

| Innovation | Origin | Participation |
|------------|--------|---------------|
| **Amateur Radio** | Ham radio operators | Radio experimentation[^ch14-arrl-radio] |
| **Homebrew Computing** | Hobbyists (1970s) | Computer experimentation |
| **Maker Movement** | Makers and creators | Varies by project - some commercial applications, some pure hobbyist |
| **Citizen Science** | Volunteers | Volunteer research[^ch14-noaa-citizen-science] |
| **Open Hardware** | Community builders | Publicly shared designs; commercial use allowed[^ch14-oshwa-definition] |

In the 1970s, the Homebrew Computer Club brought hobbyists together to experiment with computers. Its first meeting was in a Menlo Park garage on March 5, 1975. Members shared hardware and software and displayed their projects at later meetings. Club participant and Computer History Museum founding chairman Len Shustek recalls Steve Wozniak showing the Apple I and describes the club's open sharing as helping speed the personal-computer revolution.[^ch14-homebrew-history]

Maker communities work across 3D printing, robotics, and open hardware. A study of an open-source 3D-printer community documents user contributions to printer innovation; Arduino's Include Robot is an open-source 3D-printed teaching robot. Some projects explore sustainable technology: the RepRapable Recyclebot is an open design for making 3D-printer filament from waste plastic.[^ch14-open-printer-community][^ch14-arduino-robot][^ch14-recyclebot]

Amateur-radio operators have developed communication systems including packet radio, AMTOR, and APRS.[^ch14-amateur-radio-technology]

These activities give people ways to pursue curiosity, develop skills, and build communities.

### Why Profit Isn't Necessary

If profit isn't required for innovation, what is? Evidence and examples identify several motivations that can support innovation, but their strength varies by person, field, institution, and context:

| Motivation | How It Drives Innovation |
|------------|-------------------------|
| **Curiosity** | Natural drive to understand and explore the unknown |
| **Recognition** | Status and respect from peers and community |
| **Purpose** | Solving meaningful problems that matter |
| **Mastery** | Intrinsic satisfaction of developing skills |
| **Community** | Contributing to shared goals and coordinated progress |
| **Play** | Innovation as enjoyment and creative expression |
| **Legacy** | Creating something that outlasts oneself |
| **Altruism** | Desire to help others and contribute to society |

These motivations can support innovation without profit in some settings. They are not universal substitutes for every incentive, and their effects should be tested alongside public-interest mechanisms rather than assumed.

## The Innovation Ecosystem in Abundance

### What Abundance Enables

When survival doesn't depend on labor, when basic needs are guaranteed, and when people have time and resources to explore, innovation potential could expand substantially; the scale and direction would still depend on governance, participation, and evidence.

Abundance provides:

- **Time** - Not everyone needs to work for survival, freeing time and mental capacity for exploration
- **Resources** - Tools, materials, and infrastructure are accessible to all
- **Freedom** - The ability to pursue curiosity without immediate ROI pressure
- **Risk tolerance** - Failure doesn't mean losing housing or food
- **Diversity** - More people from different backgrounds can participate

This could create conditions where innovation flourishes more broadly and deeply than under scarcity, subject to testing and revision.

### The Innovation Ecosystem

An abundance society would support innovation through multiple interconnected systems:

| Category | Elements |
|----------|----------|
| **Motivations** | Curiosity and exploration, Recognition and status, Purpose and meaning, Mastery and skill development, Community and collaboration, Play and enjoyment |
| **Resources** | Time (Universal Basic Resources provides freedom), Tools and materials, Computing and infrastructure, Knowledge and education, Collaboration networks |
| **Incentives** | Recognition systems, Grants and funding, Community appreciation, Impact and contribution metrics, Legacy and reputation |
| **Infrastructure** | Open platforms, Maker spaces and labs, Research facilities, Collaboration tools, Distribution channels |
| **Culture** | Celebrating innovation, Sharing knowledge, Learning from failure, Collaboration over competition, Long-term thinking |

Each of these elements works together to create an environment where innovation thrives without profit motive.

## What Drives Innovation in Abundance

### 1. Curiosity-Driven Innovation

**What It Is:**

Curiosity-driven innovation is pure exploration and discovery. It's the "what if" and "why not" impulse that leads humans to explore the unknown without requiring immediate application or return on investment.

**Examples:**

- Space exploration and astronomy
- Fundamental science (physics, mathematics, biology)
- Artistic creation and experimentation
- Philosophical inquiry and theory
- Wilderness exploration and discovery

**Why It Works:**

Humans are naturally curious. We want to understand how things work, to explore the unknown, to push boundaries. This drive exists independently of profit motive. In fact, curiosity is often strongest when there's no pressure for immediate results.

Abundance provides something crucial for curiosity-driven innovation: **time**. When people don't need to work for survival, they can pursue questions that interest them. They can spend years on fundamental research without worrying about funding or commercialization. They can follow dead ends without fear of catastrophic failure.

**The Chen Family Example:**

David, the father in our Chen family, spends part of his time on a personal research project: studying patterns of social cooperation in online communities. He's not doing this for profit or career advancement. He's curious about how humans organize themselves, and he has the time and resources to explore this question deeply. His findings are shared openly, contributing to shared knowledge.

### 2. Purpose-Driven Innovation

**What It Is:**

Purpose-driven innovation focuses on solving meaningful problems and contributing to community well-being. It's motivated by the desire to make a positive impact rather than to extract value.

**Examples:**

- Climate change solutions (carbon capture, renewable energy)
- Medical breakthroughs (disease cures, regenerative medicine)
- Educational tools (learning platforms, accessibility technology)
- Social infrastructure (community systems, governance tools)

**Why It Works:**

Purpose is a powerful motivator. Meaningful work is associated with job satisfaction and engagement.[^ch14-meaningful-work-2019] Ryan and Deci's 2000 review links self-directed motivation with greater persistence.[^ch14-sdt-2000] Across three studies, Grant and Berry found that wanting to help others strengthened the link between intrinsic motivation and creativity.[^ch14-prosocial-creativity-2011] The Charter aims to direct innovation toward genuine social needs and give people reason to stay with hard problems.

In abundance society, purpose-driven innovation is supported through:

- **Public funding** - Resources allocated to important problems
- **Community recognition** - Status for meaningful contributions
- **Collaborative networks** - Shared effort on large challenges
- **Impact measurement** - Tracking real-world benefits

### 3. Community-Driven Innovation

**What It Is:**

Community-driven innovation is collaborative problem-solving through open source development, citizen science, and shared knowledge. It uses coordinated intelligence and distributed contribution.

**Examples:**

- Open source software (Linux, Python, Apache)
- Citizen science projects (Foldit, Galaxy Zoo classification)[^ch14-foldit-cases][^ch14-galaxy-zoo]
- Community-built projects (open hardware, local solutions)
- Crowdsourced solutions (Wikipedia, open data)

**Why It Works:**

Community-driven innovation harnesses several advantages:

- **Coordinated intelligence** - Many minds solving together
- **Distributed effort** - Work shared across many contributors
- **Network effects** - Each contribution builds on others
- **Community recognition** - Status within the community
- **Lower barriers** - Shared resources reduce individual costs

The success of open source software demonstrates the power of this model. Linux, developed by thousands of contributors worldwide, ran all 500 systems on the November 2017 TOP500 list.[^ch14-top500-linux-2017] Linux also supports web servers.[^ch14-linux-webserver-example] The Linux kernel is the foundation of Android.[^ch14-android-linux-platform] Linux is built through open collaboration with company-backed contributions. Jens Axboe describes enjoyment and challenge in his work; Julia Lawall describes the impact of her contributions and the technical community.[^ch14-linux-kernel-report]

### 4. Mastery-Driven Innovation

**What It Is:**

Mastery-driven innovation is the continuous improvement and refinement of skills. It's the pursuit of excellence for its own sake, the drive to push boundaries and perfect craft.

**Examples:**

- Artistic mastery (painting, music, sculpture)
- Engineering excellence (precision, optimization)
- Scientific refinement (more accurate measurements, better methods)
- Performance optimization (speed, efficiency, elegance)

**Why It Works:**

Developing a skill can be rewarding in itself. Self-determination theory identifies competence, autonomy, and relatedness as basic psychological needs. Competence means feeling capable in what you do, autonomy means acting with a sense of choice, and relatedness means connection with others. Intrinsic motivation—doing an activity for its inherent interest and enjoyment—can be supported by competence-building feedback and meaningful challenges when people also have autonomy.[^ch14-sdt-2000] The Charter aims to give people room for flow (deep absorption), deep engagement, and lasting fulfillment, with growing skill and interest encouraging continued practice.

In abundance society, mastery-driven innovation is supported through:

- **Skill development resources** - Access to education and training
- **Community of practice** - Peers who share and critique work
- **Recognition systems** - Acknowledgment of excellence
- **Showcase platforms** - Opportunities to demonstrate mastery

## Innovation Mechanisms in Abundance

### Open Innovation Platforms

**What:**

Public platforms for collaboration where innovators can share work, find collaborators, and access resources. These are the digital infrastructure of innovation in abundance society.

**Examples:**

- Code repositories (like GitHub, but public infrastructure)
- Research sharing platforms (open access journals, preprint servers)
- Design and hardware repositories (3D printing files, circuit designs)
- Maker communities and forums

**Benefits:**

- **Low barriers to entry** - Anyone can contribute
- **Collaboration across boundaries** - Geographic, social, and disciplinary barriers reduced
- **Faster iteration** - Building on others' work accelerates progress
- **Coordinated improvement** - Each contribution benefits all

**How It Works:**

In abundance society, these platforms are public infrastructure. They're funded through public resources and governed transparently. There are no paywalls, no proprietary restrictions, no value capture from community contributions.

**Governance Structures:**

Open innovation platforms are governed through transparent, community-driven structures that ensure fair operation and prevent abuse:

**1. Platform Stewardship Model**

- **Foundation oversight** - Non-profit foundations (similar to Linux Foundation, Apache Foundation) provide infrastructure
- **Technical steering committees** - Domain experts guide technical direction and standards
- **User citizen assemblies** - Sortition-selected users decide public policy priorities within the platform's mandate; community members may petition and testify
- **Staff support** - Paid coordinators handle administration, moderation, and operations

**2. Decision-Making Processes**

- **Consensus-based decisions** - Major changes require broad community agreement
- **Technical stewardship** - Contributors earn responsibility for technical implementation through demonstrated commitment and quality work, within approved rules and mandates. Sortition-selected user assemblies decide public-policy priorities within the platform's mandate
- **Transparent voting** - All decisions documented publicly with rationale
- **Escalation paths** - Disputes resolved through defined appeals process

**3. Contribution Guidelines**

- **Clear contribution models** - Documented processes for how to contribute code, documentation, bug reports
- **Code of conduct** - Community standards for respectful interaction
- **Quality standards** - Defined criteria for acceptable contributions
- **Licensing requirements** - All contributions must use approved open licenses

**4. Moderation and Conflict Resolution**

- **Community moderation** - Trusted members review and moderate content
- **Appeals process** - Decisions can be challenged through transparent review
- **Conflict mediation** - Trained mediators help resolve disputes
- **Ban procedures** - Clear process for removing harmful actors

**5. Funding and Sustainability**

- **Public funding** - Platforms funded as public infrastructure through community resources
- **Distributed hosting** - Multiple mirrors and hosting providers prevent single points of failure
- **Volunteer coordination** - Community members contribute time and expertise
- **Sponsorship** - Organizations can support platforms without controlling them

**Examples of Existing Models:**

- **Linux Foundation** - Supports Linux, Kubernetes, and hundreds of open source projects.[^ch14-linux-foundation-support]
- **Apache Software Foundation** - Meritocratic governance model for 300+ projects
- **Mozilla Foundation** - Non-profit oversight of Firefox and open web initiatives
- **Wikimedia Foundation** - Hosts and protects Wikipedia and related knowledge projects; volunteer editors create and curate their content.[^ch14-wikimedia-roles]
- **Free Software Foundation** - Advocacy and governance for free software movement

These models demonstrate large-scale, long-term platform governance through community stewardship, transparent processes, and shared purpose.

### Innovation Grants

**What:**

Public funding for promising ideas, allocated through transparent processes with focus on impact and potential rather than profit.

**How Grants Are Allocated:**

The grant allocation process follows a structured, transparent workflow:

1. **Submission** - Innovators submit proposals through public platforms, including:
   - Project description and goals
   - Required resources and timeline
   - Expected outcomes and impact measures
   - Relevant experience and capability

2. **Initial Screening** - Automated and human review checks for:
   - Completeness of application
   - Alignment with grant categories
   - Basic feasibility assessment
   - No safety or ethical red flags

3. **Peer Review** - Multi-stage evaluation process:
   - **Expert review** - Specialists in relevant fields assess technical merit
   - **Community review** - Broader community provides perspective on potential impact
   - **Diversity review** - Ensures varied approaches and perspectives are represented
   - Scores and feedback compiled from multiple reviewers

4. **Allocation Decision** - Based on:
   - Review scores and consensus
   - Available budget for the funding cycle
   - Portfolio diversity (ensuring range of fields, approaches, and applicant backgrounds)
   - Strategic priorities (emerging challenges, underfunded areas)

5. **Funding Release** - Phased disbursement:
   - Initial allocation (30-50% at start)
   - Milestone-based releases (upon achieving agreed deliverables)
   - Final payment (upon completion and reporting)

**Grant Management:**

- **Regular check-ins** - Quarterly progress reports and community updates
- **Milestone verification** - Independent review of claimed achievements
- **Flexibility** - Allow for pivots when learning suggests new directions
- **Support services** - Mentorship, resources, and collaboration matching
- **Transparency** - All proposals, reviews, and decisions publicly accessible

**Types of Grants:**

| Grant Type | Focus | Duration | Typical Funding |
|------------|-------|----------|-----------------|
| **Exploration** | Curiosity-driven research | 1-3 years | Moderate - covers researcher time and materials |
| **Development** | Building prototypes and proofs | 6 months - 2 years | Variable - based on equipment and team needs |
| **Impact** | Solving specific problems | 1-5 years | Higher - for coordinated multi-team efforts |
| **Seed** | Early-stage experimentation | 3-12 months | Small - rapid iteration and validation |
| **Moonshot** | Ambitious breakthrough goals | 3-10 years | Substantial - for high-risk, high-reward ventures |

**Benefits:**

- **Supports high-risk, high-reward projects** - Failure is acceptable and expected as learning
- **Democratizes innovation** - Anyone can apply, regardless of background or credentials
- **Focus on public good** - Not profit value capture or private gain
- **No value capture** - Results are released under open licenses (e.g., Creative Commons, MIT) or dedicated to the public domain
- **Transparent process** - All decisions and reviews visible to public scrutiny
- **Portfolio approach** - Diversified funding across fields, approaches, and risk levels

### Maker Spaces and Labs

**What:**

Community facilities equipped with tools, machinery, and resources for creation and experimentation. This is the physical infrastructure of innovation.

**Examples:**

- **3D printing labs** - Additive manufacturing for prototypes and production
- **Robotics workshops** - Building and programming robots
- **Electronics labs** - Circuit design, testing, and fabrication
- **Art and design studios** - Creative tools and spaces
- **Bio labs** - Regulated spaces for safe biological research with appropriate oversight

**Benefits:**

- **Access to expensive equipment** - Shared resources reduce individual costs
- **Knowledge sharing** - Experienced makers mentor newcomers
- **Community support** - Collaboration and feedback
- **Lower barriers to entry** - No need to own all tools personally

**The Chen Family Example:**

Leo, at age 11, spends time at his local maker lab working on his water system project. He has access to 3D printers, tools, and materials without cost. Experienced makers help him troubleshoot problems. He collaborates with other kids on similar projects. The lab is funded as public infrastructure, free for community use.

### Recognition Systems

**What:**

Social mechanisms for acknowledging and celebrating contribution. Recognition provides status and motivation without wealth accumulation.

**Types:**

- **Innovation awards** - Formal recognition for breakthrough contributions
- **Contribution tracking** - Public records of impact and involvement
- **Community voting** - Peer recognition and appreciation
- **Reputation systems** - Building standing through consistent quality

**Benefits:**

- **Status without wealth** - Social recognition instead of financial reward
- **Motivation for contribution** - Recognition drives continued effort
- **Visibility for good work** - Important contributions get attention
- **Role models** - Successful innovators inspire others

**How It Works:**

Recognition systems in abundance society are transparent and community-driven. They're designed to highlight meaningful contribution rather than wealth accumulation. Status comes from contribution to the common good and artistic excellence.

**Safeguards against manipulation:**

- **Peer verification** - Contributions validated by multiple community members
- **Quality metrics** - Impact measured by actual use and improvement, not only activity
- **Time-weighted reputation** - Recognition builds over time, preventing rapid manipulation
- **Community oversight** - Suspicious patterns reviewed by trusted community members
- **Diverse evaluation** - Multiple criteria prevent gaming any single metric

For formally organized recognition schemes—including awards, contribution tracking, community votes, and recorded reputation—these safeguards form a shared floor. Participation is voluntary, and each scheme has a stated, purpose-specific scope; informal praise is outside this floor. No scheme creates a universal individual reputation score or cross-context dossier. Identifiable records are minimized and consent-based, with accessible correction, appeal, and withdrawal where compatible with narrowly justified records, consistent with Chapter 9's privacy protections. Criteria and conflicts are disclosed, with independent review for bias and capture and clear ways for overlooked contributors to be recognized. Reviewable rules and outcomes support public accountability and regular revision or ending of a scheme.

Prestige never determines basic rights, UBR/essentials, or citizen-assembly selection by sortition, and cannot by itself determine access to scarce public resources. Any narrow competence assessment follows the published rules in Chapters 5 and 7. Chapter 8's rights protections remain controlling.

### Knowledge Sharing

**What:**

Open access to research, development, and learning. Knowledge is treated as a public good rather than proprietary asset.

**Mechanisms:**

- **Open access journals** - All research freely available
- **Public repositories** - Code, designs, and documentation shared openly
- **Educational platforms** - Free learning resources for all
- **Mentorship programs** - Experienced innovators guide newcomers

**Benefits:**

- **Faster learning** - No barriers to accessing knowledge
- **Building on existing work** - Innovation accelerates when knowledge is shared
- **Avoiding duplication** - People don't reinvent what already exists
- **Democratizing knowledge** - Everyone can access and contribute

**Contrast with Current System:**

Currently, much knowledge is locked behind paywalls, patents, and trade secrets. A 2017 study of global-health research found that 42% of 3,366 PubMed-indexed articles published from 2010 through 2014 were available only through a subscription or payment.[^ch14-global-health-paywall-2017] A 2019 study of K–12 education-policy research reported single-article purchase prices usually between US$36 and US$42.[^ch14-article-price-k12-2019] In 2024 reporting, adjusted operating-profit margins were 38.4% for RELX's Scientific, Technical & Medical segment (Elsevier), 27.7% for Springer Nature, and 22.8% for Wiley's Research segment.[^ch14-publisher-margins-2024] In 2012, Harvard's Faculty Advisory Council said annual costs for journals from certain providers approached $3.75 million and that continuing those subscriptions on existing terms was financially unsustainable.[^ch14-harvard-library-cost-2012] Subscription barriers give institutions able to pay an advantage in access to research. In one natural experiment, a U.S. Department of Energy policy required articles from 17 National Laboratories to become free to read within 12 months. After the policy took effect in October 2014, citations from patent bodies to covered articles increased by an average of 42%; firms with smaller patent portfolios showed the strongest increase. The study uses patent-body citations as a measure of inventors' use of research.[^ch14-doe-open-access-effect] In an abundance society, knowledge is a common resource.

### Safety and Ethics Oversight

**What:**

Governance frameworks for responsible innovation in sensitive areas. Their purpose is to support the safe and ethical development and use of powerful technologies for the benefit of all.

**Areas Requiring Oversight:**

| Area | Oversight Mechanism |
|------|---------------------|
| **Biological Research** | Institutional biosafety committees, pathogen screening, dual-use research review |
| **Artificial Intelligence** | Algorithmic auditing, impact assessments, alignment research, deployment review |
| **Human Research** | Ethical review boards, informed consent requirements, privacy protections |
| **Chemical Research** | Hazardous materials protocols, environmental impact assessment |
| **Nanotechnology** | Material safety testing, environmental release monitoring |

**How It Works:**

In abundance society, safety oversight is transparent and community-involved. Key mechanisms include:

- **Ethics boards** - Diverse community members review sensitive research proposals
- **Safety certifications** - Required training before accessing high-risk facilities
- **Public registries** - High-impact research registered and open for scrutiny
- **Whistleblower protections** - Safe reporting of safety violations or ethical concerns
- **International coordination** - Global standards for transboundary risks

**Benefits:**

- **Harm prevention** - Identify and mitigate risks before deployment
- **Builds trust** - Transparency reduces public fear and suspicion
- **Maintains standards** - Review, testing, and oversight can support quality and safety without relying on profit pressure
- **Enables responsible innovation** - Safe pathways for exploring high-risk, high-reward areas

## Preventing Stagnation

### The Stagnation Risk

A common concern about abundance society is that without profit pressure and competition, innovation will slow or stop entirely. People will become complacent. Quality will decline. Progress will stall.

This concern deserves a serious answer. Stagnation is a risk. The systems proposed here aim to keep innovation moving, and their results would need to be tested.

### Strategies to Prevent Stagnation

#### 1. Maintain Challenge

Innovation thrives on challenge. Abundance society must maintain ambitious goals and difficult problems to solve.

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|----------------|
| **Ambitious Goals** | Moonshot projects, big challenges that require breakthrough thinking |
| **Healthy Competition** | Innovation challenges and prizes (not market competition) |
| **Continuous Learning** | Education and skill development as cultural norms |
| **Exploration** | Support for curiosity-driven work and fundamental research |

**Examples:**

- **Moonshot projects** - Mars colonization, disease eradication, climate restoration
- **Innovation challenges** - Community-organized competitions for specific problems
- **Skill development** - Continuous learning supported by abundant time and resources
- **Exploration funding** - Dedicated resources for pure curiosity-driven research

**How Innovation Challenges Differ from Market Competition:**

Innovation challenges are time-limited competitions focused on solving specific problems, with prizes awarded for successful solutions.

Challenges proposed here would use open sharing as the default and protect access to essential and publicly supported technologies. Limited patent or trade-secret exceptions would be evaluated for their public purpose and effects on access.

XPRIZE foundation competitions and government-sponsored innovation challenges are existing examples of prize-based innovation. XPRIZE's Carbon Removal rules allow teams to keep intellectual property developed during the competition and protect confidential submissions.[^ch14-xprize-prize-rules]

#### 2. Celebrate Innovation

Culture shapes behavior. A culture that celebrates innovation will sustain it.

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|----------------|
| **Recognition** | Awards, public acknowledgment, ceremonies |
| **Showcases** | Regular demonstrations, exhibitions, and presentations |
| **Stories** | Highlighting innovators and their journeys |
| **Cultural values** | Making innovation a celebrated social norm |

**How It Works:**

In abundance society, innovators are celebrated like athletes and celebrities are today. Their work is showcased publicly. Their stories are told. Their contributions are recognized. This creates positive reinforcement and inspires others.

#### 3. Support Risk-Taking

Innovation requires risk. The Charter would protect people’s basic needs when a project fails. The aim is to give more people room to experiment, learn from setbacks, and try again.

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|----------------|
| **Fail Fast** | Cultural acceptance of failure as learning |
| **No Punishment** | Failed experiments don't result in loss of basic needs |
| **Resources** | Support available for trying new things |
| **Safety Net** | Basic security enables bold experimentation |

**The Advantage of Abundance:**

Losing a job can mean losing housing, healthcare, and food. That threat gives people good reason to avoid uncertain work. Under the Charter, failed projects would not take away those basic resources. People would have support to learn from setbacks and try again. Research that could harm people or the environment would still need the safety oversight described in this chapter.

#### 4. Maintain Diversity

Diversity of approach, perspective, and motivation matters for innovation. In a study of U.S. doctoral research, Hofstra and colleagues measured scientific novelty through new combinations of concepts. They found higher novelty rates among underrepresented racial and gender groups; those contributions were taken up less often, and equally impactful work was less likely to lead to successful scientific careers.[^ch14-diversity-innovation-paradox] Institutions need to recognize these contributions and give others a chance to build on them.

[^ch14-diversity-innovation-paradox]: Bas Hofstra et al., “The Diversity–Innovation Paradox in Science,” *PNAS* 117, no. 17 (2020): 9284–9291. The dissertation dataset spans 1977–2015; the paper's main analysis follows doctoral cohorts from 1982–2010. Novelty is measured as new combinations of concepts in dissertation text. Gender and race are inferred from names linked with population records. The study measures scientific novelty, later uptake, and academic career outcomes in U.S. doctoral research; its results do not substantiate a claim across multiple domains. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1915378117

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|----------------|
| **Multiple Approaches** | Support for diverse methods and perspectives |
| **Different Fields** | Arts, sciences, crafts, humanities all valued |
| **Varied Motivations** | Curiosity, purpose, mastery, community all supported |
| **Inclusive** | All backgrounds and perspectives welcomed |

**Why Diversity Matters:**

Different approaches lead to different discoveries. Diverse perspectives identify different problems and solutions. When innovation is monocultural, driven by a single motivation or methodology, it misses opportunities. Abundance society supports innovation across all fields and from all perspectives.

#### 5. Continuous Improvement

Innovation itself should be continuously improved. Regular assessment and adaptation keep the system healthy.

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|----------------|
| **Feedback Loops** | Regular assessment of innovation outcomes |
| **Iterative Development** | Continuous refinement of innovation systems |
| **Learning Culture** | Always improving how we innovate |
| **Benchmarking** | Tracking progress over time |

### Technology

**Current Model:**

- Profit-driven R&D by corporations
- Patent protection and intellectual property
- Competitive advantage through secrecy
- Trade secrets and closed development

**Problems:**

- Innovation directed toward profitable problems, not important ones
- Duplication of effort due to secrecy
- Access limited by cost and licensing
- Short-term focus on quarterly returns

**Abundance Model:**

- Open source development as default
- Collaborative innovation across organizations
- Public benefit as primary focus
- Knowledge sharing and open protocols

**Examples:**

- **Linux development** - Thousands of contributors, open and collaborative
- **Open source AI** - Public models and research
- **Community hardware** - Open designs for robots, tools, equipment
- **Public research** - Government and community-funded science

**The Shift:**

Technology innovation in abundance society is envisioned as more collaborative than competitive. Open knowledge is the proposed default, with access to essential and publicly supported technologies protected. Limited patent or trade-secret exceptions elsewhere should be evaluated for public purpose and their effects on access. Wider sharing may accelerate innovation; that is a goal to test, not a guaranteed outcome.

### Science

**Current Model:**

- Academic publication with paywalls
- Grant funding with intense competition
- Some commercialization and patenting
- Peer review system

**Problems:**

- Access limited by institutional affiliation and payment
- Competition can inhibit collaboration
- Funding pressure distorts research priorities
- Replication evidence: In a project that attempted replications of 100 psychology studies, 36% of replications were statistically significant. Separately, 47% of original effect sizes fell within the 95% confidence intervals for replication effect sizes. The percentages describe different outcomes in this selected sample; broader replication rates require evidence for the relevant fields and samples.[^ch14-osc-replication]

**Abundance Model:**

- Fully open access to all research
- Public funding without commercial pressure
- Collaboration over competition
- Focus on important problems, not publishable ones

**Examples:**

- **Open science movement** - Free access to all research
- **Citizen science** - Public participation in research
- **Public research institutions** - Funded as public infrastructure
- **Collaborative projects** - Global teams working together

**The Shift:**

Science becomes truly public. Research funded by society is accessible to society. Collaboration replaces competition. The focus shifts from publishing papers to solving important problems.

## Addressing Common Concerns

### Taking the Concerns Seriously

Innovation without profit is one of the hardest questions in this proposal. Legitimate concerns deserve direct answers, not dismissal.

**What We Acknowledge:**

- **Transition risks:** Moving from a profit-driven system requires careful coordination and may face opposition from entrenched interests
- **Incentive complexity:** Different types of innovation may require different motivational structures
- **Coordination challenges:** Large-scale, capital-intensive projects need mechanisms for resource allocation
- **Quality variance:** Without market pressure, some areas may experience inconsistent quality without proper oversight
- **Free rider problem:** Ensuring sufficient contribution when participation is voluntary

**What the Evidence Shows:**

The historical record of public research, open-source projects, citizen science, and maker communities offers examples relevant to these concerns:

- Open source software has produced some of the world's most critical infrastructure (Linux, Apache, Kubernetes)
- Academic research has driven fundamental breakthroughs (internet, GPS, mRNA technology)[^ch14-mrna-nidcr]
- Foldit players contributed models that helped researchers solve the crystal structure of the Mason–Pfizer monkey virus (M-PMV) retroviral protease after earlier molecular-replacement attempts had failed. Galaxy Zoo participants classified nearly one million Sloan Digital Sky Survey galaxies; the resulting catalogue was consistent with classifications by professional astronomers.[^ch14-foldit-cases][^ch14-galaxy-zoo]
- Maker communities have developed projects in 3D printing, robotics, and open hardware. Some also explore sustainable technology, including the published RepRapable Recyclebot design for converting waste plastic into 3D-printer filament.[^ch14-open-printer-community][^ch14-arduino-robot][^ch14-recyclebot]

These examples demonstrate that while profit can drive innovation, it is not the only path and may not always be the best path for certain types of innovation.

### Nuanced Objections and Responses

### Concern: "Without profit, who will take risks?"

**The Concern:**

High-risk innovation requires the possibility of high reward. Without profit, who will invest in uncertain ventures?

**Response:**

Under scarcity, failure can cost someone housing, healthcare, and income. The Charter would protect people’s basic resources through a failed project. The aim is to let more people experiment, learn from mistakes, and try again.

**Examples and supporting evidence:**

- **Shared software development:** Linux kernel development and Rust language design show communities proposing and testing technical changes.[^ch14-linux-development-process][^ch14-risk-rust]
- **Long-term scientific research:** CERN’s Large Hadron Collider and the James Webb Space Telescope show international institutions taking on difficult scientific and engineering projects.[^ch14-risk-lhc][^ch14-risk-webb]
- **Independent experimentation:** The Charter would support curiosity-led projects such as amateur rocketry and home biotechnology, with training, shared facilities, and appropriate safety review.
- **Maker and community projects:** The Charter would provide resources for open-source hardware and community energy projects, giving people room to build, test, and improve ideas.
- **Protection against failure:** A 2020 study of a French unemployment-benefit reform found that protecting unemployed people against losses from starting a business increased new firm creation.[^ch14-risk-france-ui]

**The Reality:**

Public investment would give uncertain research time and resources to develop beyond quarterly earnings cycles. The Charter would support ambitious projects through the funding and review systems described in this chapter.

**Acknowledged Limitation:**

Some high-capital ventures (semiconductor fabrication, fusion energy) still require significant resource coordination. Abundance society addresses this through public investment and collaborative models rather than private capital at risk.

### Concern: "Won't quality decline without market pressure?"

**The Concern:**

Market competition forces companies to maintain quality. Without it, won't innovation become sloppy or careless?

**The Answer:**

Good work comes from people who care about their craft, reviewers who catch mistakes, and communities that take standards seriously. Open-source projects let people inspect the code, test changes, and report problems. Linux and PostgreSQL show how shared development can support demanding technical work.[^ch14-linux-development-process][^ch14-postgresql-quality]

**Evidence:**

- **Software review and testing**: Linux kernel and PostgreSQL documentation describe review and testing processes for changes.[^ch14-linux-development-process][^ch14-postgresql-quality]
- **Encyclopedia accuracy**: Nature's 2005 comparison of online science entries reported about four errors per Wikipedia article and three per Britannica article.[^ch14-wikipedia-2005]
- **Community accountability**: The Charter would use code review processes and reputation systems to make work open to scrutiny and recognize careful contributions.
- **Mastery and craft**: The Charter would support pride in developing a skill, drawing on craft traditions and the work of open-source maintainers. Linux Foundation profiles describe contributors' enjoyment, challenge, impact, and community.[^ch14-linux-kernel-report]

**The Reality:**

Pride in work, community standards, and the desire to make something useful give people reasons to care about quality. Those motivations can exist independently of profit.

**Acknowledged Limitation:**

Quality assurance requires active community engagement. Without sufficient reviewers and contributors, some projects may experience quality drift. Abundance society addresses this through dedicated quality infrastructure, recognition for reviewers, and transparent quality metrics.

**Quality Assurance Mechanisms:**

Abundance society implements multiple overlapping quality assurance systems that work together to maintain high standards:

**1. Peer Review Systems**

- **Technical review** - Experts in the field evaluate work before release
- **Multi-reviewer requirement** - Significant contributions require approval from multiple reviewers
- **Blind review processes** - Reduces bias in evaluation
- **Review documentation** - All reviews documented and publicly accessible
- **Review quality tracking** - Reviewers themselves receive feedback on review quality

**2. Testing and Validation**

- **Automated testing** - Comprehensive test suites run on all contributions
- **Continuous integration** - Changes tested automatically before merging
- **Benchmarking** - Performance and quality measured against established standards
- **User acceptance testing** - Real users validate before wide release
- **Staged rollouts** - Gradual deployment to catch issues early

**3. Certification and Standards**

- **Industry standards** - Alignment with established technical standards (ISO, IEEE, etc.)
- **Safety certifications** - Required for high-risk domains (medical, aviation, infrastructure)
- **Quality badges** - Visual indicators of tested and certified work
- **Compliance checking** - Automated verification against standards

**4. Community Quality Infrastructure**

- **Bug tracking systems** - Public issue trackers with priority and severity classification
- **Quality teams** - Dedicated groups responsible for maintaining standards
- **Regression testing** - Ensuring new changes don't break existing functionality
- **Documentation requirements** - Quality includes clear, accurate documentation

**5. Feedback and Improvement Loops**

- **User feedback channels** - Easy reporting of issues and quality concerns
- **Post-release monitoring** - Tracking real-world performance and issues
- **Regular audits** - Periodic review of quality processes
- **Continuous improvement** - Quality standards evolve based on experience

**6. Incentives for Quality**

- **Recognition for reviewers** - Quality reviewers receive status and appreciation
- **Quality awards** - Special recognition for exceptional quality
- **Maintainer status** - Quality contributors earn trusted roles
- **Impact tracking** - Quality work measured by adoption and satisfaction

**Real-World Examples:**

| Example | Quality Mechanism | What the sources show |
|---------|-------------------|---------|
| **Linux Kernel** | Layered review process, maintainership hierarchy, automated testing | Patch review and pre-release stabilization aimed at catching regressions.[^ch14-linux-development-process] |
| **Wikipedia** | Edit history, discussion pages, patrol system, admin oversight | Nature's 2005 online science-entry comparison reported about four errors per Wikipedia article and three per Britannica article.[^ch14-wikipedia-2005][^ch14-wikipedia-review-tools] |
| **Apache Projects** | Meritocratic governance, code review, release engineering | ASF policy requires release approval, signed artifacts, and archiving.[^ch14-apache-release] |
| **PostgreSQL** | Rigorous testing, community review, minor-release compatibility | Tests of standard SQL and PostgreSQL extensions, with public review of code changes. Minor upgrades preserve the data directory; major upgrades require migration.[^ch14-postgresql-quality][^ch14-postgresql-versioning] |
| **OpenSSL** | After Heartbleed: dedicated funding, developer support, and announced external audit | In June 2014, the Core Infrastructure Initiative announced support for OpenSSL development and funding for an Open Crypto Audit Project audit.[^ch14-openssl-heartbleed] |

These projects give the Charter practical ways to organize quality checks: shared review, testing, audits, and clear responsibility for fixing problems. The proposed system would support that work with dedicated quality infrastructure, recognition for reviewers, and transparent quality metrics.

### Concern: "What about coordination and scale?"

**The Concern:**

Large-scale innovation requires coordination. How do you coordinate without corporate hierarchies and market signals?

**The Answer:**

Collaborative platforms can support distributed coordination at scale through open platforms, shared goals, community organization, and defined project roles.

**Evidence:**

- Linux development involves thousands of contributors globally with structured maintainership
- Wikipedia has millions of articles created collaboratively across 300+ languages
- Open source projects coordinate massive efforts (Kubernetes, Apache Foundation projects)
- Galaxy Zoo 1 received more than 50 million classifications from more than 150,000 people in its first year after its July 2007 launch; Zooniverse's official site reported millions of registered volunteers as of October 2, 2026. Foldit players contributed models that helped researchers solve the M-PMV retroviral protease structure.[^ch14-galaxy-zoo][^ch14-zooniverse-scale-2026][^ch14-foldit-cases]

**The Reality:**

The examples here show collaboration at scale through distributed work on the internet, shared platforms, community work, and structured project roles.

**Acknowledged Limitation:**

Coordination at scale requires governance structures and conflict resolution mechanisms. Abundance society invests in transparent governance, clear contribution guidelines, and community mediation processes to handle disagreements and ensure fair decision-making.

### Concern: "Won't innovation slow without competition?"

**The Concern:**

Competition drives improvement. Without it, won't innovation become complacent and slow?

**The Answer:**

Sharing code, data, and methods lets people build on work already done. Collaboration can speed progress by reducing duplicated effort and letting teams work on different parts of the same problem. Keeping useful knowledge closed can force others to repeat work.

**Evidence:**

- **Open-source development supports regular updates and community feedback**: Linux documents major releases every two or three months, with review and stabilization before release; React uses public bug reports, proposed changes, and release channels to gather feedback.[^ch14-linux-development-process][^ch14-react-versioning]
- **Collaborative science makes research available for others to use**: the Human Genome Project rapidly shared sequence data and finished more than two years ahead of schedule; CERN's Open Data Portal provides released data, software, and documentation for further analysis.[^ch14-nhgri-hgp][^ch14-cern-open-data]
- **Community problem-solving contributes to crisis-response technology**: OpenStreetMap mapping coordinated through the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team's Tasking Manager divides work into tasks that people can complete in parallel, with experienced mappers checking the results.[^ch14-hot-tasking-manager]
- **Shared research infrastructure and open-source AI models give researchers work they can build on**: EleutherAI's Pythia project releases models, training data, and code for further research.[^ch14-pythia-research]

**The Reality:**

Shared knowledge and coordinated work can reduce duplication and help useful ideas spread. The Charter aims to make that way of working available across research and development.

**Acknowledged Limitation:**

Competition can sometimes drive rapid iteration in specific contexts. Abundance society maintains competitive elements within collaborative frameworks, including innovation challenges, prizes, and recognition competitions that drive effort without creating destructive market dynamics or information hoarding.

## Phasing the Transition to Abundance Innovation

Moving from our current profit-driven innovation system to an abundance-based model requires deliberate, phased transitions. This section outlines practical pathways for making this shift.

This proposed innovation work is organized under Chapter 24's Foundation (Years 0–8), Expansion (Years 8–16), Global Floor (Years 16–30), and Maturity (Years 30+) periods. Support for existing nonprofit research and open infrastructure starts in Foundation. Incentive reform and shared infrastructure continue as people test what works and pass readiness gates. Open innovation norms carry through all four periods. Public review and a readiness gate apply before scaling a mechanism.

### Foundation (Years 0–8)

#### Strengthen Existing Non-Profit Innovation

Build on what already works without requiring systemic change:

| Action | Current Examples | Impact |
|--------|------------------|--------|
| **Increase public research funding** | NIH, NSF, CERN, Max Planck Institutes | Direct public investment in fundamental research |
| **Support open source foundations** | Linux Foundation, Apache, Mozilla | Strengthen existing non-profit infrastructure |
| **Expand open access mandates** | Plan S, US federal open access requirements | Make publicly-funded research publicly accessible |
| **Protect academic freedom** | University tenure, research independence | Enable long-term, non-commercial research |
| **Support maker spaces and libraries** | Public maker labs, community workshops | Build physical infrastructure for innovation |

#### Reform Incentive Structures

Change how innovation is rewarded and supported:

| Reform | Description | Expected Outcome |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| **Grant system reform** | Shift from competitive to collaborative funding models | More cooperation, less duplication |
| **Patent system reform** | Shorter patent terms, stronger compulsory licensing | Faster knowledge diffusion |
| **Tax incentives for open contribution** | Tax benefits for open source and open research | Redirect private resources to public good |
| **Public procurement for open solutions** | Government prefers open source/open standards | Market pull for open innovation |
| **Recognition systems** | National awards for open contribution | Status for public-benefit innovation |

**Nested cultural workstream: Open innovation norms (ongoing across all four transition periods)**

Shift values and norms around innovation:

| Cultural Shift | From | To |
|----------------|------|-----|
| **Ownership** | "My IP, my advantage" | "Our knowledge, our progress" |
| **Success** | Wealth accumulation | Meaningful contribution |
| **Competition** | Win-lose market dynamics | Collaborative improvement |
| **Motivation** | Financial reward | Purpose, mastery, recognition |
| **Innovation** | Exclusive, proprietary | Open, accessible to all |

### Expansion (Years 8–16)

Continue incentive reform and open innovation work begun during Foundation.

#### Build Abundance Infrastructure

Create the foundations that enable innovation without profit pressure:

| Infrastructure | Purpose | Connection to Abundance |
|----------------|---------|------------------------|
| **Public innovation platforms** | Open collaboration infrastructure | Reduces barriers to participation |
| **Universal basic resources** | Time and resources for all | Enables innovation from anyone |
| **Open knowledge repositories** | Free access to all research and designs | Eliminates paywall barriers |
| **Community innovation grants** | Public funding for promising ideas | Resources without profit value capture |
| **Distributed manufacturing** | Local production capacity | Reduces capital requirements |

### Global Floor (Years 16–30)

Continue building the public innovation platforms, Universal Basic Resources, open knowledge repositories, community innovation grants, and distributed manufacturing described above, where readiness gates support wider access.

### Maturity (Years 30+)

Continue the open innovation norms described above as shared knowledge and public infrastructure develop.

### Specific Policy Mechanisms

**1. Innovation Public Goods Fund**

- Dedicated public funding for innovation serving public good
- Allocated through transparent peer review processes
- Results required to be open license or public domain
- Modeled on NSF, NIH, but expanded scope and funding

**2. Open Source Public Infrastructure**

- Treat critical open source as public infrastructure
- Direct public funding for maintenance and development
- Similar to funding for roads, internet, utilities
- Preventes "tragedy of the shared resources" in software

**3. Knowledge Freedom Act**

- Mandate open access for publicly-funded research
- Limit patent terms for essential goods (medicines, clean tech)
- Require open licensing for government-funded innovation
- Create public domain defaults for research outputs

**4. Innovation Cooperatives**

- Worker and community-owned innovation entities
- Profits shared among contributors and community
- Governance by participants, not external shareholders
- Model for hybrid profit/non-profit innovation

**5. Time Dividend Programs**

- Reduced work weeks as productivity increases
- Freed time available for creative and innovative pursuits
- Supports innovation from people who currently lack time
- Complements Universal Basic Resources

### Historical Precedents for Transition

| Transition | From | To | Lessons |
|------------|------|-----|---------|
| **Internet** | Military/academic network | Global public infrastructure | Open protocols enable scale |
| **Open Source** | Proprietary software dominance | Mixed ecosystem with major open components | Quality and collaboration can compete |
| **Creative Commons** | All rights reserved default | Flexible licensing options | Legal infrastructure enables sharing |
| **Open Access Movement** | Paywalled research | Growing open access share | Public pressure can change publishing |
| **CERN** | National research labs | Global collaborative science | International coordination works |

### Managing Transition Challenges

**Challenge: Opposition from Incumbents**

- **Response:** Build coalitions, demonstrate value, use policy levers
- **Example:** Open-source projects let people inspect and improve code, and licenses such as PostgreSQL's permit use without a license fee.[^ch14-postgresql-quality][^ch14-open-source-license]

**Challenge: Funding Gaps During Transition**

- **Response:** Gradual shift, maintain existing support while building new systems
- **Example:** Public research funding continues while new models develop

**Challenge: Coordination Problems**

- **Response:** Build governance structures incrementally, learn from existing models
- **Example:** Linux Foundation shows how competing companies can collaborate

**Challenge: Quality Concerns**

- **Response:** Implement quality infrastructure alongside new systems
- **Example:** Wikipedia contributors use page histories, discussion pages, patrolling, and administrator oversight to review and improve articles.[^ch14-wikipedia-review-tools]

### The Transition Is Already Underway

Many elements of abundance innovation already exist:

- **Open source software** - Linux supports web servers and cloud deployments, provides Android's kernel, and ran all 500 systems on the November 2017 TOP500 list.[^ch14-linux-webserver-example][^ch14-android-linux-platform][^ch14-top500-linux-2017]
- **Open access research** - Growing share of scientific publication
- **Citizen science** - Public participants contribute observations, classifications, and problem-solving through research projects, including NOAA programs, Galaxy Zoo, and Foldit.[^ch14-noaa-citizen-science][^ch14-galaxy-zoo][^ch14-foldit-cases]
- **Maker movement** - Distributed innovation communities
- **Open hardware** - Growing movement of open designs

The transition doesn't require starting from scratch. It requires recognizing, supporting, and expanding what already works.

- **Response:** Respect for local data governance, mutual recognition agreements

### Principles for Effective International Innovation Cooperation

1. **Shared purpose** - Focus on public well-being and ecological health
2. **Equitable participation** - All regions have voice and representation
3. **Open by default** - Results accessible to all, especially developing nations
4. **Capacity building** - Strengthen innovation everywhere, not only in wealthy regions
5. **Cultural diversity** - Multiple approaches and knowledge systems valued
6. **Long-term commitment** - Sustained cooperation beyond political cycles
7. **Transparency** - Open processes and accountable governance
8. **Mutual benefit** - All participants gain value from cooperation

### Internet and ARPANET

### GPS Development

- **NAVSTAR GPS**: The U.S. Department of Defense initiated the NAVSTAR GPS project in the early 1970s, combining competing satellite navigation systems.

### Replication Crisis

- **Psychology Studies**: In the Open Science Collaboration's project, which attempted replications of 100 psychology studies, 36% of replications were statistically significant; separately, 47% of original effect sizes fell within the 95% confidence intervals for replication effect sizes (2015, *Science*). The percentages describe different outcomes in this selected sample; broader replication rates require evidence for the relevant fields and samples.
- **Cross-Disciplinary**: How reproducibility varies across fields remains an open question; the psychology project does not establish a rate for other disciplines.
- **Source**: Open Science Collaboration, “Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science” (2015).[^ch14-osc-replication]

### Diversity-Innovation Research

- **Diversity-Innovation Paradox**: In U.S. doctoral research, underrepresented gender and racial groups produced higher rates of scientific novelty, measured as new combinations of concepts. ## Innovation Beyond Profit: Closing Principles

### How Innovation Continues Without Profit

**The Question:** "What about innovation? How do we drive progress without profit motive? Won't innovation stagnate without competition?"

**The proposal:**

**1. Profit Is Not Required for Innovation**

Curiosity, purpose, and recognition can drive innovation. Open-source software, scientific research, and hobbyist projects show people contributing through shared development and public research. Profit is not the only reason people innovate.

**2. Abundance Enables More Innovation**

Access to innovation would be open to everyone. Universal Basic Resources and shared tools, research facilities, and support would give people time, resources, and freedom to work on ideas. A failed project would not take away their food, housing, or healthcare.

**3. Reducing the Risk of Stagnation**

Challenge, recognition, and culture can help maintain momentum. Systems for celebrating innovation, supporting risk-taking, and maintaining diversity may sustain progress, but their effects must be tested and revised rather than assumed. The mechanisms for preventing stagnation remain a design and evidence question.

**4. Innovation Can Be Better Without Profit**

- Focus on meaningful problems, not profitable ones
- Open collaboration instead of competitive secrecy
- Public benefit instead of private value capture
- Long-term thinking instead of short-term returns

### Key Principles for Innovation in Abundance

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Curiosity** | Support exploration without requiring immediate application |
| **Recognition** | Celebrate contribution and impact publicly |
| **Collaboration** | Build open platforms and networks |
| **Resources** | Provide tools, time, and infrastructure for all |
| **Purpose** | Connect innovation to meaningful goals |
| **Risk-Taking** | Support experimentation and normalize failure |
| **Sharing** | Make knowledge and resources open and accessible |
| **Diversity** | Encourage multiple approaches and perspectives |

## Innovation That Serves People

The Charter's aspirations for innovation include:

- **More abundant** - More people can innovate when freed from survival pressure
- **More collaborative** - Open sharing may speed progress in some settings
- **More purposeful** - Focus on meaningful problems, not profitable ones
- **More diverse** - All perspectives and approaches welcomed
- **More open** - Shared knowledge as the default, with access to essential and publicly supported technologies protected and limited exceptions elsewhere evaluated
- **More sustainable** - Long-term thinking over short-term value capture

The goal is not innovation for profit. It is innovation that helps people become free.

Profit is not the only reason people innovate. Public research and shared work helped develop the internet, Wikipedia, Linux, GPS, and open-source software. Developers described in the Linux Foundation's 2017 report point to enjoyment, challenge, impact, and community.[^ch14-linux-kernel-report]

The proposal aims to redirect innovation from serving shareholders toward serving people, open the creative process to more participants, and focus effort on important problems. Whether it achieves those outcomes, including faster innovation through wider sharing, should be tested rather than assumed.

**What drives innovation, and what kind of innovation do we want?**

The aspiration is to expand our freedom to choose innovation that serves humanity alongside profit, and to pursue flourishing, discovery, meaning, and the future we want to create.
